About This Map

Since 1972 the world has written environmental laws faster than at any time in history, and over the same years the damage those laws were meant to limit has grown. This map follows the chain of bodies that stand above one factory's permit, to show who writes the rules, who is paid when they fail, and where the failure lands.

The title comes from a finding of the United Nations' own review. In 2019 the UN Environment Programme counted 38 times more environmental laws than in 1972 and concluded that the laws were, to a large extent, not being applied. A law that exists and is not applied is a law on paper. The map does not measure how polluted any place is. It is about how the system of rules is built, who has a hand on it at each level, and who gains.

One Story, Told From the Top Down

The map's central feature is one story, arranged in seven parts and {NCH} chapters. It follows environmental law from the institutions at the top down to the people and places at the bottom: the international bodies and treaties, the national government, the people who draft laws and standards, the courts, the markets built around the rules, the places where pollution was allowed to concentrate, and finally the whole chain in one view with the bodies most responsible for it.

The order matters. A ministry's permit is easier to understand once the treaties and investment agreements above it are visible. A weak fine makes more sense after seeing who drafted the law and who was appointed to enforce it. A polluted town at the end of the story is the result of decisions taken at each level above it.

To follow the story in order, press Play the story. You can also select any chapter from the list on the left. Each chapter begins with a box containing a one-line recap of the chapter before it, an introduction to the question the chapter asks, and a short passage headed What the Slides Show. The box then gives way to the slides.

Each slide picks up from the one before it and explains the significance of its figures alongside the facts. You can close any box using its ×. The controls at the bottom of the screen pause the story, change its speed and move between slides and chapters.

Slides marked Regional Example show how a wider pattern appears in one country. Other slides compare every country for which a figure is published.

What Is Drawn on Each Slide, and Why

Every slide includes a note headed On the map. It lists which countries, lines and points appear. Countries are coloured by the part they play in the chapter, for example where a treaty office sits and which country was sued. Lines join places between which a rule, a payment, a lawsuit or a cargo of waste travelled. Ringed circles are the organisations named in the slide. A diamond marks the place of a documented record. Nothing is drawn that the slide or its source does not name.

The Organisations, and Why These Ones

The map contains {NORG} organisations. Environmental law involves thousands of agencies, companies, courts and campaign groups, so the directory cannot list every one. Each was chosen because it is among the largest or most powerful of its kind, worldwide or in its region, or because the story names it.

That test is applied across several groups:

  • International bodies and treaty offices: the United Nations agencies and treaty secretariats for climate, biodiversity, waste, chemicals, wildlife trade, shipping, aviation and the deep sea.
  • Lenders and funds: the development banks and the funds that pay for environmental programmes.
  • Ministries and regulators: the environment ministries and enforcement agencies of the largest economies and the largest resource producers on each continent.
  • Courts and tribunals: the international courts, the regional human rights courts, the arbitration bodies where companies sue governments, and a few national courts.
  • Regulated companies: the largest oil, gas, coal, power, mining, chemical, pesticide, meat and grain companies, state-owned and private.
  • Trade associations, standard-setters and investors: the bodies that lobby for those companies, certify them and own their shares.
  • Environmental and legal organisations: the largest campaign and conservation groups, the law centres that bring cases, and the research groups whose counts the story uses.

The legend includes a switch, Show only the largest of each kind. It hides the {NSM} smaller organisations included because the story names them, for example one pipeline company or one state permit agency.

Each organisation is placed in its headquarters city and coloured by kind. Its presence on the map means only that it is a significant part of how environmental law is made or applied. The documented records inside the chapters provide the evidence for what any organisation did.

The Bodies and the Laws Most Responsible

The story names the organisations its evidence leads to. The last two slides widen the view. The first shows the bodies that carry the most weight in how environmental law is written and why it fails. The second shows 160 keystone legal instruments: the treaties, laws, court rulings and contracts those bodies act through. Both come from the Environmental Law Atlas map, whose instruments and actors are now part of this one, and both can be switched on at any time in the legend. An actor's card carries the atlas's notes on it and the instruments it stands behind; an instrument's card says what it does, who stands behind it and links to its text.

The Law Atlas

Click any country to see how many environmental, natural resource, farming and food laws are on record for it, and to list them all, each linked to its source. States and provinces with their own records are listed beneath. The legend has a button for the international and regional instruments. The lists are loaded from the Environmental Law Atlas when asked for, so they need a connection.

The Index and the Two Chapter Lists

The Index sits below the search box. It lists every organisation, every country named in the story and every documented record in alphabetical order. Beneath it are two lists of the same chapters: first in story order, then sorted by the region where their slides take place, so you can find the parts that happen where you live.

How to Read It

Countries are shaded only while a story slide is playing. Outside the published comparisons, shading shows the part a country plays in one chapter and nothing more. A country left unshaded has not been judged to be free of the problem; it has not been reached by the research.

The Method section explains what qualifies as a chapter, how slides are sourced, how organisations are placed, where the data comes from, and what is missing.

What the Map Shows

The map has three layers. The first is the story: how environmental law is written, licensed, drafted, tested in court, traded and finally felt, told from the top of the system down. The second is the directory of organisations. The third is the law atlas: every national, state and international environmental law on record, listed when you click a country. The documented records sit inside the chapters as the evidence for individual claims.

What Counts as a Chapter

A chapter has to meet two tests. First, does it describe something that affects how environmental law works beyond the location of one event? Second, is it about who writes, bends, sells or benefits from the rules, and not simply about how damaged the environment is? A single spill, lawsuit or news report is not a chapter by itself. It can be included as an example inside a wider chapter when it shows a recurring structure.

How the Story Is Ordered

The chapters follow the chain of authority from the top down: international bodies and treaties; national governments; the authors of laws and standards; the courts; the markets; the ground; and a closing view of the whole chain. Every chapter begins with an introduction to its subject and a short bridge that says how the slides answer it. Each new chapter opens with a one-line recap of the one before.

How the Slides Are Built

Each slide is placed where the event or institution it describes is located and carries a link to a source. If a slide is built around one of the documented records, the record is linked beneath it. Countries are coloured by the role they play in the chapter and stay coloured until the chapter ends. Where a slide names specific countries, only those countries are coloured.

Slides That Cover Every Country

Global comparison slides use the latest figure each source publishes for each country. The year is shown when you hover over a country. The datasets are:

  • Global Carbon Budget, as published by Our World in Data: each country's yearly carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels and industry. The map compares 1972 with the latest year for the 184 countries that have a figure for both. The statements that world emissions rose 144 per cent between 1972 and 2025, and that about 77 per cent of all such emissions since the industrial revolution came after 1972, were calculated from the same file.
  • UN Environment Programme, OECD and IMF, as published by Our World in Data: direct fossil fuel subsidies as a share of national income (Sustainable Development Goal indicator 12.c.1), latest year for 187 countries.
  • World Bank: total natural resource rents as a share of national income (indicator NY.GDP.TOTL.RT.ZS), latest year for 192 countries.
  • The Environmental Law Atlas: the number of national laws, regulations and policies on record for each country, compiled from the Food and Agriculture Organization's FAOLEX database and from Climate Change Laws of the World (Grantham Research Institute and Climate Policy Radar). FAOLEX covers food, farming and natural resources as well as the environment, so the counts are wider than environmental law alone. The map includes every entry the atlas holds and does not select among them.
  • UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean: the parties to the Escazú Agreement. Nineteen countries had joined when the map was built.

Carbon dioxide appears on this map as a yardstick for the whole period since 1972, because it is measured for every country. It is not the only harm environmental law addresses, and the map does not rank countries by how clean they are.

How the Organisations Were Chosen and Placed

The About section explains the inclusion test. Each organisation is placed at the centre of its headquarters city, not at a street address. When several share a city, their markers are spread around a small circle so each can be selected. This changes only how the markers appear on screen.

How the Bodies and Laws Most Responsible Were Chosen

The Environmental Law Atlas map compiles 160 keystone legal instruments and 91 actors behind them, each actor weighted from 1 to 6. All 160 instruments and all 91 actors are on this map. An actor is counted among the bodies most responsible when the atlas gives it a weight of 4 or more. That list was then checked against published rankings, and bodies it lacked were added: OPEC; the remaining largest producers in the Carbon Majors database of historical emissions; the five largest lenders to fossil fuel companies since the Paris Agreement in the Banking on Climate Chaos reports; the two largest asset managers; and the business federations with the largest presence at treaty talks. Certifiers, auditors and chemical companies that an earlier edition of this map had counted were removed from the list, because their part, though real, is smaller. The instruments are shown as the atlas compiled them; none has been removed. Lines on the map never take the short way across the Pacific Ocean, so that nothing is split between the two edges of the flat map.

Records

Each documented record rests on at least one of the following: a court or tribunal's ruling; an official inquiry or United Nations report; a published law or regulation; a company's or agency's own statement; a peer-reviewed study; or reporting by a named news organisation or research group. Each record is labelled with which of these it is. Where a company disputes a finding, the record says so.

How the Facts Were Checked

The figures for 2025 and 2026, including the summit lobbyist counts, the defender killings, the United States withdrawals and repeal, the Greenpeace judgment, the Mariana ruling and the European Omnibus, were checked against current sources when the map was built. Older facts were written from the cited sources and a sample was checked again. Every slide and record carries its source so that any one claim can be tested. One point is flagged for the reader: the 2024 dismissal of the second Keystone XL claim is given from the arbitration record without a second source. Links to a few bodies that block automated checking, such as the Inter-American Court, point to the body's own website, and the citation beside the link names the document.

What Was Left Out on Purpose

The map does not give chapters to measures of environmental condition, such as temperature, species loss or air quality rankings. Those are the outcome; this map is about the machinery. It also leaves out single incidents that do not show a wider structure, and claims that rest on one unverified report.

What Is Missing

The map does not yet cover the financing of fossil fuels by banks and pension funds in any detail; water law; fisheries agreements; conservation projects that evict the people living on the land; the sale and shrinking of protected areas; deep-sea mining; the roughly three thousand investment treaties, which are described in chapter 4 and not yet drawn treaty by treaty; chemical safety law outside the United States and Europe; or the environmental conditions attached to development loans. There is no layer of individual polluting sites or environmental conflicts.

Corrections

If a slide, record or organisation description is wrong, it should be corrected. Every slide and organisation card carries its source so that an individual claim can be checked against it.
