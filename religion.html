The Business of Belief

An investigation of organised religion as a single system, across Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism and Shinto. One story, told on the map from the top down: what every religion has in common, who commands it, how governments enforce it, what they get back, where the money goes, and what happens to the member.

About This Map

Three people in four belong to an organised religion. This map investigates the organisations they belong to as one system. It asks who runs that system, who protects it, what it takes in, and what it does to the people inside it.

The map is about organisations and not about belief. It does not ask whether any religion's teaching is true. Its subject is the churches, councils, ministries, temple boards and bodies of clergy that stand between people and what they believe. Its finding is that, under their different names, these organisations are built the same way: a class of specialists, a duty to pay them, membership from birth, a cost for leaving, and an alliance with whoever governs.

The evidence is drawn from every major religion: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism and Shinto, and from several newer movements. A slide is included because it shows how the system works, and for no other reason. The map is a dissection of one structure, so it does not try to describe each religion in turn.

One Story in 6 Parts

The story has 17 chapters and runs from the top of the system down. Part 1 defines the system and shows how rulers assembled it. Part 2 shows how the top positions are filled, by clerics choosing clerics or by governments. Part 3 shows what the state adds: official status, tax money, laws against insulting or leaving a religion, and the use of churches to end Indigenous religions. Part 4 shows what rulers and parties get back, which is votes and enemies. Part 5 follows the money: the selling of blessings and pilgrimages, the closed accounts and the land. Part 6 reaches the member: marriage and divorce under religious law, the enrolment of children through school, and what the organisation does when a child is abused inside it. The last chapter puts the whole chain in one place. It has no slides and is a summary to read.

The map has no chapter on reforms or on people who resisted. That is deliberate. Its purpose is to show the system as it is.

To follow the story in order, press Play the story. You can also select any chapter from the list on the left. Each chapter begins with a box containing a one-line recap of the chapter before, followed by the new chapter's introduction and a passage headed What the Slides Show. The box fades as the slides begin to play on the map, with the full text displayed in the panel on the right.

Each slide picks up from the one before and says what its facts mean. You can close any box using its ×. The controls at the bottom allow you to play or pause, move one slide forward or back, switch chapters, change the reading speed, show or hide the slide text, and see your position in the complete story.

Slides marked Regional Example show how a wider pattern appears in a particular country. Other slides compare every country a source reports on. Those countries are shaded according to the key, and hovering over one reveals its finding and the year.

What Is Drawn on Each Slide, and Why

Every slide includes a note headed On the map. It explains which countries, organisations, lines and points appear, and why those locations were selected. The rule is consistent: a slide only draws places named in its own source. If a source gives a finding for every country, every country it reports on is shaded. If the source names only a few examples from a larger group, the note says so. The map stays zoomed out far enough to show each location in its wider geographical context.

The Organisations, and Why These Ones

The map contains 111 organisations. Organised religion includes millions of congregations, orders, trusts and offices, so the directory cannot list every one. The selection uses a consistent test: is this organisation among the largest or most influential of its kind, or is it a body whose findings or rulings the story rests on?

That test is applied across several groups:

  • World headquarters and national churches: the central offices of the largest churches and movements, and the national bodies named in the story.
  • State religious authorities: government ministries and boards that run or regulate religion.
  • Treasuries, shrines and pilgrimage authorities: the offices that hold a religious body's assets, and the bodies that control major holy places.
  • Ministries, megachurches and broadcasters: organisations built around one preacher or one network.
  • Political and campaign groups: parties and organisations that work with or through religion.
  • Courts, regulators, inquiries and researchers: the bodies whose rulings, reports and datasets supply the evidence.

The legend includes a switch, Show only the largest of each kind. It hides the 19 smaller organisations included because the story names them. The directory is not a complete register of organised religion, and it is weighted toward Christianity and toward English-language sources; the Method section says where it is thinnest. Inclusion means that an organisation is a significant part of the subject or of the evidence. It is not, by itself, an allegation of wrongdoing.

Each organisation is placed in its headquarters city and coloured by category. Where several organisations share a city, their markers are arranged in a small ring so that each can be selected. Select an organisation to see what it is, what it controls and which chapters refer to it.

The Index and the Two Chapter Lists

The Index sits below the search box. It lists every organisation, every country named in the story and every documented record in alphabetical order. Beneath it are two lists of the same 17 chapters. The first follows the story in order. The second, Find Your Region in the Story, groups chapters by the places where their slides are set.

Places of Worship

The small dots are the 1,634,951 places of worship that volunteers have mapped in OpenStreetMap: churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, gurdwaras and shrines. Zoom in to separate nearby ones, and hover over a dot to see its name and religion. They are on the map so that the story can be set against the places it is about. The first chapter describes what is collected at each one.

OpenStreetMap is built by volunteers, so coverage is uneven. A region with few dots may simply be a region that has been mapped less.

How to Read It

Countries are shaded only while a story slide is playing. Outside the world maps that use a full dataset, a country that is not named in the story is a gap in the research, not evidence that the pattern is absent there.

An organisation's presence on the map means only that it is a significant part of organised religion or of the record about it. The documented records inside the chapters provide the evidence for the claims made about particular actions. Each record carries a source link so readers can check the underlying material and not rely on the map's description alone. Where an organisation disputed a finding or responded to it, the record says so.

The Method section explains what qualifies as a chapter, how slides are sourced, where the country data comes from, and which parts of the subject remain unresearched.

What the Map Shows

The map has three layers. The first is the story. The second is the directory of organisations, each placed at its headquarters. The third is the layer of places of worship from OpenStreetMap. Documented records appear within the story as supporting evidence and can also be viewed on their own.

What Counts as a Chapter

A chapter has to describe how the religion system works as a system. One event, one conviction or one bad actor does not qualify. A case appears only as an example inside a chapter, and only when it shows a structure that recurs. A fact about how rich a religious body is does not qualify on its own either. It belongs when it shows who controls the money and whether the givers can see it.

A second test is breadth. Each feature the map claims is shared by all major religions has to be shown in more than one of them. Where the evidence for a chapter comes from one religion only, the chapter says so and says why.

The map does not judge beliefs, rituals or private practice.

How the Story Is Ordered

The story is ordered the way an investigator would work through an organisation. It first defines the thing being examined and asks how it came to exist. It then starts at the top and goes down: who commands, who enforces, what the enforcers receive in return, where the money goes, and what happens to the person at the bottom. The final chapter is a written summary with no slides.

Every chapter begins with an introduction and a short bridge explaining what the slides add. Each slide then picks up from the one before. A one-line recap appears at the top of the next chapter's box.

How the Slides Are Built

Each slide is placed where the event or institution it describes is located and carries one source. If the slide is built around one of the map's documented records, it displays that record while the slide is playing. The text explains what a fact means immediately after presenting it, and turns large numbers into a scale readers can picture. These explanations are the map's interpretation of the evidence. The underlying figures and events are those reported by the cited source.

Countries are highlighted in the colour associated with the role they play and remain highlighted until the chapter ends. Lines connect places between which money, authority or people move. A line shows that a relationship exists. It does not claim to show the route taken.

Slides That Cover Every Country

The world maps use three datasets, all of which were already in this site's data collection. Every country each source reports on is drawn. No figures are adjusted or removed.

  • Humanists International, Freedom of Thought Report: the report rates every country against a fixed list of conditions. The map shows, for 196 countries and territories, whether a country's report records each of these: an established church or state religion; state law derived from religious law; religious control of family law or religious courts ruling on family matters; state funding of religious institutions or discriminatory tax exemptions; a compulsory or state-administered religious tax; apostasy outlawed; state funding of religious schools; and mandatory religious instruction in state-funded schools. The country pages were updated at different dates, and the year of each page is shown when you hover. The report is written from the standpoint of the non-religious, and its findings are shown as it publishes them. It is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0: Humanists International, Freedom of Thought Report, fot.humanists.international.
  • United States Commission on International Religious Freedom: the commission's 2023 list of 95 countries whose criminal law punishes blasphemy. Countries not on the list are shown in the lighter tone.
  • Pew Research Center: the Government Restrictions Index and the Social Hostilities Index for 2022, for 198 countries and territories, from the dataset Global Restrictions on Religion 2007-2022 as distributed by the Association of Religion Data Archives. Pew Research Center bears no responsibility for the analyses or interpretations of the data presented here. The opinions expressed herein, including any implications for policy, are those of the author and not of Pew Research Center.

The highest and lowest countries named in the text of the Pew slides are chosen from sovereign states, so that small territories do not dominate the list. Those territories are still shaded on the map.

How the Organisations Were Chosen and Placed

The About section explains the inclusion test. Each organisation is placed in its headquarters city, at city level. When several organisations share a city, their markers are spread around a small circle so each can be selected. This changes only how the markers appear on screen. Marker colours are adjusted against the basemap so they remain visible.

Organisation descriptions say what each body is. Where a description goes further, the claim comes from the source named on the card. Organisations that appear only as major bodies of a faith, and are not part of any chapter, carry a one-line description and a link to their own website.

The Places of Worship

The layer holds every OpenStreetMap feature tagged as a place of worship, downloaded on 26 November 2026 through the Overpass API: 1,634,951 points. Buildings mapped as outlines are placed at their centre. As asked for this map, features whose building is tagged as a school, kindergarten, college, university or town hall, and features whose name contains the words school, kindergarten, town hall, city hall or village hall, were removed: 1,000 in all. Nothing else was filtered. Each dot carries the name, religion and denomination recorded in OpenStreetMap, which are left as the volunteers entered them. At wide zooms, places that fall on the same point of the screen are thinned so the map stays readable; from zoom 9 every place is drawn. The data is © OpenStreetMap contributors and is available under the Open Database Licence.

Records

Each of the 42 documented records is supported by one of the following: a court ruling; a regulator's order; an official inquiry or government report; a body's own published accounts; a public vote; a research study; or reporting based on primary documents. Where an organisation responded to a finding or disputed it, the response is included in the record.

The map is far from complete. As a judgement and not a measured count, it gathers perhaps 3 to 6 per cent of the available evidence on how organised religion controls and collects from its members. The world maps cover nearly every country. The story's own examples do not. The first table shows how each religion is covered and the second shows each region.

Not yet covered

The chapter on abuse inside religious bodies draws only on Christian organisations, because that is where inquiries with legal powers have published their findings. The history chapter draws on the Roman Empire, Western Europe and Japan. Both are limits of this edition. Neither is a claim that other religions lack such a record.

What Was Left Out on Purpose

The map leaves out claims that could not be tied to a source checked for it. That is why several widely repeated figures are absent, among them estimates of total religious wealth and landholding, of money spent by governments to spread a faith abroad, and of the size of the halal and kosher certification trades. Organisations for which no checked claim was available appear with a neutral one-line description only.

The map leaves out single scandals. A fine, a conviction or a misused fund is not included unless it shows how the system is built.

The map leaves out charity. Religious bodies run hospitals, schools and food programmes on a large scale. That work is real, and it is not the subject here.

The map leaves out reforms, apologies and campaigns against the practices it describes, except where a slide needs one to explain a figure.

What Is Missing

The map does not yet cover caste and hereditary priesthoods; fees charged for rites such as weddings, funerals and prayers for the dead; religious police; shunning and other costs of leaving that fall outside the criminal law; religious orders and their finances; missionary organisations working today; certification industries; clergy pay and housing privileges; or the history of religious payment and state backing outside the Roman Empire, Western Europe and Japan. These are gaps in the research, not evidence that the subjects are unimportant.

Corrections

If a slide, record or organisation description is wrong, it should be corrected. Every slide and organisation card carries its source so that an individual claim can be checked without relying on the map's summary. Corrections should improve the record, not defend an error.
