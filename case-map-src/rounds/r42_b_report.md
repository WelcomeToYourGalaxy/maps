## The four flagged in the brief

**`secret-loans-banks-traders`.** What is documented is more than the Mozambique episode, which is
why this is the one promotion among the hard cases. The FCA's final notice of 19 October 2021 fines
Credit Suisse GBP 147,190,200 and finds the bank took a general representation of IMF compliance
"rather than undertake to inform the IMF of the loans in question", with the first loan's purpose
still not public knowledge in 2014. Separately, the World Bank's own board paper of 4 October 2018
has a box headed "Hidden Debt" naming Congo's oil-trader prefinancing contracts, undisclosed to the
debt management office for over two years, Togo's prefinanced debt, and Mozambique's guarantees "not
disclosed to the debt management staff and the public". Three sovereigns and two kinds of arranger,
in the lenders' and the regulator's own documents, is the practice and not the anecdote. What stays
open is the entry's own conviction line: that this is *routine*.

**`aid-traded-for-arms-sales`.** The judgment settles that the Pergau grant fell outside the
Overseas Development and Co-operation Act 1980 because the project could not be justified as
development, nothing about arms. I could not open the judgment itself (CaseMine is behind a captcha),
so I used the government's response to it: on 13 December 1994 the Foreign Secretary told the Commons
he had decided "not to appeal", and moved Pergau and three further aid-and-trade projects to funds
voted outside the Act. On the arms link the official record is a dispute, not a finding: in the
25 January 1995 debate members record that the Select Committee rejected Lord Younger's view that his
March 1988 protocol did not link aid to arms, and a minister answers that "there is no link between
our aid programme and defence sales, and there never has been."

**`forecasts-inflated-to-sell-loans`.** `overclaimed`, as the brief guessed. The IMF's own evaluation
office finds growth outcomes "consistently fell short of program projections", blames fiscal-multiplier
modelling, and says "political economy factors in program negotiations also played a significant role"
in ambitious projections. That is optimism, and pressure inside a negotiation; it is not forecasts
inflated so that a country borrows more. The two assertions share a trace and differ in intent, which
the entry's fourth paragraph already concedes.

**`companies-behind-coups`.** The title asserts authorship by the companies, and reads as history
rather than a continuing practice; the story's "this has not stopped" is the part with nothing behind
it. What the declassified record carries is the next thing over: the State Department's published note
shows Washington telling Guatemala in 1953 that the bonds offered for 234,000 acres of United Fruit
land were not "prompt and effective compensation". Governments overthrown after expropriating foreign
companies, with their home governments pressing the companies' claims, is documented. Direction by the
company is not, and the entry says so itself about United Fruit; in Chile the ITT offer was refused.

## The two I am least sure of

1. **`secret-loans-banks-traders`**, the only entry where I moved something up to `documented`. The
   boundary is real: Mozambique alone would be one proven instance, and the generalisation rests on
   one box in one World Bank paper, which names Congo and Togo without naming the arrangers for Togo.
   The IMF country reports that carry the Congo and Chad figures in the story are the obvious check
   and imf.org refused every request (403). If a reviewer reads that box as three instances rather
   than a practice, the verdict should fall back to `overclaimed`.
2. **`loans-cut-off-to-topple-governments`**, where I split the title in two. The Church Committee
   settles the instrument, in the words of NSDM 93 and in a table showing IDB lending at $46 million
   in 1970 and $2 million in 1972 with no IBRD disbursements in 1971-73. It is the purpose in the
   title, toppling, that only Chile supports, and Chile supports it strongly, since the same meeting
   that produced "make the economy scream" opened Track II. A reviewer who weighs Chile heavily could
   call the whole title `documented`.

`ratings-that-punish-relief` is the next closest call. The agencies' own reasoning is the record, but
I reached it through the African Union's peer review body and a press account of Moody's four-country
review rather than through Fitch or Moody's notices, both of which are walled.

## Entries whose sources do not support what the story says

- **`ratings-that-punish-relief`: "19 African countries could save up to $74.5 billion".** The UNDP
  brief I opened reports two samples, 13 countries for domestic-currency bonds and 16 for Eurobonds;
  the union of the two lists is 21 countries, and the brief gives $74.5 billion as the combined cost
  of rating idiosyncrasies in Africa without attaching a country count to it. No "19" appears in the
  brief. It may come from the UNDP press release, which returned 403 for me. The same figure sits in
  the entry's conviction line, so it wants checking before either is used again.
- **`secret-loans-banks-traders`: Chad's 98 per cent "by the end of 2016".** The joint World Bank-IMF
  debt sustainability analysis I opened puts Glencore's loan at about 98 per cent of Chad's commercial
  debt stock in 2017, not at end-2016. The entry cites an IMF country report I could not open, which
  may give an end-2016 figure; until someone opens it the year is unconfirmed.
- **`companies-behind-coups`: compensation "based on the value United Fruit itself had declared for
  tax".** The State Department note records only that government bonds were offered and that
  Washington called the amount inadequate. The tax-valuation point is not in the official record this
  entry leans on, though it is widely reported elsewhere.
