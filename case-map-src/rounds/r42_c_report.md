Four overclaimed entries carry a narrower title in the JSON; the two duplicates name the pattern and what is left over.

## The four the brief flagged

**`private-courts-for-investors`.** The title asserts the existence and power of investor-state
tribunals. That is settled by treaty and by the registry: ICSID's own case pages record the Tethyan
award of 12 July 2019 and the Occidental award of 5 October 2012, with the dissent attached, and the
map counts 1,332 treaty cases from UNCTAD's navigator. The documented core also duplicates
`company-sued-the-state`, which already lists claims and awards case by case and names ICSID as a
World Bank Group body. What is genuinely open is in paragraph 3, not the title: the 15-arbitrators
count, the board seats, the double-hatting and regulatory chill. The advocacy study behind the
count was answered in print by arbitration practitioners, and the entry's own last source is that
answer. If the entry stays, it needs a title that asserts one of those. The mining and oil awards
against Pakistan and Ecuador would also widen the existing pattern past privatized services.

**`development-money-through-tax-havens`.** Paragraph 2 is `offshore-routing-lost-tax` restated:
UNCTAD's $100 billion, the IMF economists' 40 per cent of FDI through empty companies, Mauritius
among the ten hosts and Senegal's $257 million treaty termination all appear in the pattern on the
page. The leftover is the one thing the page does not cover: the lenders' own portfolio. Oxfam's
count of IFC clients, 51 of 68 companies in sub-Saharan Africa in 2015 and 84 per cent of the
regional money, with the rise from $1.20 billion in 2010 to $2.87 billion in 2015, is a count of
the IFC's published disclosures, repeated by the Bretton Woods Project, and the IFC disputed the
reading rather than the numbers. That leftover reads as documented, not suspected, and belongs
beside the existing pattern. The genuinely open question — whether the haven structures cut the tax
those countries collect — is the entry's paragraph 4 and is not what the title says.

**`spying-at-the-negotiating-table`.** The leaked documents settle that signals intelligence has
operated inside economic negotiations. The GCHQ material on the 2009 London G20 (internet cafes set
up for delegates, keystroke logging, real-time call records, reports delivered during the summit) is
specific and was never contested on authenticity, and the NSA documents name Petrobras. They do not
settle the title's two extra elements. The G20 targets were allies and middle-income states, not
poor countries; and the one poor-country resource case, the 2004 bugging of Timor-Leste's cabinet,
Australia has never officially confirmed — it is known through a former officer who was himself
convicted for disclosing it, which is a disclosure by a person, not a finding.

**`banks-that-bank-the-looters`.** The record settles particular banks and episodes, not the
practice named in the title. The Senate subcommittee's own heading for Riggs is "Turning a Blind
Eye", and Riggs pleaded guilty to failing to report suspicious transactions under the Bank Secrecy
Act, not to handling stolen money. The FSA's investigation found 23 banks holding Abacha-linked
money and 15 with severe anti-money-laundering weaknesses, which is a finding of failure, named no
bank and prosecuted none. The FinCEN Files show banks moving payments they had themselves flagged as
suspicious, which is suspicion raised by the bank, not theft established. "Knowingly" holds for one
bank; "routinely, because it pays" is the suspicion.

## The two that stay suspected, and what would settle them

**`lenders-expect-the-riots`.** The pieces are sound: price rises written into programmes, protests,
deaths, and a measured association. The paper I opened (Dinkel and Nelson, December 2019) re-estimates
on 120 countries for 1972-2007, instrumenting for the fact that troubled countries get programmes
anyway, and finds repression worse under IMF programmes and worse as conditions multiply — and it
exists precisely because the earlier estimates were being argued over, so the size of the effect is
still contested. Foreknowledge rests on one leaked Ecuador strategy and on Stiglitz's account to a
reporter. What would settle it is public: a count across published IMF staff reports and World Bank
programme documents of how often the risk sections forecast unrest before a major price rise, and
whether the programme changed when they did. That count is doable now and nobody has done it.

**`rescue-money-timed-for-elections`.** The dates and amounts are documented and one of them is a
sitting president saying out loud that support depended on the result. The academic support is
thinner than the entry's paragraph 3 reads: Dreher, Marchesi and Vreeland find that countries voting
with the United States get lower IMF inflation forecasts as elections approach, but the result is
significant in their GMM estimates and not significant at conventional levels in the OLS marginal
effects, and it is about forecasts, not about when money arrives. The Claver-Carone account is a
reported remark at a private teleconference, repeated by senators. What would settle it: the US
Treasury's voting instructions and the board minutes for the 1996 Russian and 2018 Argentine
arrangements, plus a count across all IMF programmes of approvals and disbursements in the months
before elections, set against each government's voting alignment with the largest shareholders.

## The two I am least sure of

1. **`development-money-through-tax-havens`** — calling it a duplicate is a judgement about where the
   line falls. Read strictly, its title ("development money") names something the page's pattern does
   not cover, which would make it `documented` on the IFC count instead. I called it a duplicate
   because its second paragraph is the existing pattern verbatim in substance and because the Oxfam
   briefing itself would not open for me: I have the figures from two reports of it, not from the
   document. Either way the entry should not stay as a suspicion.
2. **`lending-to-known-thieves`** — the line between `suspected` and `overclaimed` is thin here. Two
   institution-level documents recording staff knowledge of large-scale theft, with lending
   continuing in both, is more than one anecdote, and the entry's own conviction line is honestly
   scoped ("how often this happened is the suspicion"). I went to `overclaimed` because a narrower
   statement naming Zaire and Indonesia is fully documented, which is the test's own tie-breaker, but
   `suspected` would be defensible.

## Where sources do not support what the story already says

These are defects for a later pass, not classifications.

- **`lending-to-known-thieves`, paragraph 2.** The memo's "reports of 50 to 80 per cent being taken
  from money meant for land purchase and resettlement" appears only in the House of Commons appendix
  (`.../cmintdev/39/39ap07.htm`), which refused every route I tried (403 to curl on both hostnames,
  PROVENANCE_REQUIRED from WebFetch with an instruction not to retry, archive.org reset). The WSJ
  mirror I did open carries the 20-30 per cent estimate, the "our controls have little practical
  effect" line and Severino's remark, but not the 50-80 per cent figure. It is unverified.
- **`lending-to-known-thieves`, paragraph 1.** The story says the report "leaked from Zaire"; the
  Christian Science Monitor piece it cites reports the leak in Brussels. Probe International, also
  cited, says Zaire owed $5 billion when Blumenthal wrote, while the CSM gives $4.1 billion for the
  debt the report deals with — the entry takes $4.1 billion, which the CSM supports, but the two
  sources on the card disagree and nothing on the card says so.
- **`markets-opened-for-subsidized-food`, paragraph 1.** The AP piece carries Clinton's words, the 3
  per cent tariff and the 80 per cent import share, but says only that "free-market advocates in
  Washington and Europe pushed Haiti"; it never mentions the IMF or the World Bank. The programme
  attribution comes from Type Investigations, which does state it plainly. The claim is supported,
  but by the second source, not the one the paragraph opens on.
- **`banks-that-bank-the-looters`, paragraph 1.** The story gives Riggs's Equatorial Guinea balances
  as "up to $700 million". The Senate report says deposits ranged from $400 to $700 million at a
  time, and in its findings gives $700 million as balances *and outstanding loans together*. Also
  worth a line: the plea was for failure to report, and it covered Pinochet's accounts as well as
  Equatorial Guinea's, which the story does not mention.
- **`banks-that-bank-the-looters`, paragraph 2.** The 42 accounts and the $1.3 billion through them
  come from the FSA's own March 2001 press release, which I could not reach (National Archives
  returned 405). The secondary I opened gives 23 banks and 15 with severe weaknesses, not the account
  count or the flow.
- **`private-courts-for-investors`, sources.** The Debevoise URL in the entry's source list is a 404.
  Separately, ICSID records the claimant as Tethyan Copper Company Pty Limited, an Australian
  company suing under the Pakistan-Australia treaty; the story describes it only by its Canadian and
  Chilean owners, which is true of ownership but hides why an Australian treaty was the instrument.
- **`spying-at-the-negotiating-table`.** The load-bearing Timor-Leste case is sourced to Wikipedia
  first; paragraph 3 is still the single sentence the round 28 check flagged; and the entry has no
  `case_ids`, so `entryPts()` has only countries to fly to. `development-money-through-tax-havens`,
  `banks-that-bank-the-looters` and `debt-for-nature-deals` also have empty `case_ids`.
- **`debt-for-nature-deals`.** The six-of-eleven board split is Latindadd's, an advocacy network, not
  the fund's own constitution, and the story says so only by naming them. The IDB complaint is an
  allegation with no finding.
