## The two I am least sure of

**`water-handed-to-companies` — documented or overclaimed.** I promoted it on the Bank's own IEG
evaluation, which shows the Bank designing projects to bring private operators into city water
across its portfolio, not just in Cochabamba, Jakarta and Manila. What makes me uneasy is the word
"advice" in the title doing two jobs: the IEG document shows the Bank *aiming* at private
participation in its own projects, which is stronger than advice, but it does not show the Bank
telling a government to privatise where the government had not asked — and on the entry's own
second case the Bank's 1998 report says the opposite (see the defect below). If the owner reads the
title's "on the lenders' advice" as a claim about each city rather than about the programme, this
should drop to `overclaimed` with the narrower title "Cities' water handed to private companies in
projects the World Bank designed to bring them in".

**`companies-pay-armed-groups` — documented or overclaimed.** A guilty plea settles one company's
conduct, and REVIEW42 is explicit that one proven instance is not a pattern. I went to `documented`
because the record is not one instance: two guilty pleas on two continents, and a third company
still disclosing the payments in its current SEC filing as ordinary and defensible practice. A
disclosure that recurs every year in a 10-K is different in kind from an exposed scandal. But the
entry's own conviction line says "routinely", and routine is exactly what no document shows — there
is no disclosure requirement, so no count. If the owner wants the title to carry "routinely", the
verdict is `overclaimed` and the narrower title is "Foreign companies that have paid soldiers and
militias to guard their operations".

## Entries whose sources do not support what the story already says

This is the part worth more than the classification. Five findings, in order of how much they matter.

1. **`water-handed-to-companies`, paragraph 2, uses half of a sentence.** The story says "a 1998
   Bank report said the loan had facilitated it". The report
   ([ICR, 23 June 1998](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/266251468039271153/text/multi-page.txt))
   calls the privatisation "parallel to but facilitated by the Project" and, in the same sentence,
   says it was not envisaged by the government or the Bank at appraisal; a few pages on it says that
   in the late 1980s, had the government proposed a private-participation option, the Bank would
   most likely have turned it down. So the entry's own document says Jakarta's privatisation was not the Bank's
   design. The story quotes the clause that helps it and omits the clause that cuts against it. That
   needs fixing however the verdict lands.

2. **`rankings-bent-for-money` leaves out two of the four countries, including the one that breaks
   the pattern.** WilmerHale investigated improper changes for China (DB2018) and for Saudi Arabia,
   the United Arab Emirates **and Azerbaijan** (DB2020). The story names only China and Saudi
   Arabia. Azerbaijan matters because its data was changed *downward*, and the report's own
   conclusion is that Mr Djankov appeared to harbour a personal animus against the country — a
   motive with no money in it, in the same investigation the entry rests on. HYP's fairness rule
   points the same way.

3. **`carbon-credits-new-land-grab` presents a judgment that is under appeal as settled.** The
   31 January 2025 Environment and Land Court judgment is correctly dated and correctly summarised,
   but Kenya Law's index lists a Court of Appeal ruling in Northern Rangelands Trust's appeal
   (Civil Appeal (Application) E019 of 2026) on 25 September 2026. The story's paragraph 3 reads as
   though the ruling stands unchallenged.

4. **`seed-laws-for-seed-companies`: the two prior-action findings are not equally evidenced, and the
   story treats them as one.** CAFOD links World Bank loan documents for the hybrid-seed-and-
   fertiliser subsidies (I opened the Kenya and Guinea completion reports and the prior actions are
   there). For the seed-certification laws in seven countries — the claim the title rests on — CAFOD
   links nothing, and I found nothing. The entry's opening claim that the Bank held Tanzania up as a
   model in 2016 for joining UPOV 1991 is also unverified: I opened the 2017 edition, not the 2016
   one, and the 2017 edition scores plant variety protection without naming Tanzania that way.

5. **`aid-cover-for-spies` uses a weaker source than the one it already carries.** The Office of
   Public Safety paragraph still rests on Wikipedia for "about 1,500 advisers to at least 52
   countries" and for Byron Engle being a CIA operative. The entry's own Federation of American
   Scientists source is the Congressional Record of 5 March 1992, in which Senator Cranston releases
   a GAO report: it gives $60 million a year training police in 34 countries by 1968 through the AID
   Office of Public Safety, the dozen exemptions to the 1974 ban, and the statement that US
   intelligence agencies "were given an important role" in the development and execution of those
   programmes. That is a better
   footing for the same paragraph and it is already in `sources`.

Two smaller notes. `aid-into-offshore-accounts` gives 7.5 per cent as the leak; the paper also gives
about 5 per cent for aggregate disbursements, and its own words are "suggestive of aid diversion" rather than a finding of theft —
worth a half-sentence in the story. `companies-pay-armed-groups`
gives Freeport nearly $20 million for 1998-2004 from the New York Times; the current 10-K discloses
the practice but no figure, so the figure stays single-sourced.
