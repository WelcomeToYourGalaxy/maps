Are they likely to be fixed anytime soon?

No study measures how many food-contamination problems get solved. This is a sorting of the 176 entries on this page into three kinds, checked against how similar problems have gone in the past.

Kind of problemShare of entriesHow past cases went
Single substances or practices that can be banned
dyes, titanium dioxide, potassium bromate, specific pesticides, phthalates, BPA, growth drugs, processing contaminants		28%49 entriesThey do get banned, but slowly and one country at a time. Brominated vegetable oil was banned in the UK in 1970 and in the US only in 2024, 54 years later. Red No. 3 took about 35 years to go from a cosmetics ban to a food ban. The chlorpyrifos ban petition dates from 2007, and the pesticide is still allowed on 11 US crops. Banned chemicals are often replaced with something as bad or worse; Harvard researchers point to BPS replacing BPA.
Persistent pollutants
lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, dioxins and PCBs, PFAS, microplastics, aflatoxin, chlordecone		27%48 entriesUnlikely to be resolved within a lifetime, even after bans. The US banned PCBs in 1979, and they are still found in fish, meat, poultry and dairy. Leaded petrol took about 97 years from the first deaths in 1924 to its global end in 2021, and 1 in 3 children still have high blood lead.
Structural problems
inspection rates, companies approving their own chemicals, chemicals never identified or tested, fraud, corporate behavior, the insect and filth limits		45%79 entriesNo past case of problems like these being fixed turned up. The FDA reaches about 5% of the foreign-inspection target set in 2011, and the US defect handbook has not been revised since 1998.

So about 28% of the problems on this page are the kind that eventually gets resolved, usually after 35 to 55 years, one country at a time, and often with a substitute chemical. About 72% are unlikely to be resolved for a long time. The 27 insect and filth entries are one rule system; counted as a single problem, the split becomes 33% that can be banned and 67% unlikely to be resolved soon.

Even the “organic”

Organic produce carries pesticide residues too. In a study of 136 commercial samples of certified organic produce, 61 (45%) contained pesticides that are not authorized for organic farming, and 4 more contained authorized ones. A 2014 study found one or more residues in 15 of 27 organic samples (55%). The reviewing scientists concluded: “We are not able to claim that the organic crops do not contain pesticide residues.”1 Canada’s own food inspection data from the same year showed 45.8% of organic samples carrying pesticide traces, against 78.4% of conventional ones.2 Among organic crops tested from US Department of Agriculture data, 9% had at least one pesticide above the legal tolerance.5

Organic farms are not supposed to use synthetic pesticides, but residue tests to confirm this rarely happen; certification rests mostly on paperwork. Critic Mischa Popoff compares it to “asking Olympic athletes to sign a document saying they used no performance-enhancing drugs but never testing them.”3 Imports are weaker still: a 2017 USDA audit found the agency was unable to provide reasonable assurance that the documents required for imported organic products were reviewed at ports of entry, a gap that matters most for untested paperwork-only imports such as those from China.4

The testing that does exist is thin. In 2019, 77 accredited certifying agents (60 in the US, 17 abroad) oversaw 44,896 organic operations worldwide, about 583 each, and they are required to residue-test only 5% of operations a year. That is about 29 operations per agent, leaving roughly 554 untested each year.4 At that rate a complete round of testing takes about 20 years,5 so even an operation certified since the US organic law of 1990 would on average have been tested only once or twice, with about 20 years between tests. Pest outbreaks can leave a farmer with little choice but synthetic sprays, and residues dissipate: conventionally grown crops often test residue-free by harvest and could pass an organic test.5 Certifiers also charge growers for each visit and earn much of their income as a percentage of the sales of the crops they approve, which Popoff calls an active conflict of interest.3

Even with this limited oversight, 722 organic operations in 50 countries lost their certification in 2019,4 and 326 growers had certification suspended in a single six-month period.5 With the organic sector growing and supply chains lengthening, those caught are likely a fraction of the true scope.
