What the % after each label means
Take the label Movement below. 0 methods on the chart are things a movement does, and each has its own measured win rate. Line those up from worst to best and take the one in the middle — that is the number shown. So half the things a movement can do worked more often than that, and half worked less often. The middle rather than the average, so one freak result cannot drag it.

Three kinds of row are left out on purpose. Methods nobody has counted are excluded, which is why some labels carry no percentage at all. Rows reporting an effect size — how far something moved a number — are excluded, because they answer a different question. So are rows measured as leaving people worse off than doing nothing. What predicts success — and how far each finding travels

These factors are not methods themselves. They are things that can make a method more or less likely to succeed, which is why they are not separate rows on the chart. There are eleven method families here, and none of these findings has been tested across more than three of them. So these should be treated as possibilities, not laws.

1 What you ask for

This is the strongest candidate. The evidence suggests narrow demands work better than broad ones, and that asking to keep something can work better than asking to change something.

The clearest example comes from strikes: strikes asking for a wage increase plus shorter hours succeeded 83.6% of the time, while strikes asking for union recognition succeeded 12.4% of the time. A 71-point difference, in the same country and decade. The pattern has also appeared in a modern petitions dataset, so two research traditions looking at two families found a similar relationship.

That is still only 2 of the 11 families. It has never been tested in litigation, referendums, boycotts, divestment or whistleblowing, and there is no obvious reason asking for something in a courtroom should work like asking an employer.

Start by examining how the demand is framed, but do not assume the same effect applies everywhere.

2 What the other side is allowed to do

In strikes, one of the strongest predictors of success is not what the workers do. It is what the employer is legally allowed to do in response. Across 4,528 US strikes with complete data, the employer's use of replacement workers was the most powerful predictor of the outcome. Other employer powers — locking workers out, freezing union money — also shifted the balance.

All of them give the opponent a way to attack the campaign's ability to keep operating and financing itself, which is also why solidarity that stops at the struck company's gate may not be enough.

Nobody has tested whether other methods have equivalent variables — whether a defendant in a lawsuit, say, holds some legal power that makes victory much easier or harder.

Possibly the most promising idea here that has never been tested across methods: look closely at what the opponent is legally allowed to do to defeat your pressure.

3 Whether the sides reach a negotiated settlement

Disputes that end through mediation, conciliation or negotiation tend to do better than disputes where one side simply gives up. In labour research this has been found twice — once through a study designed to identify a causal effect, once observationally — in two countries roughly a century apart. The strongest causal study found that bringing in a mediator raised the probability of compromise by about 30%.

There is an important problem: disputes already likely to end well may also be the ones most likely to reach mediation, so mediation may not be causing all of the improvement. There is no comparable evidence for the other families.

Negotiation may be broadly useful, but the strongest evidence is from labour. Do not assume it works the same way everywhere.

4 Whether the government supports or opposes you

The same tactic can work very differently depending on what the state does. In France, mediators tended to favour workers, so radical unions could win large strikes by getting officials involved — which also made those unions more radical. In the United States, unions faced a more hostile state, avoided relying on intervention, and succeeded instead by funding smaller groups of strategically important workers.

The same basic tactic produced opposite strategies because the surrounding political environment differed.

Do not ask only "what tactic works?" Ask "what does the government do when this tactic is used?"

5 How long the campaign lasts

For strikes, longer strikes tended to produce less valuable settlements. Long strikes were generally associated with weaker results rather than being heroic campaigns that eventually forced surrender, probably because of the difficulty of maintaining pressure and financing over time.

This has not been properly tested across the other families.

Do not assume that keeping a campaign going longer makes it stronger.

6 Organisation

Most union strikes succeeded; most non-union strikes failed. That suggests organisation matters. But the union success rate did not increase over time even as unions became better organised, so the numbers do not prove organisation itself caused the difference.

The broader lesson may be that an organisation gives a campaign the ability to coordinate people, maintain participation, raise money and keep operating — and that this may matter differently for getting into a channel than for winning once inside it.

Organisation appears important, but the evidence does not tell us how much of its effect comes from organisation itself.

7 Rules that decide the outcome before persuasion does

Some methods have formal rules that determine whether a campaign succeeds before the public or the decision-maker has changed their mind. This is clearest with referendums and other direct-democracy instruments: minimum turnout requirements, requiring a share of the entire electorate, or requiring approval by more than one voting group. The same instrument can have dramatically different success rates under different rules, and in those cases the procedural rules matter more than public opinion.

The finding applies only to methods that have formal thresholds, and most here do not. The question transfers even where the finding does not: is there a procedural rule that can defeat the campaign before anyone's mind has to be changed? Legal standing requirements, union membership thresholds and signature requirements all qualify, and campaigns usually give them far less attention than persuasion.

Before trying to change minds, check whether a procedural rule can decide the matter first.

8 How many people participate

You will hear that a movement needs 3.5% of the population to win. It is worth knowing exactly where that comes from, because it is quoted far outside what it measured.

One researcher assembled every large campaign between 1900 and 2006 that tried to overthrow a government, expel a foreign occupier or win independence, and found that every campaign which got about 3.5% of the population participating at its peak had succeeded — and all of those were nonviolent. In a country of 60 million, 3.5% is roughly two million people out on the biggest day.

Two things get dropped whenever the figure is repeated. The first is that the researcher says plainly that most successful campaigns never came near 3.5%. It is a level at which success became close to certain, not a level you have to reach. The second is what was in the dataset: campaigns aiming to remove a government, directed at a state, running over months or years. Not one strike, lawsuit, referendum or boycott is in there, and nobody has checked whether a participation threshold exists for any of them.

There is no reason to think 3.5% of anything is the number for winning a court case.

Treat it as a finding about very large campaigns against states, not a target for every campaign.

9 How democratic the opponent is

Campaigns generally do better against more democratic opponents, which makes intuitive sense. But one comparison that measured a method under both an autocracy and a parliamentary system found a difference of only a few percentage points — smaller than the differences produced by how the demand was framed, by whether the employer could replace workers, and by how researchers defined a win versus a compromise.

That is one comparison in one family, so it does not prove regime type matters little. It does mean the assumption should be tested rather than assumed.

Democracy probably matters, but its effect may be smaller or more complicated than intuition suggests.

10 Repression can sometimes backfire

Violent repression of opposition campaigns can increase sympathy for the campaign, hurting the government that carried it out. But this comes from the same dataset as the participation-threshold finding and concerns maximalist campaigns against states, so the two are not independent confirmations of a universal rule. Whether repression of a strike, occupation, lawsuit or boycott produces the same effect is unknown.

Repression can backfire, but the evidence is limited to one type of campaign.

11 Attention can matter more than direct financial damage

For boycotts, the strongest predictor of impact appears to be how much attention the boycott generates rather than how much revenue it directly costs the target. That is specifically a finding about boycotts. It would be worth testing whether the same holds for divestment campaigns and shareholder resolutions, where people usually assume the mechanism is financial pressure.

For boycotts, getting noticed may matter more than the immediate money lost.

12 Money may determine access more than victory

One large study of federal lobbying found that the resources each side had explained relatively little of who ultimately won. That does not mean money does not matter. A better reading: money may determine whether you can enter a channel at all, but once both sides are inside it, money becomes much less important in deciding who wins.

Money might buy access, specialists or relationships. After both sides have comparable access to the same process, having more of it may not by itself determine the outcome. The study covers federal lobbying in one country, so the specific number should not be generalised.

Distinguish between getting into the arena and winning once you are there.

13 Orderly versus disruptive action affects different audiences

Experimental research on public opinion finds that orderly, normative action tends to mobilise people who already agree, while disruptive action and radical flanks can influence audiences that do not. But this research measures public opinion, not whether a campaign wins its demand — and getting people to think differently is not the same as getting a government, corporation or court to change a decision.

A real finding, but it answers a somewhat different question from "what makes a campaign win?"
