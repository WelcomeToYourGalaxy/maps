What the map shows

Every country and territory in the map's boundaries file, colored by one measure at a time from the menu at the top left. The map it replaces showed one thing — a 2017 snapshot of independence written into law, for the countries one study coded. Here the same question is answered from the two current legal studies (with every rule they score), and joined by documented corruption inside central banks, corruption, secrecy and money-laundering risk in the countries and banking systems around them, what each central bank discloses, how often governments have pressed it in practice, documented moves against its leaders from 2019 to October 2026, who owns it, whether its money is issued or backed from outside, and how far its currency and balance sheet reach into the rest of the world.

Choosing a country opens its box on the right with every figure the sources hold for it, a line of its legal independence over time, the 42 individual rules in Romelli's index, documented events with their sources, and its swap lines. The year slider (for the legal and transparency measures) shows the map as it stood in any year the source covers.

Places in a currency union — the 21 euro-area countries, the eight countries of the West African BCEAO, the six of the Central African BEAC, and the eight members of the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank — share one central bank for monetary policy. Where a source scores only the union bank, its members carry that score and their boxes say so. Places that use another country's currency carry that currency's reach figures, also marked.

Nothing has been filtered out. Every place each source publishes is on the map; places a source does not cover are shown in the dark "no figure" color rather than estimated.

The three summary layers

Outside influence: how far governments and foreign powers can steer the bank. It averages what the bank's law allows the government (1 − Romelli's index, or Garriga's where Romelli has none), reported government pressure 2010–18, documented moves against the bank 2019–26 (firings, prosecutions, frozen reserves), and whether its money is issued, backed or fixed from abroad.

Inside influence: how far people inside the bank have used it, or could use it, for others' ends. It averages documented corruption cases inside the bank since 1990, officials moving between the bank and private finance, private shareholders sitting inside its governance, and how little the bank tells the public.

Ulterior influence, all around (the default): the average of the two scores above and a third for the corruption and secrecy around the bank (corruption in the country by Transparency International and the World Bank, money-laundering risk in its banks, and laws that hide who owns money). It is scored where at least two of the three have data.

Each measure runs from 0 (shielded) to 1 (exposed), and scores are shown out of 100. Counts are divided by the highest count anywhere. Bank-rescue costs are a layer of their own and are left out of the scores, because a rescue is not wrongdoing by itself.

The weights are equal by choice; no source sets them. A country with no documented inside case scores 0 on that measure. That means none was found in the sources searched, not that none happened. Euro-area countries share the ECB's scores and cases where they have no figures of their own.

Who central banks answer to

The box at the lower right, under Charts, holds two pull-down lists: "Who central banks answer to" and "Patterns behind the scenes". In "Who central banks answer to", tick one or more kinds of tie (or "Tick all"). The countries are colored by how many ties of the ticked kinds their central bank has, or by the type of owners when only "Who owns it" is ticked, and the ties between central banks and outside parties are drawn on the map. A short key, "What the map shows", says what the colors, diamonds and lines mean. Each diamond is a party at its true head office, sized by how many central banks it is tied to. Each party keeps one fixed spot at every zoom. Parties in the same city sit a few kilometers apart around the city point, so that spot shows the city, not the street address. When zoomed out they overlap: click a stack to pick from a list. Under the tick boxes, the filter narrows the parties to those tied to at least a set number of central banks, or to one chosen central bank. The index, under Find a place, searches every party, person, case, event, pattern and chart on the map. Each line joins one party to one central bank, colored by the main kind of tie between them. Point at a diamond or line to light up its ties, and click either for a box that lists each kind of tie and what the sources say. Lines are drawn straight in longitude and latitude, so they never cross the map's edge. "Patterns behind the scenes" lists the recurring patterns; choose one to show it and what it means.

Seven kinds of tie, each a row of its own under "Who central banks answer to": owning shares or sitting in the bank's governance; paying or bribing officials, or receiving money, favors or leaks from the bank; individuals shared through the revolving door, board seats and forums such as the Group of Thirty; membership of the same institutions (the Bank for International Settlements, which 63 central banks own, and the Financial Stability Board); pressuring, firing or prosecuting leaders, freezing the bank's money, or guaranteeing its currency from abroad; services such as printing banknotes, auditing and managing funds, and parties named in cases; and courts, prosecutors and inquiries that investigated or tried officials, kept apart because they check the bank rather than steer it.

Each tie comes from the case, ownership record, member list, contract or report linked beside it. A tie is not, by itself, an accusation of wrongdoing. Head offices were found from each party's own site, official registers or reference pages, where the original source did not state them; markers still placed at a country's centre are flagged in their box. Claims from books that primary records have not confirmed are labeled as the author's claim.

Patterns behind the scenes

Each pattern layer maps one recurring structure that connects central banks to outside interests across countries or over time. Some are axes and clubs: New York and London; the Basel clubs (BIS, FSB, Group of Thirty); the City of London and its offshore islands; the four firms auditing most central banks. Some are blocs: France's franc zone in Africa; the Fed's swap club against the People's Bank of China's network; the Gulf's dollar pegs. Some are chains and schemes: the Wall Street revolving door; frozen and seized reserves; the banknote-printer bribery cases; Lebanon's "financial engineering". Three are waves over time: independence laws, banking crises, and governments removing governors. Each layer colors the countries by their role, draws its connections, and gives a plain summary, a dated timeline, the sources, the claims that remain an author's alone, and what is still unproven.

The patterns were researched for this map from central bank histories and archives, official reports, court records, academic work and major news outlets, listed in each layer. The wave counts come from the Garriga, Romelli and Laeven–Valencia datasets. A reform by a shared central bank counts once for each member country.

Map controls

"No country colors" at the top of the menu leaves the countries plain so the basemap and lines show clearly. "Place names" in the basemap box turns the basemap's labels off and on.

Independence written into law

  • Romelli, Central Bank Independence – Extended (CBIE). 155 places, central bank laws from 1923 to 2023, 42 rules in six parts, each part weighted equally; 0 = no independence, 1 = full. Davide Romelli (2022), "The political economy of reforms in central bank design: evidence from a new dataset," Economic Policy 37(112): 641–688; and Romelli (2024), "Trends in central bank independence: a de-jure perspective," BAFFI CAREFIN Research Paper 217. Data: cbidata.org (2025 release, codebook 2024).
  • Garriga, Central Bank Independence dataset, version 3. 192 places, 1970–2023, coded on the Cukierman, Webb and Neyapti (1992) method: governor, objectives, policy formulation, limits on lending (weighted index). Ana Carolina Garriga (2025), "Revisiting Central Bank Independence in the World: An Extended Dataset," International Studies Quarterly 69(2), doi:10.1093/isq/sqaf024. Data: Garriga's data page.

Both measure what the law says, not how the bank behaves. A high legal score sits beside documented firings in some countries; the pressure layers exist for that reason.

What it tells the public

Dincer, Eichengreen and Geraats central bank transparency index, 112 central banks, 1998–2019, 0–15 in five parts (political, economic, procedural, policy, operational), each 0–3. N. Dincer, B. Eichengreen and P. Geraats (2022), "Trends in Monetary Policy Transparency: Further Updates," International Journal of Central Banking 18(1). Data: Eichengreen's data page (DEG, December 2020 file).

Pressure in practice

  • Binder, Political Pressure on Central Banks. 118 central banks, every quarter 2010–2018, coded from Economist Intelligence Unit country reports: whether the government pressed the bank, in which direction, and whether it threatened the leaders, the law, or demanded money printing. Carola Binder (2021), "Political Pressure on Central Banks," Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 53(4). Data: OSF.
  • Documented moves, 2019 – October 2026. Compiled for this map, one record per event, each with its source in the country's box (central banks, courts, governments, Al Jazeera, Central Banking and others). A few records rest on Wikipedia articles that cite the original reporting; the box shows which. Not exhaustive: events were included only where a readable source stated them.

Corruption inside and around the bank

  • Documented cases at central banks, 1990 – October 2026. Compiled for this map, one record per case, each with its source in the country's box (courts, prosecutors, official inquiries and audits, the banks themselves, and news agencies). Cases resting only on Wikipedia are marked. Acquittals, dismissed cases, withdrawn charges and closed investigations are listed and not counted. Unproven allegations are marked as alleged and are counted, as are open charges and investigations. Not exhaustive: a case is included only where a readable source states it.
  • Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 and 2012–2024, Transparency International, transparency.org/cpi (182 places).
  • Control of Corruption, World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators, 2025 edition, 0–100 score, from the World Bank data API (GOV_WGI_CC.SC).
  • Basel AML Index 2025, Basel Institute on Governance, public edition, 177 places, index.baselgovernance.org; scores read from the 2025 report.
  • Financial Secrecy Index 2025, Tax Justice Network, 141 places, fsi.taxjustice.net; figures from the Network's own published chart data.
  • Systemic banking crises 1970–2025: L. Laeven and F. Valencia (2026), "Systemic Banking Crises Database: 1970–2025," IMF Working Paper 26/94, Table A1 (crisis years, public cost of restructuring banks, central bank lending to banks as a share of deposits). Episodes the authors call borderline are marked.

Owners and whose money it is

  • Owners. Central banks with shareholders other than the state, from each bank's statute, annual report or exchange listing (links in each box).
  • Whose money it is. Currency unions, foreign-treasury guarantees (the CFA franc zones and the Comoros), countries using another country's currency, and currency boards, from the union and central bank statutes and finance ministries (links in each box).
  • Exchange-rate regime and anchor. E. Ilzetzki, C. Reinhart and K. Rogoff (2019), "Exchange Arrangements Entering the Twenty-First Century: Which Anchor Will Hold?", Quarterly Journal of Economics 134(2), and (2021) "Rethinking Exchange Rate Regimes," Handbook of International Economics vol. 5. Monthly anchor and fine classification to December 2025, beta release of 5 October 2026: ilzetzki.com/irr-data. The map's four regime colors group the 15 fine codes into the authors' own coarse groups; the box gives the fine code.

Reach beyond its borders

  • Currency trading. BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey, OTC foreign exchange turnover, April 2025, net-net, all counterparties ($9.51 trillion a day). Shares computed from the BIS data portal bulk file (WS_DER_OTC_TOV); the US dollar's 89.1% matches the BIS release.
  • Reserves. IMF, Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves (COFER), 2026 Q2, shares of allocated reserves, from the IMF data API. Total reserves $13.22 trillion; "other currencies" 6.06%.
  • Payments. Swift Global Currency Tracker (formerly the RMB Tracker), June 2026 data, published July 2026: share of payments by value, euro-area payments included; top 20 currencies.
  • Central bank size. BIS, central bank total assets (WS_CBTA), break-adjusted, end-2025, in US dollars and as % of GDP, from the BIS data portal.
  • Gold. World Gold Council, Gold Reserves by Country, based on IMF IFS (September 2026 edition); most countries June 2026, late reporters at their latest quarter (date in each box).
  • Swap lines. Compiled for this map from the central banks' own announcements and reports (Federal Reserve, ECB, People's Bank of China and its RMB Internationalization Report, Bank of Japan and Japan's Ministry of Finance, and others). Each line's source is in the boxes of both banks.
  • Anchor ties. From the Ilzetzki–Reinhart–Rogoff anchor file above; lines run from the issuing central bank to each country measured against its currency. Dashed lines are countries that float against that reference.

Names, currencies and shapes

Central bank names: the banks' own sites. Currencies: ISO 4217 as listed in the datasets/country-codes table. Where a country lists two legal tenders, its reach figures use the first that any reach source reports. Country shapes: the Culprits map's boundaries file; dots for places too small to draw, from Natural Earth (public domain). Basemap credits are in the map's corner.
