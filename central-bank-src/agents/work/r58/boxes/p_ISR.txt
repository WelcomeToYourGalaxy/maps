BANK OF ISRAEL

Israel's money is the shekel and its central bank is the Bank of Israel. It scores 26 of 100, where 100 means fully exposed to outside influence, and no government pressure was reported between 2010 and 2018. Ministerial pressure came later. Through 2025 finance minister Bezalel Smotrich publicly pressed governor Amir Yaron to cut interest rates, calling the decision to hold the rate at 4 percent wrong and asking him to reverse it, and on 4 September 2025 telling business leaders that if the governor would not lower rates he would lower taxes instead. The bank answered that the governor and the Monetary Committee decide on professional grounds alone; Smotrich said in the same remarks that the bank's independence was critical and that he had no intention of interfering. On 29 April 2002 State Comptroller Eliezer Goldberg reported that former governor Jacob Frenkel unlawfully received hundreds of thousands of shekels when he resigned, approved by a senior official who reported to him; Goldberg did not say whether the money should be demanded back. On 3 February 2009 Globes reported that a draft comptroller report alleged flaws in the bank's supervision of Bank Hapoalim's sub-prime investments, on which Hapoalim wrote off 1.5 billion dollars; the bank said it had responses to all the points, and this is unproven. A comptroller report on 17 October 2012 found supervision of the bank's reserves dealing deficient as those reserves grew from 28 to 75 billion dollars; the bank said some issues had been corrected during the audit. Frenkel, governor from 1991 to 2000, came from the International Monetary Fund and later chaired Merrill Lynch International; he sits in the Group of Thirty alongside private-sector members such as Jane Fraser of Citi and Philipp Hildebrand of BlackRock. Stanley Fischer, governor from 2005 to 2013, came from the World Bank, the IMF and Citigroup. The bank is also one of the 63 central banks that own the Bank for International Settlements.

3 documented cases at the central bank:

2009 · rescues or hidden losses · alleged. Globes reported on 3 February 2009, citing sources, that a draft report by State Comptroller Micha Lindenstrauss found alleged flaws in how the bank's supervision department handled sub-prime investments at Bank Hapoalim, which had written off $1.5 billion on them, and that the bank had only partial information about Israeli banks' exposure to risky instruments. This was a draft: the bank was preparing its reply and said it had responses to all the points. Regulator faulted on Hapoalim sub-prime investments (Globes)
2008-2012 · state audit finding: weak controls over reserves dealing and conflict-of-interest handling · official finding. A State Comptroller's report, reported on 17 October 2012, found faults in how the Bank of Israel managed foreign currency reserves that had grown from USD 28 billion to USD 75 billion since March 2008. It said supervision and control of the Market Operations Division were deficient, there was no control over the receipt of bids, handling of possible employee conflicts of interest was incomplete, and the two committees chaired by the Governor worked without written procedures. The Bank said some issues were corrected during the audit and others were being addressed. State Comptroller report on Bank of Israel foreign currency reserves management (Globes)
2002 · conflict of interest · official finding. State Comptroller Eliezer Goldberg's report, published 29 April 2002, said former governor Jacob Frenkel unlawfully received hundreds of thousands of shekels when he resigned. It said he demanded and received refunds and payments his predecessors did not get, approved by a senior bank official who reported to him. Goldberg did not say whether the state should demand the money back. Comptroller: Frenkel received unlawful compensation from Bank of Israel (Globes)

PRESSURE IN PRACTICE

Government pressure reported in 0 of 37 quarters, 2010–2018.

2025-09-04 · public pressure. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich ran a sustained public campaign against Bank of Israel governor Amir Yaron over the level of interest rates. Speaking to business leaders on 4 September 2025, Smotrich said Yaron "should have lowered interest rates six months ago" and told them: "If the governor doesn't lower interest rates, I will lower taxes" - that is, he would offset the bank's monetary policy with fiscal policy of his own. The same campaign ran through earlier decisions: after the committee held the rate at 4 percent in February 2025 he called the decision wrong, said no inflationary risk justified such restrictive policy and publicly called on the governor to reverse it; after the January 2025 cut he said it should have come six months earlier and should have been deeper. The Bank of Israel answered that the governor and the Monetary Committee decide on professional grounds alone, that high inflation hurts weaker households most, and that keeping it in check is essential. Smotrich also said in the same remarks that the independence of Yaron and of the bank was critical, that he had no intention of interfering, and that monetary policy was the governor's responsibility while fiscal policy was his - so this is open ministerial pressure on rate decisions, not an attempt to change the bank's powers. Israeli Finance Minister says he will cut taxes if the central bank does not lower rates (Reuters)
