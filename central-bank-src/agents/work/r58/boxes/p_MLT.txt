CENTRAL BANK OF MALTA
Malta
Malta uses the euro, so its interest rates are set by the European Central Bank, in which the Central Bank of Malta takes part. Its books have been checked from outside by two firms: KPMG under an approved term covering the financial years 2019 to 2025, and Deloitte Audit Ltd under a term covering 2023 to 2027. Its own case concerns governor Scicluna, a former finance minister: on 29 May 2024 he pleaded not guilty to fraud over the government's handling of a private hospital contract, the Vitals concession, while he was minister. He stepped aside in July 2024 and was reinstated in July 2025 after the European Central Bank was consulted and raised no objections; the charges remain unproven. Two findings sit at the European Central Bank: the European Union's complaints office found maladministration over president Mario Draghi's membership of the Group of Thirty, a club of central bankers and private bankers, and recommended in January 2018 that he suspend it, which the bank declined and he did not; and a speech of 18 May 2015 to a private London event was published only the next day. Malta's own share of the European Central Bank's US dollar pooled reserves is not run from Malta: the Central Bank of Ireland has managed it since 1 January 2008, at Malta's own request. Each euro-area country transferred foreign reserves to the ECB in proportion to its shareholding, and the ECB's page says those reserves are managed in a decentralised way by the national central banks, which act for the ECB on a disclosed agency basis; Malta is the one that handed the job to another country's central bank. The combined value of Ireland's and Malta's share of the ECB's US dollar pooled reserves is USD 1.5 billion, and Ireland's own portion of the ECB reserves amounted to EUR 0.4 billion. Malta scores 26 of 100, where 100 means fully exposed.
