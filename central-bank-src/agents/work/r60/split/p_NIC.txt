### CURRENT PARAGRAPH (being replaced)
Nicaragua's money is issued by the Central Bank of Nicaragua, which scores 31 of 100 on exposure to outside influence, where 100 means fully exposed. One case runs from 2000 to 2008 and ended in an official finding. In 2007 the country's Comptroller General, the state auditor, concluded that the bank's board sold CENI certificates, paper issued to cover the collapse of banks, for less than they were worth and then raised interest rates to the benefit of the main buyer, Bancentro; the fraud is put at 500 million dollars. The Comptroller alleged criminal involvement by six men, among them former finance minister Eduardo Montealegre, who denied wrongdoing and called it a political witch hunt, and in July 2008 a special prosecutor filed charges against 39 people including him. No central bank officials are named among the accused and no court ruling is given, though former bank president Francisco Mayorga had already been found guilty in the banking scandal. The man who now runs the bank came to it from the government: Ovidio Reyes was Chief Economist at the Ministry of Finance from 2004 to 2012, became the bank's General Manager in 2012-13 and its President in January 2014, a move between two public bodies, with no private employer named. At the end of 2024 the statute governing the bank was rewritten in a week. The presidency sent the National Assembly the Ley de Administracion del Sistema Monetario y Financiero on 19 December 2024; it went to committee on 23 December during the legislative recess, was approved on 26 December and published in La Gaceta on 30 December. It repealed the bank's own organic law and the law governing the Superintendency of Banks and Other Financial Institutions, and put the two institutions, until then independent of each other, under one statute and one common board chaired by Reyes, abolishing the Superintendency's own board; Reyes was also given coordination of the Financial Analysis Unit and the deposit fund. The law's own text says the institutions keep organic, functional, administrative, financial and budgetary autonomy and their institutional specialisation. Divergentes read it the other way, writing that the law eliminates the autonomy of the monetary and financial institutions and gives plenipotentiary powers to Reyes and to the Executive, and that removing the cross-checks between supervisor and central bank removes a safeguard against political and economic bias. Article 24 obliges every person resident or domiciled in Nicaragua, national or foreign, to give the bank and the Superintendency whatever economic, financial, statistical and regulatory information they ask for. The bank did not object publicly and its president gained authority rather than losing it; what changed was that the law governing the bank was rewritten by the Executive, without consultation. Baker Tilly Nicaragua, S.A. audited the accounts for 2024, and on 27 March 2024 the printer Orell Fussli was reported to have been partly awarded a tender to print and supply the bank's banknotes, with no value or quantity given.

2024-12-26 · law change. On 19 December 2024 the presidency sent the National Assembly a bill, the Ley de Administracion del Sistema Monetario y Financiero, which was sent to committee on 23 December during the legislative recess, approved on 26 December and published in La Gaceta on 30 December. It repealed the Organic Law of the Central Bank of Nicaragua and the law governing the Superintendency of Banks and Other Financial Institutions, and brought the two institutions, until then independent of one another, under a single statute and a single common board chaired by the president of the central bank, Ovidio Reyes; the Superintendency's own board was abolished. Reyes was also given coordination of the Financial Analysis Unit and the deposit fund, having already been given authority over the Superintendency. The law's own text states that the institutions making up the administration of the monetary and financial system enjoy organic, functional, administrative, financial and budgetary autonomy, and that each keeps its institutional specialisation and functional independence. Independent Nicaraguan outlets read it the other way: Divergentes wrote that while the law formalises administration, it eliminates the autonomy of the monetary and financial institutions and gives plenipotentiary powers to Reyes and to the Executive, and that removing the cross-checks between supervisor and central bank removes a safeguard against political and economic bias. Article 24 obliges every natural or legal person resident or domiciled in Nicaragua, national or foreign, to supply the central bank and the Superintendency with the economic, financial, statistical and regulatory information they ask for, which opposition outlets said would end banking secrecy. The central bank did not object publicly, and its president gained authority rather than losing it; what changed was that the statute governing the bank was rewritten by the Executive, in a week, without consultation.
