What this map shows

Where children have been sexually abused, exploited, sold or trafficked by organised groups and inside institutions, who protected the people responsible, and the patterns that repeat across countries and decades. The map is about those patterns, not individual crimes: cases of a single offender acting alone, with no network, no institution protecting him, no officials involved, no trade across borders and no wider finding, have been left out. So have removals of children by governments, neglect in care homes and child labour where the records show no sexual abuse, exploitation or sale of children. It holds {N_CASES} documented cases in {N_COUNTRIES} countries, {N_NETS} networks and institutions, {N_FLOWS} documented movements of children, offenders and money, and {N_PATS} patterns behind the scenes.

Nobody keeps a world register of child abuse networks, and most of what exists is found only when someone looks: a police raid, a court, a grand jury, a royal commission, a journalist. The map keeps two kinds of evidence apart.

  • Documented cases. Each dot is a case, inquiry finding, prosecution or official report connected to a wider pattern, with the forum that established it and a link to the source.
  • Measured indicators. Country figures published by others: the size of the trafficking trade, modern slavery, reports of online abuse, and how much stands in the way. These color the countries, including the many where little has been documented.

Coloring the countries

Likely scale and impunity (the default) is 0 to 100. Sixty percent is the likely size of the trade and forty percent is how little stands in the way. The cases on the map are not part of the score, because the number of cases found in a country depends mostly on whether anyone has investigated there. Darker means more.

  • Likely size of the trade. The average of what is available: GI-TOC's human trafficking market score (2025, out of 10), Walk Free's estimated people in modern slavery per 1,000 (2023), and NCMEC CyberTipline reports per 100,000 people ({NCMEC_YEAR}), the last two on a log scale.
  • How little stands in the way. The average of GI-TOC's score for officials working with criminal groups (state-embedded actors), its resilience score and its score for help to victims and witnesses (both inverted), and the US State Department's 2025 trafficking ranking (Tier 1 = 0.1, Tier 2 = 0.4, Watch List = 0.65, Tier 3 = 0.9, special case = 0.85).

Territories with no indicator of their own borrow their sovereign's figures (Greenland from Denmark, Puerto Rico from the United States, and so on), and their box says so. No record is not the same as no abuse; see the pattern "Where nobody is counting".

The other layers show each measure on its own: cases by setting, cases where officials took part or covered up, the years from the first complaint to the first action, and each indicator.

The cases

Every case names the forum that established it. The status words mean:

  • Convicted: a court convicted at least one person.
  • Official inquiry finding: a royal commission, statutory inquiry, grand jury, parliamentary or government review found it happened.
  • Admitted: the institution or state admitted it, apologised or paid redress.
  • Settled: compensation paid in a settlement, usually without admission.
  • Charged: charges brought; no verdict yet or not reported here.
  • Investigated by the press: documented by a major news investigation or NGO report without an official finding.
  • Alleged, Acquitted, Disproved: shown as grey rings. Disproved claims are kept because false stories change how real ones are heard.

Cases are grouped in eight settings: churches and religious movements; state care, homes and youth groups; the powerful (politics, wealth, fame, sport); organised crime and trafficking rings; travelling offenders; online networks and platforms; war, armies, peacekeepers and spies; and stolen children and adoption trafficking. A filled dot is placed at the town or site, and cases at the same spot are set side by side on a small ring around it; a ring at a country's center means the source named no town. Dot size grows with the number of children where a source gives one.

"First complaint on record" is the earliest year a source says a complaint reached police, an employer, a church, a school or officials. It does not mean anyone acted on it.

No victim or survivor is named. People are named as offenders only when convicted, charged, or found responsible by an official inquiry; anyone else is described as accused, with who accused them, and acquittals are stated. Nothing on this map points to where abuse material can be found: no site addresses, no access methods, no search terms.

Lines and networks

All line types are shown when the map opens; each can be turned off in the key, or all at once with the switch beside "Lines". Trafficking routes, travelling offenders, abusers moved by their institution, children sent abroad for adoption, children removed by states, payments for abuse, and where abuse material is hosted. Every line links its source in the box of the countries at either end. Lines follow the years chosen.

Networks and institutions are shown when the map opens, placed at their headquarters and sized by how many countries they are documented in. Clicking one draws dotted lines to the countries where it is documented, and grey dotted lines to countries where its presence is only likely. Presence is not proof of abuse in that country.

Patterns behind the scenes

Each pattern layer colors the countries by their role in a structure that repeats across cases, draws its documented connections, and gives a plain summary, the figures behind it, a dated timeline, the sources, what remains unproven, and a short reading of what it means. The patterns were researched for this map from inquiry reports, court records, lobbying and bankruptcy filings, UN reports, adoption statistics and the cases themselves:

  • Moved instead of stopped: institutions relocating abusers, across borders.
  • Years of waiting: the delay between the first complaint and the first action.
  • Deadlines, lobbying and bankruptcy: limitation periods, the spending against reform, and bankruptcy filings.
  • Waves of reckoning, and the countries that never looked.
  • States that shielded their own: immunity, extradition refusals, military jurisdiction, pardons.
  • The adoption pipeline that keeps moving, from one sending country to the next.
  • Children taken by governments, then sexually abused or given false identities.
  • Travelling offenders: crack down here, it moves there, and then online.
  • Peacekeepers and aid workers in crisis zones.
  • War and the taking of children.
  • When spies, soldiers and police are named: proven, disproved and still only claimed.
  • Platforms, servers and the money behind them.
  • Officials who took part or looked away.
  • Where nobody is counting.
  • False alarms that drown real cases.

Limits

The cases on the map measure exposure as much as abuse. Countries that held inquiries (Ireland, Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom) show more because they looked. Many countries in Africa, South and Central Asia and the Middle East have few cases here because few official findings exist in sources that can be reached, not because children there are safer. The indicators are estimates by their publishers, and none measures child sexual abuse alone. Figures can differ between sources; each record gives the one its source states.

If you or a child you know is in danger, contact local emergency services. In the United States, the National Human Trafficking Hotline is 1-888-373-7888 and the NCMEC CyberTipline is report.cybertip.org.
