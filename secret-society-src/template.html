Drug Underworld Map

Where the drug underworld sits inside governments and companies — 193 states scored, and the cases that reached a court
Map rundown−
There is no census of captured institutions. Nobody has counted them, and the reason is structural rather than clerical: UNODC's estimate is that around 0.2% of criminal proceeds laundered through the financial system are seized. What reaches a court is a small and non-random sample of what exists, biased toward the countries with independent prosecutors — which are the countries where capture is shallowest.

So this map does not claim a percentage. It carries layers that can each be checked, and keeps them apart.

The first is a 193-country score built from the Global Organized Crime Index 2025. Its state-embedded actors indicator measures officials, police and security personnel using state power for illicit advantage; 80 of 193 countries score 7.5 or higher, the band the Index calls severe. That indicator covers all organised crime, so on its own it is not a drug measure. It is combined here with all four drug-market indicators as a geometric mean, because capture by the drug underworld requires both a penetrable state and a drug economy large enough to be worth buying it. A country with severe state capture and no drug trade scores low, and so does a large drug economy inside a state nobody has managed to buy.

The second layer is the documented record: convictions, indictments, designations and regulatory settlements where capture was established or formally charged. It holds 182 public-office records across 84 countries and 240 company records across 68, listed together for each country because both are documented and both are institutions — what differs is whether the institution is an office or a firm. A head of state convicted in New York. A cabinet minister who ran the federal police, convicted of taking cartel money and later ordered to repay his country $2.4 billion. A navy chief taken in a sting. Roughly 30% of one national legislature investigated. A sitting governor charged with letting a cartel deliver his election. A bank pleading guilty to conspiring to launder, with a branch manager paid in gift cards. A mayor who wrote the shipment a letter on municipal notepaper. The second layer's distribution measures where prosecution happens, not where capture does. Where the density is highest, the record is best; where the map is blank, the trade is documented and the names are not.

The third layer is the trade itself: an inventory of 1,179 named criminal organisations, cells and factions worldwide, and the corridors along which product moves between them. Cartels and their regional cells, prison-born organisations that outgrew the prisons, mafias, confraternities, biker clubs, street and port networks, and armed groups that tax a route rather than run it. The corridors connect them — the Balkan route out of Afghanistan, the Atlantic crossing to West Africa, the Pacific littoral north to Mexico, the captagon line from the Levant to the Gulf. This layer answers a different question from the two above. It is not evidence of capture and feeds no score; it shows who is operating where, and along which lines, which is the context the first two layers sit inside.
