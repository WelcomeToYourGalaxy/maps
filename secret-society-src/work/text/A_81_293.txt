Comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-free

Summary
By its resolution 79/241, the General Assembly requested the Secretary-General
to convene a qualified group of experts to prepare a comprehensive study of the
question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its aspects. The group was mandated to
assess the status of existing and potential nuclear-weapon-free zones, as well as
examine options and recommendations towards strengthening existing zones and the
possible establishment of new zones, including in the Middle East.
The present report reflects the outcome of the work of the group and its
deliberation during its three one-week sessions. It was also prepared taking into
account contributions submitted to the report of the Secretary-General, entitled
“Comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its aspects”
(A/79/950), written submissions from Member States and views expressed during the
open-ended consultative meetings with the Chair of the group.
In undertaking this task, the group has been guided by the principles and
guidelines set out by the Disarmament Commission in its 1999 report and has
remained mindful of the diversity of regional contexts, as well as of the mechanisms
embedded in the treaties governing nuclear-weapon-free zones.
The group put forward these reflections, consistent with the mandate established
by the General Assembly, not as governmental representatives and without prejudice
to any positions held by States.
In underlining the continued relevance of nuclear-weapon-free zones as
practical instruments contributing to international peace and security, the group
concluded that the study could serve as a comprehensive, although not exhaustive,
reference document for further consideration of options, recommendations and
appropriate action on issues related to nuclear-weapon-free zones.
The group recommended that the Secretary-General ensure the widest possible
dissemination of the study and that interested stakeholders should be encouraged to
examine and give due consideration to it.

Foreword by the Secretary-General
The qualified group of experts to prepare a new comprehensive study of the
question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its aspects, established pursuant to
General Assembly resolution 79/241, met in New York for three sessions of one week
each in 2025 and 2026. The Chair of the group convened two informal consultative
meetings of two days each, which were open-ended so that all Member States could
engage in interactive discussions and share their views.
As mandated by the General Assembly, the group, during its meetings, prepared
a new comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-free zones, aimed at
assessing the status of existing and potential nuclear-weapon-free zones, as well as
examining options and recommendations towards strengthening existing zones and
the possible establishment of new zones, including in the Middle East. In the
preparation of the study, the group built on the 1999 report of the Disarmament
Commission and its annex I, on principles and guidelines for establishing nuclear-
weapon-free zones.
Throughout the process, the group made use, among others, of my report on a
comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its aspects
(A/79/950), written input received from Member States and oral statements made by
Member States during both meetings of the group and informal consultative meetings
with the Chair of the group.
I welcome that the group was able to adopt a consensual study that presents, in
detail, its in-depth discussions and conclusions, which cover, among others,
developments since the first comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-
free zones, the status of existing and potential new zones and options and
recommendations towards strengthening existing zones and the possible
establishment of new zones, including in the Middle East.
I am also pleased that the appointment of women to the group was 40 per cent,
which, unfortunately, remains below the equitable representation of women and men
required by the General Assembly in its resolution 79/241. Owing to the financial
constraints faced by the United Nations, participation was limited to 15 qualified
experts, rather than the originally intended 25.
I invite States Members of the United Nations and relevant bodies of the
international disarmament machinery to consider and implement the
recommendations made by the group.
I take this opportunity to thank the Chair, Maria Cecilia B. Cavalcante Vieira of
Brazil, and all the experts for their work.

I. Introduction
1. Nearly five decades after the submission of the first comprehensive study of the
question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its aspects (A/10027/Add.1), undertaken
at a time when only one nuclear-weapon-free zone had been established, the General
Assembly, in its resolution 79/241, recognized the need for a new comprehensive
study of this issue in the light of developments in international law related to nuclear-
weapon-free zones and the emergence of new nuclear-weapon-free zones in densely
populated areas.1
A. Mandate as established by the General Assembly
2. The General Assembly, by its resolution 79/241, requested the Secretary-
General to convene a qualified group of up to 25 experts, which was representative,
based on the principle of equitable geographical distribution and gave due regard to
gender balance, to prepare a new comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-
weapon-free zones, aimed at assessing the current status of existing and potential
nuclear-weapon-free zones, as well as examining options and recommendations
towards strengthening existing zones and the possible establishment of new zones,
including in the Middle East.
3. The General Assembly requested the Secretary-General to take the
administrative measures necessary for the establishment of the qualified group of
experts during the eightieth session of the General Assembly, including through a
wide call for nominations from Member States, to work in New York for a period of
three weeks divided into three meetings, with the possibility of flexible arrangements,
as well as two 2-day informal consultative meetings, open-ended, so that all Member
States could engage in interactive discussions and share their views with the Chair of
the group.
4. The General Assembly also requested the Secretary-General to inform Member
States that would not have experts in the group that, upon prior notification, they
would have the rights of access to the meetings, of addressing the group and of
submitting to it written material.
5. The General Assembly further requested the Secretary-General to seek the views
of Member States, existing nuclear-weapon-free zones, regional organizations,
organizations of the United Nations system with a special competence in disarmament
and non-proliferation, disarmament and peace-related institutes, and relevant

II. Developments since the first comprehensive study of
the question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in all its
aspects, including multilateral approaches to
nuclear-weapon-free zones
A. Conceptual approaches to nuclear-weapon-free zones at the
multilateral level
15. The first comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-weapon-free zones in
all its aspects (1975) (see General Assembly resolution 3472 (XXX)) attempted to
formulate a concept of nuclear-weapon-free zones, identify the principal issues
involved in such zones and analyse their implications both for zonal and extra-zonal
States. It concluded with calls for a pragmatic and flexible approach in the
establishment of the zones. Since then, the emergence of additional
nuclear‑weapon‑free zones reflected wider developments in disarmament,
non-proliferation and arms control efforts, particularly within the framework of the
Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons. States have also undertaken
initiatives seeking to reinforce the status of these zones through enhanced cooperation
and the creation of supporting institutional mechanisms. This evolution has been
shaped closely by regional security considerations, political dynamics and
consciousness of the increasing regional and global nuclear risks and threats, as well
as devastating consequences that would arise from nuclear war. Each zone established
carries distinct characteristics, reflecting the specific needs, priorities and negotiated
arrangements at the time of its creation. At the same time, all existing nuclear-
weapon-free zones share the common objective of reinforcing international peace and
security by strengthening nuclear non-proliferation, advancing nuclear disarmament,
reducing nuclear risks and promoting confidence-building through legally binding
regional commitments.
16. On 11 December 1975, the General Assembly, by its resolution 3472 B (XXX),
adopted a declaration on the definition of the concept of a nuclear-weapon-free zone,
following its examination of the comprehensive study conducted pursuant to
resolution 3261 F (XXIX). The declaration includes a definition of the concept of a
nuclear-weapon-free zone, the principal obligations of nuclear-weapon States towards
such zones and the States included therein, and the scope of the relevant definitions.
17. On 30 June 1978, the first special session of the General Assembly devoted to
disarmament, in its final document (see resolution S-10/2), declared that the
establishment of nuclear-weapon-free zones on the basis of arrangements freely
arrived at among the States of the region concerned constituted an important
disarmament measure. It was also underlined in the final document that the process
of establishing such zones in various parts of the world should be encouraged with
the ultimate objective of achieving a world entirely free of nuclear weapons.

18. On 30 April 1999, the Disarmament Commission adopted its report A/54/42,
including annex I (establishment of nuclear-weapon-free zones on the basis of
arrangements freely arrived at among the States of the region concerned), which set
out non-exhaustive principles and guidelines based on current practices and available
experiences intended to assist in the establishment of additional zones.
19. On a recurrent basis, the General Assembly considers the question of nuclear-
weapon-free zones through the adoption of resolutions2 by which the Assembly
reaffirms existing frameworks and encourages their full and effective
implementation, as well as resolutions and decisions on the establishment of new
nuclear-weapon-free zones, including in the Middle East.
20. Pending the complete elimination of nuclear weapons, non-nuclear-weapon
States Parties to nuclear-weapon-free zones have a legitimate interest in legally
binding negative security assurances by nuclear-weapon States not to use or threaten
to use nuclear weapons. Nuclear-weapon States have sought to provide security
assurances against the use of nuclear weapons to non-nuclear-weapon States through
the signature and ratification of protocols to nuclear-weapon-free zone treaties
(A/79/950, para. 4), underscoring the critical role that zones play in fostering trust
and transparency between nuclear-weapon States and non-nuclear-weapon States. In
this context, discussions on negative security assurances, including those that have
taken place in the Conference on Disarmament and other relevant disarmament
forums, remain relevant to efforts to strengthen existing nuclear-weapon-free zones
and to advance broader international efforts towards more effective negative security
assurances for non-nuclear-weapon States.
B. Establishment and expansion of nuclear‑weapon‑free zones
21. As of the publication of the study, the five existing regional nuclear-weapon-
free zones collectively cover the territories of 113 Member States. In addition,
Mongolia has internationally recognized nuclear-weapon-free status.
22. The Treaty for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the
Caribbean (Treaty of Tlatelolco), establishing a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Latin
America and the Caribbean, was the first and only instrument establishing a nuclear-
weapon-free zone in a densely populated area at the time of the first study. In the
aftermath of the 1962 Cuban missile crisis and after four years of negotiations, the
Treaty opened for signature on 14 February 1967.3 Since the publication of the first
study, four other zones have been established through regional treaties, and Mongolia
declared itself a single-State nuclear-weapon-free zone.
23. The South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone Treaty (Treaty of Rarotonga) opened for
signature on 6 August 1985 and entered into force on 11 December 1986. Nuclear
testing in the Pacific and regional States’ desire to prevent further contamination
triggered the emergence of the zone.4 The treaty covers a vast area, extending from
the west coast of Australia to the boundary of the zone in the Tlatelolco area in the

24. In September 1992, Mongolia declared its territory a nuclear-weapon-free zone
at the United Nations and proposed to have this status internationally guaranteed.
That initiative followed the decrease in cold war tensions and Mongolia’s own
experience of bordering two nuclear-weapon States.6 The General Assembly adopted
resolution 53/77 D, entitled “Mongolia’s international security and nuclear-weapon-

Pending the five nuclear-weapon States’ acceptance of the concept of a single-State
nuclear-weapon-free zone, it was decided that Mongolia would be considered a State
with nuclear-weapon-free status. In October 2000, the five nuclear-weapon States
issued a joint statement (A/55/530-S/2000/1052, annex) and, in September 2012, a
joint declaration (A/67/393-S/2012/721, annex), providing political security
assurances to Mongolia.
25. The Treaty on the South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone (Treaty of
Bangkok) was signed on 15 December 1995 and entered into force on 27 March 1997.
The Treaty is a key legal instrument in supporting the purposes of the Association of
Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), which includes preserving South-East Asia as a
region free from nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction.
26. The Pelindaba Treaty establishing the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone was
opened for signature on 12 April 1996 and entered into force on 15 July 2009. While
opposition to nuclear testing in Africa helped to catalyse the idea, the Treaty is also
rooted in a wider regional commitment of ensuring that the continent remains free of
nuclear weapons, reinforced by South Africa’s historic decision to dismantle its
nuclear arsenal.
27. The idea of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia emerged as a collective
endeavour in February 1997 at the summit of the heads of all five States of the region
in Almaty, Kazakhstan (see A/52/112). The Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone
in Central Asia (Semipalatinsk Treaty) was signed on 8 September 2006 and ratified
by all five Central Asian States (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan
and Uzbekistan), with an entry into force on 21 March 2009. It is the only zone
situated entirely in the northern hemisphere and in a landlocked region bordering two
nuclear-weapon States directly.

D. Possible establishment of new zones, including in the Middle East
33. In considering the establishment of new zones, the Middle East has featured
prominently in deliberations and resolutions. The proposal to establish a nuclear-
weapon-free zone in the region has been expressed since the 1970s. As a region where
a universally recognized nuclear-weapon-free zone has yet to be established, the
prospective zone is viewed as a central confidence-building measure for regional
security and a key component of the broader Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons architecture.
34. The first formal United Nations consideration of a Middle East nuclear-weapon-
free zone dates to General Assembly resolution 3263 (XXIX). In that resolution, the

in the region of the Middle East, it was indispensable that all parties concerned in the
area proclaim solemnly and immediately their intention to refrain, on a reciprocal
basis, from producing, testing, obtaining, acquiring or in any other way possessing
nuclear weapons.
35. Since 1974, on a recurrent basis, the General Assembly has addressed the
question of a Middle East zone through resolutions concerning the establishment of a
nuclear-weapon-free zone in that region, recalling its earlier resolutions and
reaffirming its support for regional steps towards the realization of such a zone.

E. Nuclear‑weapon‑free zones, multilateral nuclear disarmament and
non-proliferation7
37. Since the publication of the first comprehensive study of the question of nuclear-
weapon-free zones in all its aspects, additional States possessing nuclear weapons
have emerged outside of the now nearly universal Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons.
38. While the Treaty of Tlatelolco constituted the first practical and legally binding
example of a nuclear-weapon-free zone, adopted shortly before the conclusion of the
Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, the concept of nuclear-weapon-
free zones subsequently received explicit multilateral treaty recognition in the Treaty.
Article VII of the Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons affirms the
right of any group of States to conclude regional treaties in order to assure the total
absence of nuclear weapons in their territories, a principle that has been reaffirmed in
the outcome documents of the Review Conferences.8
39. At the 1995 Review and Extension Conference, States Parties adopted a package
of decisions, most notably decision 2, by which they encouraged the establishment of
zones free of all weapons of mass destruction, and decision 3, by which the Treaty on
the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons was extended indefinitely. States Parties
also adopted a resolution on the Middle East, as an integral part of the package
providing the basis for the indefinite extension of the Treaty, urging concrete steps
towards the establishment of a Middle East zone free of weapons of mass destruction
and calling upon States Parties to the Treaty to support regional efforts towards its
early creation (NPT/CONF.1995/32 (Part I), p. 13).
40. Further to Treaty on the Non‑Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons developments
and after negotiations at the Conference on Disarmament, the Comprehensive
Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty opened for signature in 1996. The provisions of that Treaty
reinforce zones by strengthening verification and monitoring the test ban worldwide.
Regional treaty provisions prohibiting nuclear explosive testing complement and
reinforce the international norm against nuclear testing and the objectives of the
Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty.
41. In the preamble to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons,9 which
entered into force in January 2021, there is a reaffirmation of the conviction that the
establishment of the internationally recognized nuclear-weapon-free zones on the basis
of arrangements freely arrived at among the States of the region concerned enhances
global and regional peace and security, strengthens the nuclear non-proliferation regime
and contributes to realizing the objective of nuclear disarmament.

42. While the overall estimated number of nuclear weapons has decreased since the
first study and the cold war peak, previous achievements now appear increasingly
remote against a backdrop of profound transformation in the international security
landscape, marked by growing strategic uncertainty and emerging risks, as global
norms and institutions underpinning the disarmament and non‑proliferation
architecture come under strain. In this challenging environment of heightened risks
and threats, nuclear-weapon-free zones contribute significantly to preserving peace
and security. In particular, and while not being a substitute for the total elimination
of nuclear weapons, the zones help to mitigate the nuclear risks that could arise from
escalating crises or miscalculations, to the States Parties to the relevant nuclear-
weapon-free zone treaties. They foster dialogue, promote information exchange and
confidence-building and support the development of a balanced, effective and
sustainable regional security architecture.

international support for that policy demonstrates that the objective of nuclear-
weapon-free zones could be achieved by individual non-nuclear-weapon States that,
owing to their geographical location or credible political or legal reasons, cannot be
part of zones.
44. Taken together, these developments demonstrate that nuclear-weapon-free
zones have evolved into an integral component of the international disarmament and
non-proliferation architecture. Through legally binding regional commitments,
implementation of safeguards, confidence-building measures and institutional
cooperation, they complement and reinforce broader multilateral instruments while
responding to the specific security circumstances of the regions concerned.

A. Treaty for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America
and the Caribbean (Treaty of Tlatelolco)
1. Treaty and the establishment of the zone
45. The Treaty for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the
Caribbean, known as the Treaty of Tlatelolco for the borough in Mexico City where
the old Secretariat for Foreign Affairs of Mexico was located, is a legally binding
instrument resulting from negotiations conducted between 1964 and 1967 among 21
Latin American and Caribbean States.
46. Although the Treaty negotiation process was open and inclusive, not all States
in the region took part from the beginning. Additionally, some regional States attained
independence only after the Treaty had been opened for signature. The States Parties
created an institutional link through the Agency for the Prohibition of Nuclear
Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean,10 established under the Treaty as an
organization to oversee its implementation, which also provided a mechanism for
continued consultations and facilitated the eventual accession to the Treaty of States
in the region that were yet to do so.11

47. The Treaty of Tlatelolco was opened for signature on 14 February 1967. Mexico
is its depository State and Mexico City hosts the headquarters of the Agency for the
Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean. The Treaty
entered into force for each State Party on a different date, given its provisions for
provisional application, and achieved universality in 2002,12 35 years after it had been
opened for signature.
48. The Treaty of Tlatelolco marked a pioneering step as the first ever legal
instrument to establish a nuclear-weapon-free zone in a densely populated area,
prohibiting nuclear weapons within a defined geographical zone.13 Additionally, the
Treaty contains one of the few explicit definitions of nuclear weapons in international
law.14 It also establishes, under the responsibility of the Agency for the Prohibition of
Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean, a comprehensive control
system to verify compliance with the obligations assumed by States Parties.
Currently, all 33 members of the Latin American and Caribbean region are Parties to
the Treaty and members of the Agency.
2. Extent and composition of the zone
49. The Treaty distinguishes the concepts of “territory” and “zone of application”.
The territory is “the territorial sea, air space and any other space over which the State
exercises sovereignty in accordance with its own legislation” (art. 3). In this regard,
the 33 States Parties to the Treaty implement their obligations to ensure the realization
of the prohibition of nuclear weapons within their respective territories.
50. In contrast, the zone of application of the Treaty “is the whole of the territories”
for which it is in force and, upon fulfilment of the requirements set out in article 28,
paragraph 1, the zone shall also include high seas adjacent to the territories of the
region (art. 4). This was determined through precise geographical coordinates
defining a zone of application larger than the sum of the territories of the States
Parties. The scope of the zone of application does not constitute a declaration or claim
of sovereignty or jurisdiction over the areas of the high seas that lie within said zone.15
The inclusion of both concepts, “territory” and “zone of application”, enabled
regional and extra-regional States to address common concerns at the time of
negotiations, relating to the “transit” of nuclear weapons and the lack of international
consensus on the breadth of the “territorial sea”.16
51. In 2002, the zone of application provided for in article 4 became fully effective.
The Latin American and Caribbean nuclear-weapon-free zone is regionally adjacent
to the zone established by the Antarctic Treaty and to the South Pacific Nuclear Free
Zone. Additionally, its eastern limit reaches vast spaces of the Atlantic Ocean. The
zone of application of the Treaty of Tlatelolco covers around 80 million square
kilometres.17

3. Obligations and commitments by zonal States
52. Article 1 of the Treaty of Tlatelolco sets out both positive and negative
obligations, in the form of the following fundamental elements: (a) it affirms that
nuclear energy shall be used exclusively for peaceful purposes; and correspondingly:
(b) prohibits States Parties from engaging in any activity associated with nuclear
weapons, namely their testing, use, manufacture, production or acquisition;
(c) extends these prohibitions to identical activities undertaken in relation to nuclear
weapons pertaining to third States, including their receipt, storage, installation,
deployment or possession; (d) makes clear that such restrictions apply both within the
territories of the States Parties and “in any way” in the territories of third States; and
(e) clarifies that these prohibitions cover actions undertaken directly or indirectly,
whether by the States Parties themselves or on behalf of any other entity, thereby
underscoring the comprehensive nature of the obligations assumed.
53. Article 1 is concise, with explicit prohibitions to ensure no nuclear weapons are
placed or developed in the zone of application of the Treaty, and it avoids ambiguities,
as it balances the commitment to use nuclear energy exclusively for peaceful purposes
with the prohibition of nuclear weapons, with a view to contributing to nuclear
disarmament. It must be recalled that these obligations and prohibitions predate the
Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons.
4. Obligations and commitments by extra-zonal States
54. Some extra-zonal States are legally bound to the Treaty of Tlatelolco by means
of both Additional Protocol I and Additional Protocol II annexed to the instrument.
55. Additional Protocol I to the Treaty of Tlatelolco, to which France, Netherlands
(Kingdom of the), the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland and the
United States of America are Parties, applies to territories within the Treaty’s
geographical zone for which these extra‑zonal States bear de jure or de facto
international responsibility, requiring them to enforce in those territories the
prohibitions set out in article 1 of the Treaty, in accordance with articles 3 (Definition
of territory), 5 (Definition of nuclear weapons) and 13 (International Atomic Energy
Agency safeguards).
56. Additionally, the General Assembly has supported the denuclearization of Latin
America and the Caribbean. It has welcomed the activities under the Treaty of
Tlatelolco, for more than six decades, in a constant manner, from the inception of the
initiative in 1963, with resolution 1911 (XVIII), and resolution 2286 (XXII) of 1967,
up to resolution 80/15, adopted in 2025.

7. Dispute settlement resolution mechanism and enforcement
62. Article 24 of the Treaty of Tlatelolco establishes that, unless the Parties
concerned “agree on another mode of peaceful settlement”, any question or dispute
concerning the interpretation of the instrument shall be referred to the International
Court of Justice with the prior consent of the Parties to the controversy. To date, this
provision has not been invoked.
63. The Agency for the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the
Caribbean also contributed to the peaceful settlement of disputes, for example,
through the establishment and work of a commission of good offices, which helped
to facilitate accession to the Treaty by States with territorial disputes.
8. Institutional arrangements
64. The Agency was established to ensure compliance with the obligations of the
Treaty, and it is responsible for holding periodic and extraordinary consultations
among member States on matters related to the provisions outlined in the Treaty,
including the implementation of the control system. Additionally, the Agency
articulates the political positions of its member States in international forums on
disarmament and the non-proliferation of nuclear weapons. The Agency has the
following main organs:
(a) General Conference: constituted by all the States Parties to the Treaty. It
holds regular sessions every two years and may also hold extraordinary sessions
whenever necessary. The General Conference may consider and decide on procedures
for the control system to ensure observance of the Treaty; it elects the members of the
Council and the General Secretary of the Agency; it receives and considers reports by
the Council and the General Secretary; it may request and consider studies designed
to facilitate the fulfilment of the aims of the Treaty; it is the competent organ to
authorize the conclusion of agreements between the Agency and other Governments
and international organizations; it adopts the Agency’s budget and fixes the scale of
financial contributions to be paid by member States. Each State member of the
Agency has one vote at the General Conference, and the decisions of the General
Conference are taken by a two-thirds majority of the members present and voting.
The General Conference has held at least one meeting per year since 1969;
(b) Council: formed by five State Parties to the Treaty of Tlatelolco elected
by the General Conference for a four-year term. The Council holds sessions
continuously, at least every two months. The main function of the Council is to
“ensure the proper operation of the control system”; in this regard, the Council
assesses and considers the reports submitted by member States under article 14. The
Council has held more than 320 meetings since 1969;
(c) Secretariat: led by a General Secretary, who is the chief administrative
officer of the Agency, supported by the necessary staff and serving a four-year term,
renewable once. The General Secretary may not be a national of the country in which
the Agency has its headquarters (Mexico). The mandate of the General Secretary is
to ensure the proper operation of the control system, and for that purpose, the General
Secretary presents annual reports to the General Conference and the Council. The
secretariat organizes and convenes the sessions of both the General Conference and
the Council.
65. The Agency participates in nuclear disarmament and non-proliferation forums
within the United Nations system and in other settings and also undertakes many
activities on dissemination of the value of the zones, and nuclear disarmament
education.

10. Lessons learned
69. At the time of its negotiation, the Treaty of Tlatelolco did not include all
members of the region. Nevertheless, the institutional framework established by the
Treaty provided a space for dialogue between the Parties and those countries that
initially expressed concerns about acceding to the instrument. The Agency for the
Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean played a
fundamental role in this regard.
70. As it preceded the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and
other treaties regarding nuclear weapons, the Parties to the Treaty of Tlatelolco
needed to inform other multilateral negotiations to make further legally binding
provisions compatible and consistent with the obligations already acquired through
the Treaty of Tlatelolco.
71. The geographical delimitation of the nuclear-weapon-free zone in Latin
America and the Caribbean provoked extensive discussions among regional States
and the States that later acceded to Additional Protocols I and II to the Treaty. These
discussions concerned the inclusion within the zone of application of the Treaty of
maritime areas beyond the national jurisdiction of regional States and, therefore,
involved considerations related to the law of the sea, as well as questions of
international responsibility concerning territories administered de jure or de facto by
extra-regional States.
72. The control system of the Treaty of Tlatelolco was defined as a concise, simple
but effective set of measures to ensure compliance with Treaty obligations.

B. South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone Treaty (Treaty of Rarotonga)
1.⁠ ⁠Treaty and the establishment of the Zone
75. The South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone Treaty (Treaty of Rarotonga) was opened
for signature on 6 August 1985 and entered into force on 11 December 1986. The
Treaty was borne out of the region’s first-hand experience of nuclear-weapons testing
and its strong opposition to such testing and any dumping of nuclear waste material
in the Pacific.
2. Extent and composition of the Zone
76. Article 12 of the Treaty of Rarotonga provides that the Treaty is open for
signature by any member of the then South Pacific Forum, now the Pacific Islands
Forum. Upon entry into force in 1986, there were eight original Parties to the Treaty.
A further five members of the Pacific Islands Forum have since ratified the Treaty.
On 3 March 2025, the Marshall Islands signed the Treaty of Rarotonga.
77. The geographical scope of the Treaty of Rarotonga is set out in annex 1 thereto.
The Zone extends from the west coast of Australia to the boundary of the nuclear-
weapon-free zone established by the Treaty of Tlatelolco in the east, and from the
equator to 60 degrees south, where it meets the boundary of the denuclearized zone
established by the Antarctic Treaty. The zone is also bordered to the north-west by the
South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone as delimited by the Indonesia and Papua
New Guinea borders. Article 12, paragraph 3, of the Treaty of Rarotonga provides
that if a member of the Pacific Islands Forum whose territory is outside the Zone
becomes a Party to the Treaty, annex 1 is deemed to be amended so far as is required
to enclose at least the territory of that Party within the boundaries of the Zone.
78. The area of the Zone predominantly covers ocean areas, making it unique
compared to other nuclear-weapon-free zones.

3. Obligations and commitments by zonal States
79. Under article 3 (Renunciation of nuclear explosive devices), each Party commits
to a comprehensive renunciation of nuclear explosive devices. This includes
abstaining from the manufacture, acquisition, possession or control of any such
device, by any means anywhere, whether inside or outside the South Pacific Nuclear
Free Zone; refraining from seeking or receiving assistance to manufacture or acquire
such devices; and avoiding any action that would assist or encourage another State to
manufacture or acquire them.
80. The Treaty’s use of the term “nuclear explosive devices” makes clear that the
renunciation of nuclear explosive devices extends to nuclear explosions for peaceful
purposes.
81. Regarding peaceful nuclear activities, article 4 requires each Party to support
the continued effectiveness of the international non-proliferation system based on the
Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and the IAEA safeguards system.
This includes undertaking not to provide nuclear material to any non-nuclear-weapon
State unless the material is subject to the safeguards required by Article III.1 of that
Treaty. Similarly, a Party may not provide nuclear material to a nuclear-weapon State
unless such material is subject to any applicable IAEA safeguards.
82. Article 5 requires each Party to prevent the stationing of any nuclear explosive
device in its territory. “Territory” means each Party’s internal waters, territorial sea
and archipelagic waters, the seabed and subsoil beneath, the land territory and the
airspace above them. Under article 5, each Party retains its sovereign right to decide
whether to allow visits by foreign ships and aircraft to its ports and airfields, transit
of its airspace by foreign aircraft and navigation by foreign ships in its territorial sea
or archipelagic waters in a manner not covered by the rights of innocent passage,
archipelagic sea lanes passage or transit passage of straits afforded under the United
Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea.
83. Article 6 requires each Party to prevent in its territory the testing of any nuclear
explosive device and not to take any action to assist or encourage the testing of any
nuclear explosive device by any State.
84. The Treaty also has an anti-dumping provision in article 7, which, inter alia,
requires each Party: (a) not to dump radioactive wastes and other radioactive matter
at sea within the Zone; (b) to prevent the dumping of radioactive wastes and other
radioactive matter by anyone in its territorial sea; and (c) not to take any action to
assist or encourage the dumping by anyone of radioactive wastes and other
radioactive matter at sea anywhere within the Zone;
85. Article 8, paragraph 2 (c), and annex 2 require each Party to apply IAEA
safeguards as set forth in an agreement negotiated and concluded with IAEA, on all
source or special fissionable material in all peaceful nuclear activities within the
territory of the Party, under its jurisdiction or carried out under its control anywhere.
This agreement with IAEA must be equivalent in its scope and effect to an agreement
required in connection with the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons
on the basis of the material reproduced in document INFCIRC/153 (Corrected) of
IAEA.
86. Article 13 provides that the Treaty shall remain in force indefinitely, subject to
certain conditions.
87. Finally, article 14 provides that the Treaty shall not be subject to reservations.
The Treaty does not extend this prohibition to its Protocols.

88. The Treaty of Rarotonga only places obligations on the Parties to the Treaty and,
via the Treaty’s Protocols, on the five nuclear-weapon States.
5. Negative security assurances
89. The Treaty of Rarotonga has three Protocols:
(a) Protocol 1 is drafted to require France, the United Kingdom and the United
States to apply the prohibitions of the Treaty in respect of the territories situated
within the Zone for which they are internationally responsible;
(b) Protocol 2 requires the five nuclear-weapon States not to use or threaten
to use nuclear explosive devices against any Party to the Treaty or any territory within
the Zone for which a nuclear-weapon State Party to Protocol 1 is internationally
responsible. Protocol 2 also requires each nuclear-weapon State not to contribute to
any act of a Party to the Treaty that constitutes a violation of the Treaty, or to any act
of another Party to a Protocol that constitutes a violation of a Protocol; and
(c) Protocol 3 requires the five nuclear-weapon States not to test nuclear
explosive devices within the Zone.
90. All nuclear-weapon States have signed the Protocols to which they are able to
become a Party. The United States – which signed the Protocols on 25 March 1996 –
has yet to ratify them. On ratification of the Protocols, each of the ratifying nuclear-
weapon States made reservations and interpretative declarations in relation to the
Protocols. The Protocols were ratified on the following dates: the Union of Soviet
Socialist Republics on 21 April 1988, China on 21 October 1988, France on
20 September 1996 and the United Kingdom on 19 September 1997.
6. Verification, compliance and control
91. Article 8 establishes a control system for the purpose of verifying compliance
with the Parties’ obligations under the Treaty. The control system is comprised of
reports and exchanges of information (art. 9), consultations (art. 10, and annex 4,
para. 1), the application of IAEA safeguards to peaceful nuclear activities (annex 2)
and a complaints procedure (annex 4).
92. The decision-making body for the Treaty is the Consultative Committee, which
is established pursuant to annex 3 and convened by the Secretary General of the
Pacific Islands Forum (formerly, the Director of the South Pacific Bureau for
Economic Co-operation). The Consultative Committee may be convened for
consultation and cooperation on any matter arising in relation to the Treaty or for
reviewing its operation. A quorum for the Consultative Committee is constituted by
representatives of half the Parties, and decisions are made by consensus or, failing
consensus, by a two-thirds majority of those present and voting.
93. Most recently, the Consultative Committee has been used by the States Parties
to cooperate on the Pacific Islands Forum Leaders’ commitment to operationalize the
provisions of the Treaty, as necessary.
7. Dispute settlement resolution mechanism and enforcement
94. As outlined above, annex 4 to the Treaty of Rarotonga establishes a complaints
procedure to address alleged breaches of the Treaty. It has never been invoked. The
complaints procedure consists of the following steps:

Party complained of. The latter Party must be allowed a reasonable opportunity to
provide an explanation and to resolve the matter (annex 4, para. 1);
(b) If unresolved by consultations between the Parties, the complainant Party
may request that the Consultative Committee under the Treaty be convened to

(c) The Consultative Committee must first afford the Party complained of a

procedures to be undertaken by IAEA pursuant to agreements referred to in annex 2,
paragraph 1. This reflects the primacy and expertise of IAEA as the global nuclear
safeguards regulator.
8. Institutional arrangements
96. The Pacific Islands Forum is the Pacific region’s principal political grouping.
Established in 1971 as the South Pacific Forum, the Pacific Islands Forum oversees
the Consultative Committee under the Treaty of Rarotonga. The pre-eminent
decision-making body of the Pacific Islands Forum is the Pacific Islands Forum
Leaders’ Meeting.
97. The Pacific Islands Forum secretariat is the depositary and secretariat for the
Treaty. In addition to providing policy advice, coordination and assistance in
implementing the decisions of Pacific Islands Forum Leaders, the secretariat also
works to advance partnerships between the Forum and its stakeholders within and
beyond the Pacific region.

forum for advancing this work through its initiatives in human health, climate change,
environment, water, food security and energy. Members of zones can support IAEA

10. Lessons learned
100. As the second nuclear-weapon-free zone to enter into force, the Treaty of
Rarotonga has a special place in the history of disarmament and non-proliferation as
an arrangement designed by the countries of the Pacific for the Pacific. In successive
statements, Pacific leaders have recognized the significant contribution of the Treaty
and its inspiration for other nuclear-weapon-free zones around the world. The banning
of dumping of radioactive waste in the Zone was a key innovation in the Treaty of
Rarotonga and has been adopted for subsequent nuclear-weapon-free zones.
101. The Treaty of Rarotonga continues to expand its membership, with the Marshall
Islands having signed the Treaty on 3 March 2025. Moreover, through constructive
engagement with nuclear-weapon States, the Treaty has achieved broad recognition
of the Zone by all nuclear-weapon States in the form of negative security assurances.
Leaders continue to call on remaining non-Parties to accede to or ratify the Treaty
and to urge States that have yet to do so to ratify all Protocols to the Treaty.
102. While the Treaty of Rarotonga does not have a dedicated secretariat, its
Consultative Committee meets as required. Through the Consultative Committee,
States Parties continue efforts to operationalize the provisions of the Treaty as
necessary. The use of hybrid meetings has facilitated enhanced participation by States
Parties in a vast and geographically dispersed region.
C. Treaty on the South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone (Treaty
of Bangkok)
1. ⁠Treaty and the establishment of the Zone
103. The Treaty on the South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone (Treaty of
Bangkok) was signed on 15 December 1995 and entered into force on 27 March 1997.
104. The Treaty reaffirms the importance of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons in preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons and in
contributing towards international peace and security, and the need for the regional
treaty in order to assure the total absence of nuclear weapons.
105. The establishment of the Treaty of Bangkok was rooted in regional efforts to
promote peace and stability amidst cold war tensions. A significant milestone was the
adoption of the Declaration of the Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality in 1971.
26-11249 23/74
[[page 24]]
A/81/293
Initiated by the five founding members of ASEAN, the Declaration underscored the
Association’s commitment to maintaining a region free from external interference,
with a particular emphasis on nuclear non-proliferation. Following the end of the cold
war, ASEAN strengthened this commitment through the establishment of the Treaty.
106. The Treaty remains a cornerstone of the ASEAN security architecture and
continues to serve as a key instrument in the region’s contribution to global
disarmament and non-proliferation efforts amidst the growing risks of nuclear danger
and conflict. This is further strengthened by the accession of Timor-Leste to the Treaty
in 2025, bringing the total number of States Parties to the Treaty to 11, and enlarging
the geographical scope of the Zone. Timor-Leste’s accession underscores its
adherence to the long-standing commitment of ASEAN to preserving South-East Asia
as a nuclear-weapon-free zone and to strengthening efforts on nuclear
non-proliferation and disarmament.
2. Extent and composition of the Zone
107. The Treaty of Bangkok applies to the territories, continental shelves and
exclusive economic zones of the States Parties within the Zone (Brunei Darussalam,
Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Malaysia, Myanmar,
Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Timor-Leste and Viet Nam). The term “territory”,
pursuant to article 1 (b) of the Treaty, includes “the land territory, internal waters,
territorial sea, archipelagic waters, the seabed and the sub-soil thereof and the
airspace above them.”
3. Obligations and commitments by zonal States
108. Articles 3, 4, and 5 of the Treaty set out the obligations and commitments to be
undertaken by zonal States, including the obligation for the application of IAEA
comprehensive safeguards.
109. Paragraph 1 of article 3 stipulates that each State Party undertakes not to
develop, manufacture, acquire, possess or control nuclear weapons; station or
transport them; or test or use them anywhere inside or outside the Zone. Paragraph 2
of article 3 prohibits States Parties from allowing these activities to be conducted in
their territories by other States. Paragraph 3 of the article further stipulates acts and
activities that each State Party is not allowed to undertake, namely, dumping at sea or
discharging into the atmosphere any radioactive material or wastes within the Zone;
disposing of such material on land in other States’ territories, except as stipulated in
paragraph 2 (e) of article 4; or allowing other States to do so in its territory. Paragraph
4 of article 3 stipulates that each State Party undertakes not to seek or receive
assistance, or assist or encourage others, in any act in violation of paragraphs 1, 2 and
3 of the article.
110. Article 4 of the Treaty sets out the obligations and commitments of the States
Parties regarding the use of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes. Each State Party
also undertakes to use exclusively for peaceful purposes nuclear material and
facilities which are within its territory and areas under its jurisdiction and control.
While realizing their right to use nuclear material and facilities exclusively for
peaceful purposes, States Parties must ensure nuclear safety in accordance with the
guidelines and standards recommended by IAEA. States Parties are also obliged to
make relevant information available to other States Parties upon request, with the
exception of that related to personal data, protected by intellectual property rights or
commercial confidentiality, or relating to national security. Each State Party
undertakes to support the international non-proliferation systems based on the Treaty
on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and the IAEA safeguards system, and
to dispose of radioactive wastes and other radioactive material in accordance with

[[page 25]]
A/81/293
IAEA standards and procedures on land within its territory or on land within the
territory of another State which has consented to such disposal. Paragraph 3 of article
4 stipulates that the provision of source or special fissionable material, or equipment
or material for the processing, use or production of special fissionable material must
be subject to conditions set out in the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons and IAEA safeguard agreements.
111. Article 5 requires the State Party to conclude and maintain a comprehensive
safeguards agreement with IAEA, to ensure that all nuclear material activities within
its jurisdiction are used exclusively for peaceful purposes.
112. In addition to obligations under the Treaty, ASEAN member States continue to
reaffirm their commitment through their signature and ratification of international
instruments that strengthen the implementation of the Treaty, including the Treaty on
the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons.
4. Obligations and commitments by extra-zonal States
113. The Protocol to the Treaty is an important legal instrument, which is open for
signature by the five nuclear-weapon States recognized under the Treaty on the
Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons. The Protocol sets out the commitments
undertaken by the nuclear-weapon States.
114. The ASEAN member States, which are the States Parties to the Treaty, have
been working with the nuclear-weapon States to sign the Protocol.
5. Negative security assurances
115. The Protocol to the Treaty aims to ensure negative security assurances by the
nuclear-weapon States for the Zone. Under article 2 of the Protocol, each State Party
undertakes not to use or threaten to use nuclear weapons against any State Party to
the Treaty or within the South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone.

(b) Safeguards, including relationship with the International Atomic Energy Agency,

concluded their respective IAEA safeguards agreements, and some have also
concluded additional protocols to those agreements, which reinforce their
implementation of the Treaty. In addition, several member States have signed or
acceded to international nuclear safety, security and liability-related conventions

compliance as States Parties to the Treaty.
118. Noting the role and mandate of IAEA in promoting peaceful uses of nuclear

signed in 2019. This has provided ASEAN with access to capacity-building support

121. Article 11 provides for a framework for reporting and information-sharing

are relevant to the implementation of the Treaty. They may also exchange information
on matters arising under or in relation to the Treaty.
122. Articles 12 and 13 establish mechanisms to address situations that may be

Treaty, thereby supporting transparency, confidence-building and effective
implementation. Under article 12, States Parties may seek clarification regarding such

Article 13 further provides for a fact-finding mechanism, whereby a State Party may
request the Executive Committee to dispatch a fact-finding mission to another State

123. Article 14 sets out the remedial measures to be taken in case there is a breach
of the Treaty. In such cases, the Executive Committee engages with the State Party
concerned to encourage timely corrective actions aimed at restoring full compliance,
while being kept informed of the steps taken or envisaged. The matter may be elevated
to the level of the Commission for the South-East Asia Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone

responses, which may include engagement with IAEA and, where relevant, the
Security Council and the General Assembly of the United Nations. Article 14 also
covers situations involving the Protocol to the Treaty, providing for the convening of
a special meeting of the Commission to consider appropriate measures should
compliance concerns arise.

136. The experience of ASEAN in implementing the Treaty on the South-East Asia

adaptability and cooperation in advancing nuclear disarmament and non-proliferation
objectives in an evolving global security environment.

underscores the value of regular consultation, information exchange and the sharing
of best practices. Such interactions provide useful insights into different approaches
to treaty implementation, engagement with the nuclear-weapon States and
strengthening of confidence-building measures. ASEAN will continue to benefit from
these exchanges with other nuclear-weapon-free zones.

D. African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty (Treaty of Pelindaba)
1. ⁠Treaty and the establishment of the Zone
142. The objective to create a nuclear-weapon-free African continent began when the
Organization of African Unity (OAU; now the African Union) adopted the
Declaration on the Denuclearization of Africa at its first ordinary session, held in
Cairo in July 1964, expressing the continent’s readiness “to undertake in an
International Treaty to be concluded under the auspices of the United Nations not to
manufacture or acquire control of nuclear weapons” and in this regard called upon
“all peace-loving nations to adhere to the same undertaking” and for “all nuclear
powers to respect and abide by this Declaration.”
143. Between 1970 and 1990, the General Assembly kept the issue on its agenda
through annual resolutions, and the end of the cold war, together with South Africa’s
accession to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and the
dismantling of its nuclear weapons programme, created a favourable context. In 1992,
in a session in Dakar, the OAU Council of Ministers reaffirmed that conditions were
ripe to implement the Declaration on the Denuclearization of Africa and, working
with a United Nations-appointed group of experts, produced a draft treaty in six
meetings from 1991 to 1995, which was finalized at Pelindaba, South Africa, and
adopted by the OAU Assembly of Heads of State and Government. The African
Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty (commonly known as the Treaty of Pelindaba) was
26-11249 29/74
[[page 30]]
A/81/293
signed in Cairo on 11 April 1996 and, pursuant to article 18, entered into force on
15 July 2009, upon the twenty-eighth ratification.
144. The Pelindaba Treaty recognized that progress toward establishing a nuclear-
weapon-free zone in the Middle East would help alleviate long-standing security
concerns of Northern and Eastern African States and thus strengthen peace and
security in the region. A nuclear-weapon-free zone in the Middle East is therefore of
particular interest to Africa, as it would create a contiguous zone free of nuclear
weapons and could include the participation of certain African States. Consistent with
this logic, the Preamble of the Treaty affirms that the establishment of other nuclear-
weapon-free zones, especially in the Middle East, would enhance the security of
States Parties to the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone, and reiterates the continent’s
commitment to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and full
implementation of all its provisions.
2. Extent and composition of the Zone
145. Consideration of the extent and composition of the African Nuclear-Weapon-
Free Zone required adherence to relevant OAU resolutions, while at the same time
avoiding creating obstacles to the implementation of the Treaty’s protocols by the five
nuclear-weapon States and other States with territory within the Zone.
146. The Treaty defines the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone as “the territory of
the continent of Africa, islands States members of OAU and all islands considered by
the Organization of African Unity in its resolutions to be part of Africa”. The Treaty
also defines “Territory” as “the land territory, internal waters, territorial seas and
archipelagic waters and the airspace above them as well as the sea bed and subsoil
beneath.”
147. The extent of the geographical area covered by the African Nuclear-Weapon-
Free Zone is included in annex I to the Treaty, which is a map showing the limits of
the Zone. On this map, the Chagos Archipelago and Diego Garcia are included with
the declaration: “appears without prejudice to the question of sovereignty.”
148. As at 16 January 2026, 54 member States of the African Union have signed
and/or ratified the Treaty, with 44 members having deposited their instruments of
ratification with the African Union Commission.
3. Obligations and commitments by zonal States
149. The Pelindaba Treaty establishes a comprehensive set of obligations to ensure
that Africa remains free of nuclear explosive devices. Article 3 requires States Parties
to renounce such devices entirely, prohibiting their research, development,
manufacture, stockpiling, possession, control or acquisition, as well as any form of
assistance, encouragement or receipt of assistance for these purposes. “Nuclear
explosive device” is defined broadly to encompass any nuclear weapon or other
explosive device capable of releasing nuclear energy, in assembled or unassembled
form, excluding the means of transport or delivery of such a weapon or device if
separable from and not an indivisible part of it.
150. Consistent with this renunciation, the Treaty also prohibits the stationing (art. 4)
and testing (art. 5) of nuclear explosive devices anywhere within the Zone. By means
of article 4, the Treaty thus codifies what is not codified in the Treaty on the
Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons; however, each Party decides independently
whether the transit of such weapons through its territories is allowed, with the caveat
that this is done without prejudice to the purposes and objectives of the Treaty. One
of the motivations of States Parties for article 5 was to address the issue of the testing
30/74 26-11249
[[page 31]]
A/81/293
of nuclear weapons conducted on the continent, given the harmful consequences for
African populations.
151. Region‑specific provisions were included to address the unique historical and
security context of Africa. Unlike other nuclear-weapon-free zones, the African
Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone included a country that already possessed a nuclear
weapon capability. Therefore, article 6 requires the declaration, dismantling and
destruction or conversion of any nuclear explosive devices and associated
manufacturing facilities previously possessed by a State Party, allowing verification
by IAEA and by the Commission established under the Treaty, without that implying
the creation of a parallel regional safeguards system. The inclusion of such an article
demonstrated that the possession of nuclear-weapon capabilities by a State in a region
should not in itself be a barrier to the establishment of a nuclear-weapon-free zone.
152. Environmental concerns led to the inclusion of article 7, which prohibits the
dumping of radioactive waste, drawing on the 1991 Bamako Convention on the Ban
of the Import into Africa and the Control of Transboundary Movement and
Management of Hazardous Wastes within Africa as a guideline. To reinforce the
protection of peaceful nuclear activities and prevent cross‑border humanitarian
impacts, article 11 prohibits any armed attack on nuclear installations, thereby
contributing to the strengthening of international law by legally protecting nuclear
sites from attack by member States.
4. Obligations and commitments by extra-zonal States
153. The Pelindaba Treaty has three Protocols regarding the obligations of extra-
zonal States:
(a) Protocol I calls on nuclear-weapon States not to use or threaten to use a
nuclear weapon against any Party to the Treaty and against any territory within the
Zone;
(b) Protocol II calls on the nuclear-weapon States not to test or assist in or
encourage the testing of a nuclear explosive device in the Zone;
(c) Protocol III calls on Parties which are de jure or de facto in control of
territories situated within the Zone (namely France and Spain) to apply the provisions
contained in articles 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 of the Treaty and to ensure the application
of the safeguards specified in annex II to the Treaty to the territories under their
control.
154. China, France, the Russian Federation and the United Kingdom have signed and
ratified Protocols I and II. The United States has signed but not ratified Protocol I and
II. France has signed and ratified Protocol III. Spain has neither signed nor ratified
Protocol III.
155. It must be noted that France, the Russian Federation and the United Kingdom
have made reservations and interpretive declarations with their ratifications.
5. Negative security assurances
156. States Parties to the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty consider that
negative security assurances should not be perceived as recognition for the continued
possession of nuclear weapons nor as an end in themselves, but rather as pragmatic,
interim measures to strengthen the non-proliferation regime and the objective of a
nuclear-weapon-free world. The Zone provides regional protection against possible
nuclear attacks on their territories, chiefly through its three Protocols to the Treaty,
most notably Protocol I.

157. The States Parties consider that qualified assurances provided under the
Pelindaba Treaty at present are not seen as sufficient in view of the non-ratification
by one nuclear-weapon State and the reservations and declarations that have been
included by three others.
158. In addition, the States Parties view the assurances under Security Council
resolution 984 (1995), as given to their nuclear-weapon-free zone, as insufficient, in
the light of the 1996 International Court of Justice advisory opinion in Legality of the
Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons and the Final Documents of the 2000 and 2010
Review Conferences of the Parties to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons, both of which made clear that security assurances should be granted within
the Treaty framework. Moreover, unilateral statements by nuclear-weapon States do
not meet long-standing non-nuclear-weapon States criteria for legally binding,
effective, unconditional, non-discriminatory and irrevocable negative security
assurances and, in this regard, the States Parties advocate for urgent negotiations on
universal and legally binding security assurances.
6. Verification, compliance and control
159. The Pelindaba Treaty establishes several mechanisms to verify compliance.
160. First, article 9 of the Treaty and annex II thereto deal with the verification of
peaceful uses. They provide that the nuclear activities of Parties are to be conducted
for exclusively peaceful uses, verified through the conclusion of comprehensive
safeguards agreements with IAEA to ensure that nuclear material is not diverted from
peaceful nuclear activities. Additionally, Parties are not to provide source or special
fissionable material to any non-nuclear-weapon State unless it complies with IAEA
comprehensive safeguards. The requirement of a comprehensive safeguards
agreement with IAEA as a condition of export is seen as a way to affirm Africa’s
commitment to non-proliferation not only in its region but in other regions as well.
161. Second, article 12 of the Treaty establishes the African Commission on Nuclear
Energy as the Treaty implementation body, which ensures compliance by reviewing
State Parties’ annual reports and IAEA safeguards, implementing a complaints
procedure and promoting cooperation in peaceful nuclear technology.
162. The annual reporting obligation that forms part of the verification of compliance
is set up through article 13 and annex III.
163. While not specifically provided for in the Treaty, Parties are required to take
appropriate legal, administrative and other measures to implement the prohibitions.
Consequently, national reports submitted to the Commission appraise these actions
and measures and provide the Commission with the ability to verify whether there are
gaps in the implementation of the Treaty by a Party.
164. A copy of the overall conclusions of the most recent report by IAEA on its
inspection activities in the territory of the Party concerned is also provided to the
Commission, highlighting the interlinkage between the IAEA verification and the
verification by the Commission.
7. Dispute settlement resolution mechanism and enforcement
165. The existence of a link between a breach of obligations and a threat to
international peace and security is indispensable. The inclusion in the Pelindaba
Treaty of a complaints procedure and settlement of disputes enforces transparency,
enhances the effectiveness of the mechanism for ensuring compliance and is meant to
avoid any unilateral action by a Party that might suspect another Party of breaching
its Treaty obligations. Thus, no Party, whether zonal or extra-zonal, can conduct

[[page 33]]
A/81/293
illegal activities unchallenged, which enhances the security of all Parties as well as
of the Zone.
166. Article 15 of the Treaty stipulates that any dispute arising out of the
interpretation of the Treaty shall be settled by negotiation, by recourse to the
Commission or another procedure agreed to by the Parties, which may include
recourse to an arbitral panel or to the International Court of Justice.
167. Annex IV to the Treaty sets out the procedure for complaints and settlement of
disputes. Provision is made at the start of the process for bilateral consultation
between the Parties to resolve issues directly. If the matter is not so resolved in 30
days, the complainant Party may refer the complaint to the Commission.
168. Should the Commission consider that there is sufficient substance in the
complaint to warrant an inspection in the territory of that Party or territory of a Party
to Protocol III, the Commission may request IAEA to conduct such inspection. The
Commission may also designate its representatives to accompany the IAEA
inspectorate team. The inclusion of a provision whereby the Commission may have
its representatives accompany the IAEA inspectors also has the objective of enabling
African officials to learn more about implementing inspections and to develop greater
expertise in nuclear science and technology in Africa, which was one of the objectives
of the Treaty. Annex IV also makes provision for the Commission to establish its own
inspection mechanism if needed.
169. If a breach is confirmed, State Parties hold an extraordinary session to discuss
the matter and may make recommendations to the non-compliant party and the
African Union. The African Union has the option to refer the issue to the United
Nations Security Council, if necessary.
8. Institutional arrangements
170. The institutional arrangements for the Pelindaba Treaty primarily centre around
the African Commission on Nuclear Energy, the secretariat to the Commission, IAEA
and the African Union Commission.
171. The African Commission on Nuclear Energy is composed of 12 members elected

Commission’s budget and programme of work.

procedure developed in annex IV; encouraging regional and subregional programmes
for cooperation in the peaceful uses of nuclear science and technology; and promoting
international cooperation with extra-zonal States in the peaceful uses of nuclear
science and technology.
174. Through a decision taken in Cairo in 1996, the Commission is headquartered in
South Africa. Its secretariat is based in Pretoria and operates as the administrative and

9. Peaceful uses of nuclear energy
176. The African Commission on Nuclear Energy plays the crucial role of promoting
the peaceful uses of nuclear science and technology for economic and social
development. While doing so, the Commission upholds that all States should respect
each country’s sovereign choices and decisions relating to the peaceful uses of nuclear
energy in line with international obligations, without jeopardizing their policies or
international cooperation agreements and arrangements for peaceful uses of nuclear
energy and their nuclear fuel cycle policies.
177. Under the Treaty, each Party undertakes to conduct all activities for the peaceful
use of nuclear energy under strict non-proliferation measures; to provide assurance of
exclusively peaceful uses; to conclude a comprehensive safeguards agreement with
IAEA for the purpose of verifying compliance; and not to provide source or special
fissionable material, or equipment or material especially designed or prepared for the
processing, use or production of special fissionable material for peaceful purposes to
any non-nuclear-weapon State unless subject to a comprehensive safeguards
agreement concluded with IAEA.
178. The primary institutional function of IAEA under the Pelindaba Treaty is to
serve as the verification body with regard to the peaceful use of nuclear energy. IAEA
also supports the Treaty’s objective of promoting the peaceful use of nuclear science
and technology by encouraging Parties to use its assistance programmes.
179. In order to focus on intra-African cooperation in the development of nuclear
science and technology, some African members of IAEA established the African
Regional Cooperative Agreement for Research, Development and Training related to
Nuclear Science and Technology in 1990. The Pelindaba Treaty encourages
strengthened intra-African cooperation through the Agreement.
180. The African Commission on Nuclear Energy has also designated regional
collaborating centres in some States in Africa. The Commission collaborates with
these States on the basis of a collaborating centre agreement to promote and provide
support to States members of the African Union in developing their knowledge and
capabilities towards the safe and secure peaceful uses of nuclear technology on the
continent. These regional collaborating centres are established to assist the
Commission in implementing selected programmatic activities. The Commission also
utilizes cooperation agreements with other regional organizations and States for
training through the regional collaborating centres.
10. Lessons learned
181. One of the key elements that contributed to the adoption of the Pelindaba Treaty
was the sustained diplomatic efforts and political commitment of African leaders,
initiated in 1964, to make Africa a nuclear-weapon-free continent. This political
commitment was carried forward through various resolutions and decisions at the
34/74 26-11249
[[page 35]]
A/81/293
United Nations, OAU and other relevant forums and was sustained throughout the
period until the adoption of the treaty.
182. The importance of addressing the specific regional historical context must also
be emphasized. Suspicions about a South African nuclear weapons programme drove
the issue and also contributed to the development of the region-specific aspects of the
Treaty. This historical context provided valuable lessons for the negotiators to draw
upon, as can be seen in the strong role provided for IAEA to address proliferation
concerns, as well as the provision to keep open the option for an African inspection
mechanism.
183. The synergy at the continental level with the organs of the African Union and
the departments of the African Union Commission assists in addressing regional
development priorities using peaceful applications of nuclear science and technology.
184. The Treaty includes nine articles providing for States Parties’ obligations in
areas ranging from nuclear disarmament to safety, security and safeguards and
peaceful uses of nuclear energy, demonstrating that a prohibition on nuclear weapons
can coexist with, and even promote, the peaceful use of nuclear science and
technology for economic and social development.
185. Through the Pelindaba Treaty, African States have taken collective
responsibility for regional security and nuclear non-proliferation. Accordingly,
African States are implementing the provisions of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation
of Nuclear Weapons together with additional regional specific provisions, as can be
seen in the inclusion of elements that go beyond non-proliferation, such as the
security of nuclear installations, their protection from attacks and provision for the
safe management of radioactive materials. This highlights the flexibility of nuclear-
weapon-free zones to address international obligations as well region-specific
concerns.
186. The Pelindaba Treaty, particularly through articles 1 and 3 thereof, provides one
of the most comprehensive legal architectures among existing nuclear-weapon-free
zones, by utilizing a broad definition of a nuclear explosive device as covering any
nuclear weapon or other explosive device capable of releasing nuclear energy in
assembled or unassembled form, while excluding delivery systems only where they
are separable from and not an indivisible part of it, and by imposing comprehensive
prohibitions. This combination creates a holistic and future-proof regulatory
framework that closes both direct and indirect pathways to nuclear weaponization. Its
clarity, breadth and normative strength make the Pelindaba model highly suitable for
adoption or adaptation by other nuclear-weapon-free zones seeking legal certainty,
verification coherence and stronger global non-proliferation norms.
187. Noting the geographical overlap between the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free
Zone and the prospective Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and other
weapons of mass destruction, the above-mentioned lessons could be used to further
advance the negotiations on the said Middle East zone.
E. Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia (Treaty
of Semipalatinsk)
188. The Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia borders two nuclear-weapon
States; it is the only such zone located entirely in the northern hemisphere and situated
in a landlocked region. Furthermore, one of its members, Kazakhstan, formerly
possessed an arsenal of nuclear weapons and hosted a nuclear test site on its territory.

1. ⁠Treaty and the establishment of the Zone
189. The Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia (Treaty of
Semipalatinsk) is the fifth and, to date, the most recent instrument concluded to
establish a nuclear-weapon-free zone in a densely populated area. It is a legally
binding commitment by five Central Asian States – Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan,
Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan – not to manufacture, acquire, test or
possess nuclear weapons.
190. In September 1993, at the forty-eighth session of the United Nations General
Assembly, the President of Uzbekistan stated that his country was “a staunch
supporter of proclaiming Central Asia a nuclear-free zone”. In 1994, the Foreign
Minister of Kyrgyzstan expressed a similar position. Consequently, Kyrgyzstan and
Uzbekistan emerged as the primary proponents of the establishment of the Nuclear-
Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia. The adoption of the Almaty Declaration by the
Heads of State of the Central Asian countries on 28 February 1997 represented a
pivotal milestone, whereby the initiative to establish a nuclear-weapon-free zone in
Central Asia was formally recognized as a collective endeavour of all five nations.
191. In September 1997, in Tashkent, the foreign ministers of the Central Asian
States issued a statement appealing to the United Nations specialized agencies to
establish a group of United Nations experts, with the participation of a regional group
of experts, to consider ways and means of preparing and implementing a treaty
establishing a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia.
192. In December 1997, the General Assembly adopted resolution 52/38 S, entitled
“Establishment of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia”, which was sponsored
by all five States of the region and was the first in a series of resolutions adopted
under that title. Promotion of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia gained
traction in various other international forums, including within the framework of the
Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons Review Conferences, the
Conference on Disarmament and the Disarmament Commission.
193. In July 1998, Kyrgyzstan hosted a meeting involving experts from Central Asia,
the nuclear-weapon States and the United Nations. A document was presented at the
meeting, prepared by Kyrgyzstan in cooperation with international experts, entitled
“Basic elements of the treaty on a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia”. It was
stipulated that the drafting of the treaty text would take place under the auspices of
the United Nations. To this end, a special group of experts was established, which
discussed the draft treaty on a nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central Asia, prepared
with the assistance of the United Nations, in 1998–2000.
194. IAEA provided legal and technical expertise throughout the negotiation phase.
In particular, IAEA experts participated in drafting articles on nuclear safety, security,
the application of safeguards, and environmental security. Non-governmental
organizations also provided expert assistance, particularly during the period 1998–
2002.
195. In September 2002, the Parties, with the participation of the United Nations
Regional Centre for Peace and Disarmament in Asia and the Pacific, agreed upon the
preliminary text of the treaty. The final text was agreed upon in February 2005 in
Tashkent. It was signed on 8 September 2006 at the former Semipalatinsk nuclear test
site in Kazakhstan; hence, it is unofficially known as the Treaty of Semipalatinsk. The
Treaty was subsequently ratified by all five Central Asian States and entered into
force on 21 March 2009. Kyrgyzstan was designated as depositary of the Treaty. Since
2010, the General Assembly has considered biennially the agenda item entitled
“Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia” and adopting resolutions
thereon.

196. On 6 May 2014, in New York, the five nuclear-weapon States simultaneously
signed the Protocol to the Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia. As
of early 2026, the Protocol has been ratified by four nuclear-weapon States.25
197. In 2022, the General Assembly declared the region of Central Asia a zone of

of the Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia, emphasizing its
significance in the attainment of peace and security.
2. Extent and composition of the Zone
198. The Central Asian Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone includes Kazakhstan,
Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan.
199. A distinctive feature of the Zone is that its membership consists exclusively of
landlocked countries. In the western part of the Zone, two States – Kazakhstan and
Turkmenistan – border the Caspian Sea. This fact was considered as being of no
fundamental significance to the map of the Zone and the scope of application of the
Treaty in the western part of the Zone is defined by the land territory on the Caspian
coasts of Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan.
3. Obligations and commitments by zonal States
200. The Parties to the Treaty are obliged to contribute to the attainment of the
ultimate goal of eliminating nuclear weapons and of general and complete
disarmament under strict and effective international control. The Parties reaffirm their
obligations set out in the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons.
201. The basic obligations of the Parties are defined in article 3, paragraph 1, of the
Treaty, and they are similar to the basic obligations that are contained in other treaties
on nuclear-weapon-free zones.
202. At the same time, article 8 of the Treaty obligates the Parties to conclude a
safeguards agreement with IAEA and an Additional Protocol. This combination
distinguishes the Treaty of Semipalatinsk from other existing nuclear-weapon-free
zone treaties and may serve as an example for future zones.
203. Furthermore, the Treaty is the first nuclear-weapon-free zone treaty to
incorporate a provision obligating the Parties to implement the Comprehensive
Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty. In addition, the Treaty on a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in
Central Asia is the first to establish the obligations of the Parties concerning
environmental rehabilitation.
204. With regard to other agreements, article 12 provides that the Treaty does not
affect the rights and obligations of the Parties under other previously concluded
international treaties. In this connection, the Treaty imposes upon the Parties the
obligation to take all necessary measures for effective implementation of the purposes
and objectives of this Treaty in accordance with the main principles contained therein.

5. Negative security assurances
209. The signature by the five nuclear-weapon States of the Protocol to the Treaty on
a Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone in Central Asia, and its subsequent ratification by four
of them, has provided negative security assurances against the use or threat of use of
nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices against any Party to the Treaty.
210. The signatures and ratifications of the Protocol were facilitated by consultations
with the nuclear-weapon States and the expert assistance of the United Nations, in
accordance with the principles and guidelines for the establishment of nuclear-
weapon-free zones set forth in the report of the Disarmament Commission in 1999
(A/54/42, annex I, sect. C). These consultations resulted in the formulation of several
provisions that proved acceptable to the Central Asian States as well as to the nuclear-

withdrawn, thereby limiting its membership to the five States of Central Asia.

2 (a), including with respect to the western part of the region on the Caspian Sea. The
Russian Federation underscored this point upon signature of the Protocol by issuing
a declaration to the effect that it proceeds from the premise that the obligations of the
States Parties under article 1 of the Protocol are limited exclusively to the scope of
application (i.e. the geographical scope) of the nuclear-weapon-free zone in Central
Asia, as defined in article 2 (a) of the Treaty.

countries. The landlocked nature of the Central Asian region has rendered the issue
of transit of relatively lesser significance for four of the nuclear-weapon States that
have signed the Protocol to the Treaty. China and the United Kingdom did not address
the matter in their declarations. France issued an interpretative statement broadly
reaffirming the sovereign right of each Party, pursuant to article 4 of the Treaty, to
resolve issues related to transit. The Russian Federation entered a reservation whereby
it reserves the right not to be bound by its obligations under the Protocol should any
State Party allow the calling at its ports or the landing at its airfields of foreign

techniques for soil quality control and the management of scarce water resources.
(b) Relationship with international organizations, including the International Atomic
Energy Agency and other arrangements
230. All Central Asian States actively engage in technical cooperation programmes
with IAEA. Such interaction is conducted on a bilateral basis through country
programme frameworks, as well as through regional projects. Furthermore, IAEA
provides expert assistance to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan in the implementation of
their nuclear power plant construction plans.
10. Lessons learned
231. The history of the establishment of the Central Asian Nuclear-Weapon-Free
Zone underscores the importance of joint efforts by the States of the region in securing
a coordinated mechanism within the shortest possible time frame to engage with
international organizations, primarily the United Nations, as well as with nuclear-
weapon States.
232. Outstanding border issues that may affect the boundaries of the Zone, but are
not of fundamental significance to the objectives of its establishment, should be set
aside. In this regard, the experience gained in defining the western part of the Zone
may serve as a reference.
233. The process of establishing the Central Asian Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone
demonstrates that addressing environmental remediation issues resulting from
activities related to the development of nuclear weapons can incentivize certain States
to support the initiative to establish a nuclear-weapon-free zone.
234. Regular consultations among diplomats from the countries of the Zone
constitute a useful mechanism. Concurrently, the establishment of a dedicated
secretariat body for the Zone would enhance the functioning of such mechanisms,
particularly by preserving institutional memory and fostering both intra-zonal and
interzonal cooperation.
235. Until the early 2000s, a critical focus of verification and compliance monitoring
was the implementation of various arms control and disarmament treaties in Central
Asia. The relevant inspection expertise may be applicable in other zones and in the
establishment of future ones. Additionally, the experience of Kazakhstan in
denuclearization and, in particular, the decommissioning of nuclear testing
infrastructure could provide valuable insights for the establishment of new nuclear-
weapon-free zones

F. Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and all other weapons of

1. ⁠Efforts to establish a Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and other
weapons of mass destruction
236. The establishment of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in the Middle East has always
been regarded as an essential component for regional and global security and as a
supportive factor in achieving comprehensive and lasting peace in the Middle East.
From the adoption of General Assembly resolution 3263 (XXIX), the first one on such
a zone, until now, the establishment of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in the Middle East
has been strongly supported by the United Nations and at consecutive Review
Conferences of the Parties to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons,
as a measure for disarmament and non-proliferation as well as for achieving the
universality of that Treaty. Persistent and ongoing conflicts and tensions in the region
have further reinforced the importance and necessity of establishing such a zone in
the Middle East, particularly in view of the volatile global and regional situation and
its adverse effects on the further advancement of negotiations towards the
establishment of the zone.
237. Since 1974, the countries of the region have initiated negotiation processes with
the goal of establishing the zone, including the Working Group on Arms Control and
Regional Security, established at the Madrid Peace Conference in 1991, which held
five sessions at which threats perceptions were analysed and possible actions aimed
at establishing the zone were considered, but which, unfortunately, did not produce
any concrete outcome.
238. From 1980 to 2018, there was consensus on the annual General Assembly
resolution on the establishment of the zone. During those years, the resolution held
that, pending and during the establishment of the zone, States of the region should
declare solemnly that they will refrain, on a reciprocal basis, from producing,
acquiring, or in any other way possessing nuclear weapons and nuclear explosive
devices. They will also refrain from permitting the stationing of nuclear weapons on
their territories by third parties. Through that annual resolution, they also agreed to
place all their nuclear facilities under IAEA safeguards, to declare their support for
the establishment of the zone and to deposit such declarations with the Security
Council. This in itself showed that the momentum of the 1973 war brought all Middle
Eastern countries into agreement by consensus with other States Members of the
United Nations on the basic elements needed for the establishment of the zone.
239. As of 2018, the voting pattern on the annual resolution changed, making it no
longer possible to achieve consensus. It is therefore necessary to further understand
the causes of this negative turn of events and to develop recommendations with a
view to overcoming it. To stimulate progress, several initiatives were launched,
unilaterally or collectively, including:
(a) The decision by the members of the League of Arab States to join the
majority in favour of the indefinite extension of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation
of Nuclear Weapons at the 1995 Review and Extension Conference. Such a decision
became possible after Djibouti and Oman, the only Arab countries that were not States
Parties to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, were successfully
convinced to ratify the Treaty before the adoption of the resolution, in return for the
strong commitment reflected in the resolution on the Middle East adopted as an
integral part of the indefinite extension package;
(b) The 2002 Arab Peace Initiative, launched by King Abdullah of Saudi
Arabia and supported unanimously by the Heads of State and Government of the

240. The following elements reflect approaches that have been discussed or could

2. Geographical scope: extent and composition
241. The geographical scope of a Middle East nuclear-weapon-free zone has been
addressed primarily through the principle of regional self-definition. General
Assembly resolutions and reports of the Secretary-General have generally refrained
from prescribing fixed boundaries. They have instead affirmed that the extent of the
zone should be determined by the States of the region. Within the context of the Treaty
on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, this approach has been reflected in
repeated references to the “region of the Middle East”, allowing for the
accommodation of evolving considerations.
242. In practice, United Nations documentation related to the Middle East nuclear-
weapon-free zone since the issuance of the report of the Secretary-General on the
establishment of a nuclear-weapon-free zone in the region of the Middle East
(A/45/435) has most commonly envisaged a core geographical composition
encompassing the States of the League of Arab States, together with the Islamic
Republic of Iran and Israel. This understanding has been implicitly reflected in General
Assembly and IAEA resolutions and the mandate of the United Nations-convened
Conferences on the Establishment of a Middle East Zone Free of Nuclear Weapons and
Other Weapons of Mass Destruction, in which all States of the Middle East have been
invited to participate. In General Assembly decision 73/546, “all States of the Middle
East” is defined on the basis of the report of the Director General of IAEA on the
application of Agency safeguards in the Middle East (GOV/2018/38-GC(62)/6),
which contains a footnote explaining that all States of the Middle East means
“members of the League of Arab States, the Islamic Republic of Iran and Israel”.
3. Object and purpose of the zone
243. As stated in General Assembly and Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons resolutions, the object and purpose of a treaty establishing a Middle East
zone free of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction would be to
ensure, through a legally binding and effectively verifiable regime, the total absence
of all such weapons from the territories, jurisdiction and control of the States of the
region. The treaty would prohibit, without exception or discrimination, the
development, acquisition, possession, stockpiling, deployment and use of nuclear
weapons and other weapons of mass destruction.
244. The treaty would seek to contribute to regional and international peace and
security, while addressing the specific security dynamics and threat perceptions,
including persistent armed conflicts, foreign occupation and mutual distrust.
245. The prospective treaty would reinforce and operationalize existing international
non-proliferation and disarmament obligations within a regional framework tailored
to the Middle East. Drawing on precedents from other treaties establishing nuclear-
weapon-free zones, the treaty would require the application of comprehensive IAEA
safeguards to all nuclear material in the region and promote full compliance with the
Convention on the Prohibition of the Development, Production, Stockpiling and Use
of Chemical Weapons and on Their Destruction and the Convention on the Prohibition
of the Development, Production and Stockpiling of Bacteriological (Biological) and
Toxin Weapons and on Their Destruction. In doing so, the treaty would address
regional gaps in adherence and implementation.

4. Distinct character of a Middle East zone covering all categories of weapons of
mass destruction: Relationship with existing international legal instruments,
particularly the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, the
Chemical Weapons Convention and the Biological Weapons Convention
246. A defining characteristic of a Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and other
weapons of mass destruction would be its comprehensive scope, encompassing nuclear,
chemical and biological weapons within a single and integrated framework. Unlike
existing nuclear-weapon-free zones, which focus exclusively on nuclear weapons, a
Middle East zone has consistently been conceived, through General Assembly
resolutions, the outcomes of Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons
Review Conferences and Security Council resolution 687 (1991), as a region-wide
prohibition on all categories of weapons of mass destruction. This integrated approach
would seek to address the specific security realities of the Middle East.
247. The treaty establishing such a zone would seek to complement, reinforce and
regionalize existing international legal instruments and not to replace or duplicate
them. By integrating these global regimes into a single regional instrument, the
establishment of the Middle East zone would represent a coherent, distinctive and
unprecedented contribution.
248. It is noteworthy that there is only one country in the region that is not a State
Party to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, two out of four
States not Parties to the Chemical Weapons Convention are in the Middle East and
three countries in the region have not yet joined the Biological Weapons Convention.
5. Scope of obligations and prohibitions applicable to States Parties to a treaty,
including with regard to joining relevant multilateral legal instruments
249. Building on the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and other
international instruments prohibiting weapons of mass destruction, as well as the
treaties establishing existing nuclear-weapon-free zones, States Parties would be
expected to:
(a) Ensure the total absence of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass
destruction within their territories, including land, airspace and territorial waters
under their jurisdiction or control. This obligation would extend to all activities
related to the development, acquisition, possession, deployment or use of such
weapons;
(b) Refrain from permitting the stationing, testing or transiting of nuclear,
chemical or biological weapons by any other State within the boundaries of the zone;
(c) Refrain from any act that would be inconsistent with the objectives of the
zone;
(d) Join, or remain Parties to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons as non-nuclear-weapon States; the Chemical Weapons Convention; and the
Biological Weapons Convention; as well as place all nuclear material under
comprehensive safeguards with IAEA, in accordance with their respective obligations
under these instruments;
(e) Undertake appropriate measures to facilitate verification, compliance and
transparency to ensure that the objectives of the Treaty are met.
6. Verification
250. A robust verification regime is essential to ensure compliance and promote trust
and confidence among the members of the zone. The general parameters are well

253. The inalienable right to peaceful uses of nuclear energy is fundamental to the

the Middle East would need to be able to enjoy this right without any discrimination
and in accordance with their obligations under the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons and their IAEA safeguards.
254. Effective arrangements to promote and facilitate cooperation among members
of such a zone in the field of peaceful uses of nuclear energy would encourage States
to join and remain within the nuclear-weapon-free zone, as well as enhance
transparency and build confidence among the members. Cooperation among different
nuclear-weapon-free zones could also serve as an additional incentive to join a
nuclear-weapon-free zone, as well as to create new ones.

biological and chemical materials and technologies, and ensure that this right is fully

257. Provisions on clarifications, consultations and cooperation in multilateral

ambiguities or interpretative divergences, thereby facilitating the cooperative
resolution of disputes while reserving recourse to arbitration or the International
Court of Justice as a last resort.

mechanism, featuring clear timelines and sequenced actions and measures, that would
enhance transparency and predictability while avoiding politicization. To support
these procedures, a designated body could be established under the future treaty to
oversee and coordinate the mechanism, receive and relay requests for clarification
and facilitate bilateral or multilateral consultations.
9. Entry into force and duration
259. The question of entry into force is a critical element for a treaty establishing a
Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction.
Some views have consistently underscored the need to balance the legal
considerations with the complex security dynamics, suggesting that overly rigid
entry-into-force requirements could risk delaying or preventing the treaty’s
conclusion and implementation. It might be necessary to elaborate a hybrid, flexible
and creative approach to the question of entry into force of the prospective treaty,
building on the experiences of existing zones while recognizing the context-specific
challenges.
260. One possible approach, similar to that taken in the Treaty of Semipalatinsk,
would be to condition entry into force on the ratification of the treaty by all States of
the Middle East. This model, which emphasizes universality and equality, is
potentially the most coherent as it ensures that no State is placed at a strategic
disadvantage. However, experience with regard to other relevant international
instruments indicates that such a condition may significantly delay entry into force if
consensus proves difficult to achieve.
261. An alternative scenario could involve a minimum-threshold approach, whereby
the treaty would enter into force upon ratification by specified States in the region
defined on the basis of specific agreed criteria.
262. A phased or conditional entry-into-force mechanism could also be considered,
particularly in the light of the comprehensive coverage of weapons of mass
destruction in the future treaty. Under this scenario, core prohibitions could enter into
force upon ratification by a group of States, while additional obligations would
become operative upon broader participation or fulfilment of agreed benchmarks. The
relationship between entry into force and adherence to existing international legal
instruments would represent another important consideration.

10. Institutional arrangements
263. Effective implementation of the prospective treaty would require a robust and
responsive institutional architecture capable of supporting, inter alia, verification,
compliance, consultation and confidence-building. The architecture supporting the
zone should aim to strike a balance between leveraging existing international
structures and promoting regional ownership.
264. At the core of the institutional framework would be a conference of States
Parties, serving as the principal governing body. As with the Treaties of Tlatelolco,
Pelindaba and Bangkok, such a conference would be responsible for reviewing
implementation, adopting decisions and providing further guidance.
265. Supporting an institutional architecture, including a conference of States
Parties, would normally require a permanent secretariat or another form of
administrative unit to ensure effective functioning, continuity and institutional
memory. Such a body would also support functions of cooperation, coordination,
consultation and dispute settlement in addition to serving as a liaison with existing
relevant international structures. This role can be undertaken by a dedicated
permanent secretariat, as in the case of the Agency for the Prohibition of Nuclear
Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean or the African Commission on Nuclear
Energy, or it can be delegated to an existing structure, such as ASEAN in the case of
the Treaty of Bangkok. It is noteworthy that, to date, there is currently no single
regional organizational structure that includes all the States of the region in its
membership.
266. On the other hand, there is a growing conviction of the utility of depending on
existing international mechanisms with regard to verification and compliance.
11. Dialogue and cooperation with existing nuclear-weapon-free zones, including on
lessons learned and best practices
267. The establishment of each nuclear-weapon-free zone can be viewed as a step
toward achieving a world free of nuclear weapons. This feature of nuclear-weapon-
free zones requires dialogue and cooperation among the existing nuclear-weapon-free
zones, as well as between existing and prospective zones, while fully taking into
account the unique characteristics of each one.
12. Protocols on effective assurances by nuclear-weapon States against the use or

commit to respecting the denuclearized status of the zone and undertake not to use or
threaten to use nuclear weapons against any of its members.
269. Non-nuclear-weapon States have consistently urged the nuclear-weapon States
to join these protocols without interpretive declarations, reservations or
preconditions. The Middle East could benefit from overcoming the challenges faced
by other zones in the light of the reservations and interpretative declarations
deposited. There has been growing interest in and proposals for assessing the current

G. Mongolia’s nuclear-weapon-free status
1. Establishment of the status
271. In 1992, the President of Mongolia, Punsalmaagiin Ochirbat, declared the
country’s nuclear-weapon-free status and pledged to work toward acquiring security
guarantees.
272. For more than three decades, Mongolia has sought to strengthen both its own
national security and international security by broadening the scope of the nuclear-
weapon-free zone regime beyond its traditional boundaries. The establishment of its
nuclear-weapon-free status is intrinsically linked to its historical experience.
273. During the cold war, Mongolia was situated at the intersection of broader
geopolitical tensions. Concerns arose regarding the potential implications of these
dynamics for Mongolia’s security, including the possibility that its territory could be
affected by military developments involving neighbouring States.
274. Mongolia’s nuclear-weapon-free status is not based on a multilateral regional
treaty but reflects a sui generis, single-State approach.
275. According to article VII of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons, any group of States has the right to conclude regional treaties ensuring the
total absence of nuclear weapons in their respective territories. When that Treaty was
adopted, the overall approach was to ensure that many non-nuclear-weapon States
would become parties to nuclear-weapon-free zone treaties.
276. Nevertheless, the 1975 United Nations study recognized that nuclear-weapon-
free zone obligations may be assumed by individual countries, notably “obligations
relating to the establishment of nuclear-weapon-free zones may be assumed not only
by groups of States, including entire continents or large geographical regions, but also
by smaller groups of States and even individual countries”.27
277. Subsequently, General Assembly resolution 3472 B (XXX) (1975), defined the
concept of nuclear-weapon-free zones and formalized the “group-State” approach in
that same year. At the 1997 session of the Disarmament Commission, Mongolia
proposed considering not only the traditional group-State zones but also the concept
of single-State zones. Mongolia emphasized the importance of the latter for stability
and submitted a working paper on the subject (A/CN/10/195) for consideration. The
idea received considerable support. However, at that time, it was not possible to reach
consensus on the establishment of single-State zones.
278. It was emphasized in the 1999 Disarmament Commission report containing
principles and guidelines for establishing further nuclear-weapon-free zones that the
definition provided in General Assembly resolution 3472 B (XXX) was indicative.

(1995) were applicable to the case of Mongolia. However, they are non-treaty-based
and therefore constitute political commitments rather than legally binding guarantees.
287. Mongolia welcomed the joint statement by the nuclear-weapon States as a step
toward institutionalizing its status at the international level. Since then, the country
has continued to seek ways to further solidify its nuclear-weapon-free status.
288. In 2001, the Department of Disarmament Affairs organized an informal meeting
in Sapporo, Japan, between representatives of Mongolia and the nuclear-weapon
States to discuss the legal situation regarding Mongolia’s status. As a result of the
meeting, it was suggested that Mongolia could either pursue a trilateral treaty with its
neighbours concerning its status or negotiate a broader treaty involving Mongolia and
the five nuclear-weapon States. Consequently, Mongolia drafted a trilateral treaty and
an accompanying protocol for the non-neighbouring nuclear-weapon States to
endorse and support the agreement.
289. In 2009, during trilateral informal meetings held in Geneva between
representatives of Mongolia, the Russian Federation and China, there was a general
exchange of views on Mongolia’s drafts. The country also held consultations with the
other nuclear-weapon States. However, consensus could not be reached on the
establishment of an international treaty on Mongolia’s status.
290. In the light of the possibility of pursuing non-treaty-based forms of negative
security assurances, Mongolia and the nuclear-weapon States signed parallel
declarations in 2012. Through these declarations, the nuclear-weapon States
committed to respect Mongolia’s nuclear-weapon-free status and not to assist in any
action that would violate it – an arrangement described as “light security assurance”
in the context of non-traditional zones – while Mongolia formally reiterated the
obligations it had undertaken, including those reflected in its national legislation.

10.⁠ ⁠Lessons learned
300. Mongolia’s 1992 declaration and subsequent actions to recognize its nuclear-
weapon-free status have demonstrated how individual States can exercise leadership
in strengthening non-proliferation norms. By setting a precedent for impactful
unilateral commitments, Mongolia helped shape global expectations and discussions
around innovative models for nuclear-weapon-free zones. The broad endorsement it
has received confirms that leadership in norm-building is widely accessible to all
States.
301. Single‑State nuclear-weapon-free status may be a viable complement to regional
nuclear-weapon-free zones, especially where political conditions make regional
agreements difficult; however, recognition and assurances require sustained
diplomatic and multilateral engagement.
302. Mongolia’s nuclear-weapon-free status can thus play a bridge-building role
between traditional and emerging non-traditional zones by strengthening and
broadening the nuclear-weapon-free zone and Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons regimes. Some of the lessons learned in this process are as follows:
(a) Mongolia was historically exposed to the effects of broader geopolitical
tensions during the cold war period;

(b) Negotiations on establishing single-State nuclear-weapon-free status have

A. Existing zones
1. Contribution of nuclear-weapon-free zones to the strengthening of the
international security architecture
318. By legally excluding nuclear weapons from clearly defined regions, nuclear-
weapon-free zones give effect to agreed disarmament and non-proliferation
commitments and limit the avenues through which nuclear weapons might otherwise
be introduced, developed or acquired. Thus, they contribute significantly to
strengthening the international security architecture.
319. At the regional level, nuclear-weapon-free zones enhance security by fostering
greater trust, predictability and cooperation among participating States. They provide
a framework for sustained dialogue, institutionalize jointly agreed norms and legally
binding commitments, and promote the implementation of common projects and
activities. By prohibiting the development, acquisition and deployment of nuclear
weapons within designated areas, nuclear-weapon-free zones also contribute to
nuclear risk reduction and promote security and stability among regional actors.
320. These measures help to build an interdependent system based on shared
understandings and mutual obligations. They also often benefit from dedicated
institutional structures that facilitate monitoring, enable timely communication,
promote the awareness, formulation, formalization and implementation of agreed
norms, and provide mechanisms to prevent, address and resolve possible tensions or
disputes. At the international level, nuclear-weapon-free zones reinforce nuclear
disarmament and non-proliferation obligations, while fostering dialogue, stability and
engagement, including with nuclear-weapon States, particularly in relation to the
conclusions and implementation of relevant protocols and the provision of negative
security assurances.
321. Nuclear-weapon-free zones are also complementary to international nuclear
disarmament efforts and non-proliferation treaties. The role of nuclear-weapon-free
zones is acknowledged in article VII of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons and is consistent with its three pillars: nuclear disarmament,
non-proliferation and peaceful uses of nuclear energy. Nuclear-weapon-free zones
also support and reinforce IAEA safeguards system and the verification framework
established under the Treaty. All nuclear-weapon-free zone treaties require their
Parties to conclude comprehensive safeguards agreements with IAEA, thereby
strengthening the application of safeguards and ensuring that nuclear activities remain
exclusively peaceful. In some cases, nuclear-weapon-free zones also encourage or
require adherence to voluntary additional verification measures, such as the Model
26-11249 59/74
[[page 60]]
A/81/293
Protocol Additional to the Agreements between States and the International Atomic
Energy Agency for the Application of Safeguards, thus providing increased
assurances and serving as a significant confidence-building measure. They can also
provide additional political and legal assurances that reinforce non-proliferation
obligations, contributing to a more robust and multilayered verification architecture.
Nuclear-weapon-free zones thus strengthen both the effectiveness and efficiency of
the IAEA safeguard system.
322. The objective of the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, which has not yet
entered into force, is supported by nuclear-weapon-free zones that incorporate legally
binding prohibitions on nuclear explosive testing within their zones. By embedding
such obligations into regional treaties, nuclear-weapon-free zones help to consolidate
and universalize the norm against nuclear testing, thereby supporting the core
objective of the Treaty. In addition, nuclear-weapon-free zones play a significant role
in sustaining political momentum for the Treaty’s entry into force. They encourage
adherence to testing moratoriums, promote compliance with verification-related
practices and foster regional cooperation in support of the Treaty’s verification
mechanism. Through their institutional frameworks and regular consultations,
nuclear-weapon-free zones also provide platforms for dialogue and confidence-
building, reinforcing transparency and trust among States regarding prohibitions on
testing. Taken together, these contributions strengthen the global norm against nuclear
testing and complement the Treaty by advancing its principles through practice.
323. Many of the Parties to the treaties on nuclear-weapon-free zones are also Parties
to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons. The Treaty draws on and
contains some of the same provisions contained in treaties establishing nuclear-
weapon-free zones such as the development, testing, possession, use and threat of use
of nuclear weapons. Some nuclear-weapon-free zones implement provisions related
to victim assistance, environmental remediation and international cooperation,
embedding them in regional contexts, which relate to similar obligations for the
Parties to the Treaty.
324. Nuclear-weapon-free zones can contribute to the strengthening of national
measures related to the implementation of controls of export control systems of
nuclear and dual-use materials and technologies, including in the fulfilment of
obligations outlined by the Security Council in its resolution 1540 (2004). By
establishing legally binding regional obligations, they encourage States to adopt and
maintain robust national export control systems to prevent the transfer of items that
could support the development of nuclear weapons. In doing so, nuclear-weapon-free
zones enhance collective vigilance, improve compliance with non-proliferation norms
and reinforce the broader international architecture related to the transfer of nuclear-
related materials and technologies.
325. Nuclear-weapon-free zones contribute to advancing the goal of attaining and
sustaining a nuclear weapon-free world. They therefore play an important role in
strengthening both regional and global security. They contribute to enhanced
cooperation with international organizations and promote disarmament education, as
well as reflection on challenges in the field of disarmament and nuclear
non-proliferation. By providing structured spaces for dialogue, and in the light of
evolving security dynamics, nuclear-weapon-free zones are well positioned to foster
common approaches to both long-standing and emerging issues. In this context,
nuclear-weapon-free zones can serve as practical laboratories for the development
and implementation of innovative solutions, thereby sustaining and expanding their
positive contribution to the international peace and security architecture. They could
also act as valuable models for the establishment of future zones, helping to advance
the broader objective of a world free of nuclear weapons. They also contribute to
60/74 26-11249
[[page 61]]
A/81/293
reducing nuclear risks by diminishing incentives for the deployment or acquisition of
nuclear weapons within their regions.
2. Strengthening capacity
326. The institutional capacity of nuclear-weapon-free zones varies significantly
from zone to zone. The Latin American and the Caribbean zone established an
organization to oversee the implementation of the Tlatelolco Treaty – the Agency for
the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons in Latin America and the Caribbean. The
Pelindaba Treaty established the African Commission on Nuclear Energy, which
serves as the secretariat for the implementation of the Treaty. The South-East Asian
and South Pacific zones are embedded in regional intergovernmental organizations –
ASEAN and the Pacific Islands Forum, respectively. The Central Asian zone has no
institutional support. Each of these structures comes with different capacity
challenges. Zones can get lost amid the myriad priorities of regional organizations.
The absence of institutional structures can affect institutional memory.
327. To strengthen their implementation and further enhance their unique
contributions to peace and security, and depending on the specific context of their
own regions, some nuclear-weapon-free zones could benefit from institutional
strengthening. For example, some nuclear-weapon-free zones could benefit from
dedicated secretariats or implementation support units, where not already established,
to support institutionalization, monitoring and ongoing dialogue among States
Parties.
328. Ensuring stable, predictable and long-term funding remains a key challenge, and
it is essential for sustaining progress and maintaining effective institutional
arrangements, within the specific conditions of each region.
329. Enhanced cooperation among nuclear-weapon-free zones is also beneficial. By
fostering interzonal exchanges, zones can benefit from peer learning, share best
practices and better coordinate their approaches at the international level. Similarly,
training and networking opportunities can support the development of staff within
coordinating bodies and strengthen institutional capacity.
330. Cognizant of the considerable variance between zones, the following capacity-
building options and recommendations are presented:
(a) Zones could share their experience in managing institutional support to
create best practices in strengthening such support. This could be incorporated into a
series of publications for zone members and for the benefit of prospective zones;

3. Strengthening negative security assurances
331. The provision of negative security assurances remains a central element of the
nuclear‑weapon‑free zone architecture, contributing to the security of zonal States
and reinforcing the integrity of such zones, as well as the broader objectives of
disarmament and nuclear non‑proliferation.
332. While existing treaties establishing nuclear-weapon-free zones, in general,
include legally binding protocols open for accession by nuclear‑weapon States,
practice has shown that the scope, interpretation and implementation of these
assurances continue to vary. In this context, strengthening negative security
assurances requires approaches that advance their effectiveness and reliability while
taking into account the diversity of circumstances across regions and treaties. To date,
only one of the nuclear‑weapon‑free zone treaties has its negative security assurance
protocol signed and ratified by the five nuclear‑weapon States, although four of them
issued interpretative declarations or reservations at the time of ratification. This
remains a crucial pending issue for the rest of the existing nuclear‑weapon‑free zone
and must be kept in mind for the establishment of future zones, including in the
Middle East.
333. Experience to date indicates that challenges relating to negative security
assurances arise, among others, from differing interpretations of treaty obligations,
the formulation of interpretative declarations and reservations, and the absence of full
adherence to relevant treaties and/or protocols. Some nuclear‑weapon States have
indicated that commitments to military alliances and the absence of common treaty
frameworks can affect the extension of assurances.
334. At the same time, approaches outside treaty-based frameworks have been
explored. The experience of Mongolia is illustrative in this regard, reflecting political
commitments undertaken by nuclear-weapon States not to use or threaten to use
nuclear weapons and not to contribute to acts that would undermine the
nuclear‑weapon‑free status. While such arrangements do not substitute legally
binding protocols, they may nevertheless contribute to confidence‑building,
predictability and the progressive strengthening of the normative framework
surrounding negative security assurances.

335. Enhanced dialogue among zonal States, as well as their institutional bodies and
nuclear-weapon States, may offer a practical tool for addressing outstanding issues
related to scope, interpretation and implementation. Such engagement, already in
place in certain contexts, could facilitate a more structured exchange, with a view to
identifying mutually acceptable pathways for strengthening assurances over time,
including in regions where the establishment of new zones remains under
consideration.
336. In the light of these considerations, as discussed by the group, the following
options and recommendations are presented:
(a) Zones could engage in regular consultations, including through their treaty
bodies or coordinating mechanisms, with nuclear-weapon States, in order to address
questions relating to the interpretation of, reservations and declarations to and
implementation of negative security assurances;
(b) Zones could promote continued engagement with nuclear-weapon States
with a view to facilitating progressive accession to relevant treaty protocols, including
through clarification of concerns related to scope, definitions and geographical
coverage;
(c) Nuclear-weapon States are encouraged to continue to uphold their
commitment to the legal status of nuclear-weapon-free zones, sign and ratify all
relevant protocols open for signature as soon as possible and take concrete measures
to reinforce their negative security assurance obligations;
(d) States considering the development of future zones could invite
nuclear‑weapon States to participate in deliberations on protocols related to negative
security assurances;
(e) Zonal depositories could submit to the United Nations relevant
documentation for the compiling of declarations, reservations and interpretative
statements made by nuclear‑weapon States in relation to nuclear‑weapon‑free zone
protocols, with a view to improving accessibility and understanding;
(f) Zones could foster exchanges among existing nuclear-weapon-free zones
and with nuclear‑weapon States with a view to identifying best practices and
developing shared understanding on negative security assurances, including to be
shared with new and prospective zones, while preserving the specific characteristics
and legal frameworks of each zone;
(g) Zones and nuclear‑weapon States could consider measures, such as
confidence‑building arrangements, information exchange and periodic consultations,
including explanations of nuclear policies and doctrines that may reinforce the
relevance and reliability of negative security assurances. In parallel, zones could take
into account experiences such as that of Mongolia, where non‑treaty‑based assurance
arrangements have contributed to confidence‑building, and consider how such
approaches may inform efforts to strengthen assurances in situations in which
treaty‑based mechanisms face constraints, including through political undertakings or
parallel declarations. In this context, continued consideration of this issue in relevant
multilateral forums could contribute to the exploration of options for effective and
legally binding approaches to negative security assurances.

peaceful uses of nuclear energy.

C. Future zones
1. Geographical zones and possible models
343. There is strong support for consideration of new nuclear‑weapon‑free zones,
given the role that such zones have and can play in keeping geographical regions free
of nuclear weapons and in strengthening disarmament and non‑proliferation, as well
as regional and international peace and security. The development of new zones would
also be consistent with article VII of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons, while potentially reinforcing that Treaty.
344. It has been argued that the current rise in strategic tensions is a reason why the
world could benefit from more nuclear-weapon-free zones. At the same time,
conversations are ongoing about the need for new or revitalized security architecture.
On both these issues, the development of new nuclear-weapon-free zones could
contribute to reducing nuclear risks in relevant regions and contributing to
confidence-building measures that reinforce regional security.
345. Any new zones should be developed in accordance with the relevant General
Assembly resolutions and the 1999 recommendations by the Disarmament
Commission and based on voluntary consensus agreements among States in the
relevant regions and the suitability and the specific needs of those regions, including
unique geographical makeup and political institutionalization. Any proposal for the
creation of a new zone in a specific region would need to be considered on its
individual merits and involve sustained regional diplomacy and engagement with
nuclear-weapon States, especially on possible protocols, as well as intergovernmental
organizations such as IAEA and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty
Organization.
346. Efforts, including through the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear
Weapons and the General Assembly, have rightly focused on the creation of a Middle
East zone free of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction. However,
26-11249 67/74
[[page 68]]
A/81/293
various studies, informal workshops and other processes have previously considered
the possibility of zones in Central Europe, the Arctic and North-East Asia.
347. It must be noted that the circumstances in which the regional models seen in
Latin American and Caribbean, Africa, Central Asia, South-East Asia and the South
Pacific emerged were specific to the context at the time of their establishment.
Moreover, notwithstanding common features, these zones are not identical, and there
is no generic template for their establishment.
348. Therefore, States considering the establishment of new zones may also consider
a range of complementary models such as the following:
(a) Subregional nuclear‑weapon‑free zones, which, where applicable, pending
broader regional agreements, could be envisaged, allowing for a smaller group of
neighbouring States to advance non-proliferation and nuclear disarmament objectives
tailored to their specific security context. Early proposals for a European
nuclear‑weapon‑free zone, such as the Rapacki Plan,30 illustrate how subregional
arrangements can be designed to address localized risks even when broader
continental consensus is lacking;
(b) Non‑contiguous States could also consider collaborating to establish
nuclear‑weapon‑free arrangements even when they do not share geographical borders,
creating flexible models that accommodate political or strategic realities. Such
proposals share commonalities with subregional models but allow for States to
collaborate even when they do not share borders;
(c) Political commitments could also serve as possible options or interim
measures to traditional nuclear‑weapon‑free zones, allowing groups of States to
advance non‑nuclear objectives without the need for a formal treaty-based zone. Such
commitments could take a variety of forms that allow States to reinforce nuclear
restraint outside a legally codified nuclear‑weapon‑free zone. These could include
political arrangements in which the non-nuclear‑weapon States make a political
commitment not to deploy or station nuclear weapons on their territories and
nuclear‑weapon States politically pledge not to use or threaten to use nuclear weapons
against these non-nuclear‑weapon States. Political commitments offer adaptable
pathways for countries to advance disarmament and non‑proliferation goals even
when regional and strategic conditions do not permit the immediate establishment of
a traditional nuclear‑weapon‑free zone.
349. Noting that the creation of new zones remains the prerogative of the States
concerned and that this issue requires further research and consultation, and having
considered the elements raised herein, the following options and recommendations
are presented regarding alternative zone models and possible new zones:
(a) All States continue to recognize the benefits of nuclear-weapon-free zones
to promote disarmament and non-proliferation, reduce tensions and build lasting
security benefits for their members;
(b) All States, through General Assembly resolutions, the Treaty on the
Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons review cycle and other relevant forums,
continue to support the right to establish new nuclear-weapon-free zones, consistent
with relevant Assembly resolutions, the 1999 Disarmament Commission guidelines
and article VII of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons;

2. Single States with nuclear-weapon-free status
350. The five existing nuclear-weapon-free zones were established through regional
treaties concluded by groups of States. Mongolia represents a distinct case as the only
existing example of a State that has declared nuclear-weapon-free status at the
national level and obtained sustained international recognition and political support
for that status.
351. As stated above, in the section on the establishment and expansion of nuclear-
weapon-free zones, Mongolia declared its territory nuclear weapon-free in 1992. In
2000, it adopted legislation defining and regulating the status. The legislation
prohibits, among others, the development, acquisition, possession, stationing,
transport, testing or use of nuclear weapons, as well as the transport of nuclear
weapons and their parts or components through its territory. It applies to individuals,
legal persons and foreign States on the territory of Mongolia and is supported by
national implementation measures.
352. The General Assembly has supported Mongolia’s nuclear-weapon-free status
since 1998 and has continued to consider the matter biennially. In 2000, the five
nuclear-weapon States issued a joint statement providing political security assurances
to Mongolia. In 2012, Mongolia and the five nuclear-weapon States issued parallel
declarations. The five nuclear-weapon States reaffirmed their earlier assurances and
expressed their intent, for as long as Mongolia maintained its nuclear-weapon-free
status, to respect that status and not to contribute to any act that would violate it.
353. Mongolia’s status nevertheless differs in legal and institutional form from the
five regional nuclear-weapon-free zones. It is based on a unilateral declaration,
national legislation and implementing measures, General Assembly support and
political assurances from the nuclear-weapon States. It was not established through a
multilateral treaty, does not form part of a treaty organization and does not benefit
from legally binding, protocol-based negative security assurances of the kind
associated with regional nuclear-weapon-free zones.
354. The experience of Mongolia illustrates a possible model for States that wish to
contribute to nuclear disarmament and non-proliferation by declaring themselves
nuclear weapons-free but are unable to participate in an existing nuclear-weapon-free
zone or for which the establishment of a wider regional zone is not currently feasible.
Such a model could combine a nationally declared status with comprehensive
domestic prohibitions, nuclear safeguards and other implementation measures,
international recognition and assurances provided by relevant nuclear-weapon States.
It could complement the regional treaty-based approach, noting that such an approach
remains the benchmark for nuclear-weapon-free zones.
355. However, there exists no generally agreed international framework or guidance
for establishing or recognizing single-State nuclear-weapon-free status. Article VII of
the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and the definition contained
in General Assembly resolution 3472 B (XXX) envisage zones created by groups of
26-11249 69/74
[[page 70]]
A/81/293
regional States. A single-State arrangement may also lack the element of reciprocal
obligations among neighbouring States and may not provide a standing mechanism
for consultation, reporting, interpretation or consideration of alleged violations.
356. Given this context, several key issues will be central to any possible single-State
model.
357. The first is the benefits derived from a declaration of nuclear-weapon-free status
in addition to, among others, membership of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of
Nuclear Weapons. The contribution made by the declaration of nuclear-weapon-free
status to regional and international security is also important. For example, States
making such declarations in regions of high nuclear risk, where no regional model is
in prospect, could be an important confidence-building and risk reduction measure.
358. Second, there is the nature of negative security assurances. Political assurances,
such as those provided to Mongolia, may offer recognition and reassurance but do not
have the same legal effect as obligations undertaken through ratified protocols to
regional nuclear-weapon-free zone treaties. Future single-State arrangements would
need to consider the scope and legal character of any assurances, the conditions
governing their application and the procedures for consultation in the event of an
alleged violation.
359. Third, verification and implementation would also require specific attention.
IAEA safeguards may provide assurances concerning the peaceful use of nuclear
material but do not cover all possible elements of a single-State arrangement. Possible
future arrangements would therefore need to define their substantive scope clearly
and establish appropriate arrangements for verification, national reporting,
information exchange and international consultation.
360. In the light of the foregoing, the following options and recommendations are
presented regarding single States with nuclear-weapon-free status:
(a) The characteristics of a possible single nuclear-weapon-free State model
warrant further exploration and evaluation. Further research could be undertaken by
interested States Parties, think tanks, research bodies or non-governmental
organizations and could focus on the role of single States in strengthening the
nuclear‑weapon‑free zone regime; better defining the roles and functions of such
States, the nuclear-weapon States and other stakeholders, such as intergovernmental
organizations; and the content of declarations and possible verification/monitoring
measures. Further research could propose guidance on what a single-State model, or
models, could look like;
(b) The Office for Disarmament Affairs, drawing on Mongolia’s experience,
could conduct outreach to States seeking to explore the possibility of implementing a
status such as that of Mongolia. The Office could seek the views of interested States
on their security concerns and how a nuclear-weapon-free declaration might mitigate
those concerns;
(c) Events such as conferences of Parties to treaties establishing nuclear-
weapon-free zones or informal workshops such as those held in Astana could include
dedicated time for discussion on the issue of single-State nuclear weapon-free
declarations;
(d) The nuclear-weapon States could agree to hold a consultative meeting to
consider what kind of assurances they could provide to single States with nuclear
weapon-free statuses and what elements of any possible declarations would make
support difficult;

V. Conclusion
361. Nuclear-weapon-free zones have become an important and enduring
contribution to nuclear disarmament and non-proliferation, and to regional and
international peace and security. Their evolving development through concerted and
innovative efforts over the past six decades has demonstrated both the flexibility of
the concept and its capacity to respond to diverse global and regional challenges. At
the same time, the international security environment has undergone profound
changes. Their continued effectiveness and sustainability require political
commitment, effective implementation of relevant commitments and obligations,
constructive dialogue among States – both Parties to the zones and nuclear-weapon
States – and adequate institutional and technical capacities. Drawing upon the
experience accumulated by existing zones and Mongolia, including efforts to
strengthen cooperation among them, the options and recommendations identified in
the present study may assist both in strengthening these zones and in advancing the
possible establishment of nuclear-weapon-free zones in other regions, especially in
the Middle East, while considering the diversity of regional circumstances.
