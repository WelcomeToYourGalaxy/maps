Summary
The reporting period (October 2025–July 2026) began amid significant political
friction between members of the Transitional Presidential Council and the Prime
Minister, Alix Didier Fils-Aimé, ahead of the end of the mandate of the Transitional
Presidential Council on 7 February. On that day, executive power was ultimately
transferred to the Council of Ministers, led by the Prime Minister. On 23 February, a
National Pact for Stability and the Organization of Elections was adopted by a range
of political, civil society and economic actors. However, as of mid-July,
implementation of the Pact – particularly the security objectives – remained slow,
further eroding domestic confidence in the transition. Although some progress was
recorded in electoral preparations, the electoral calendar remained uncertain.
Since December 2025, security operations by the Haitian authorities have
contributed to halting the expansion of gangs in Port-au-Prince and cleared some
areas from gang control, including in downtown, Delmas and surrounding areas. The
absence of a national security strategy, limited resources and persistent ties between
gangs and key stakeholders continued to constrain the overall security response.
The scale of the security challenges, combined with the limited capacity of
national institutions, resulted in a fragmented security response with a proliferation
of armed actors. On the one hand, the Government’s security approach is relying
heavily on international actors, including a foreign private military contractor and the
Gang Suppression Force, which started its operations in June. On the other hand, the
population is increasingly turning to self-defence groups, private security companies
and the Protected Areas Security Brigade to fill the security vacuum. Although self-
defence groups represent the main buffer against gang attacks in many areas, some
engaged in criminal activities and human rights violations and risk perpetuating the
country’s cycle of violence by contributing to the next generation of gangs.
After holding together for more than two years, there were violent fractures in
the Viv Ansanm gang alliance during the reporting period, driven by competition over
territory and revenue. Infighting in Port-au-Prince, particularly in Cité Soleil and
Croix-des-Bouquets communes between March and July, killed several hundred
people and displaced thousands. During clashes, incidents of sexual violence surged
as a tool of retaliation between rival gangs.
March 2026 also marked the onset of a new wave of violence in the lower
Artibonite Department, with coordinated attacks of an unprecedented intensity by the
Gran Grif, Kokorat San Ras and Canaan gangs across dozens of localities. In the
Centre Department, the 400 Mawozo and Canaan gangs continued to consolidate their
control over several towns and to secure key routes to the border with the Dominican
Republic, through which they traffic goods, including arms and ammunition, and
generate significant revenue from road checkpoints. In the South-East Department,
recent gang attacks marked a concerning new development in what had previously
been a relatively safe region.

Despite intensified security operations by the Haitian authorities supported by a
private military contractor, gangs have demonstrated considerable resilience and
adaptability, particularly owing to the support of the ecosystem of actors who have a
stake in gang activities. This includes specific political, economic and security actors
who back gangs when it serves their interests, as well as the intermediaries they use.
Targeting gang leaders without addressing the networks that sustain them will
inevitably fall short. Sanctioning these enabling actors remains key to the
effectiveness of the United Nations sanctions regime.
Between October 2025 and April 2026, 3,165 people were killed and 1,551
injured as a result of armed violence, with 65% of casualties occurring during security
operations and 30% during gang attacks. Gangs led by designated individuals
continued to commit serious human rights abuses, among them killings, sexual and
gender-based violence, kidnapping for ransom, forced recruitment of children,
destruction of property and extortion. Beyond sanctioned individuals, many other
gang leaders continued to engage in actions that meet designation criteria. Sexual
violence has also escalated sharply since early 2026, with the collective rape of
women and girls by armed men constituting one of its most severe manifestations.
Gang violence impeded access to humanitarian assistance and basic services,
including education, and led to the closure of several medical facilities and charitable
organizations.
Successive waves of intense armed violence reflect the continued ability of
gangs and other non-State armed actors to illicitly acquire firearms and, more
critically, ammunition. While authorities in Haiti, the Dominican Republic and the
United States of America have taken steps to address trafficking and enforce the arms
embargo, these efforts remain insufficient given the scale of the challenge.
Transnational networks, often displaying unsophisticated methods, have proven to be
resilient and efficient in trafficking firearms and ammunition during the reporting
period. Diversions from national stockpiles – from Haiti and, to a lesser extent, from
the Dominican Republic – continue to replenish the arsenals of gangs and other
non-State armed actors.
Insecurity and the absence of effective governance have created conditions
conducive to the growth of illicit trafficking and transnational organized crime more
broadly. Beyond arms, the Panel investigated record seizures of cocaine and cannabis
during the reporting period, with networks using Haiti and its territorial waters as
corridors to transport narcotics northward through the Caribbean and beyond. The
trade in elvers also raised significant concerns, with a poorly regulated and opaque
commercial sector remaining highly exposed to illicit activity, exploited by one
specific network identified by the Panel.

I. Introduction
A. Mandate
1. On 17 October 2025, the Security Council adopted resolution 2794 (2025), in
which it renewed the sanctions regime on Haiti, consisting of an arms embargo as
well as a travel ban and an asset freeze against individuals and/or entities designated
by the Security Council Committee established pursuant to resolution 2653 (2022)
concerning Haiti as being responsible for, complicit in or having engaged in, directly
or indirectly, actions that threaten the peace, security and stability of Haiti. Through
resolution 2794 (2025), the Council also designated two additional individuals:
Dimitri Hérard (HTi.008), the former head of the National Palace General Security
Unit, for his support to gangs, and Kempes Sanon (HTi.009), the leader of Les
Argentins gang. Currently, the sanctions list includes the names of nine individuals
and two entities.1
2. The Council extended, for a period of 13 months from the date of adoption of
resolution 2794 (2025), the mandate of the Panel of Experts on Haiti, as specified in
paragraph 21 of resolution 2653 (2022), and further decided that this mandate shall
also apply with respect to the measures imposed in resolution 2794 (2025).
3. This report is submitted pursuant to paragraph 13 of resolution 2794 (2025) and
covers investigations conducted up to 10 July 2026.
B. Methodology
4. The Panel conducts its investigations in a professional and technical manner,
adhering to the principles of transparency, objectivity, impartiality and independence.
The Panel’s working methods are in full conformity with the best practices, as
recommended by the Informal Working Group on General Security Council Sanctions
Issues (see S/2006/997). The Panel placed particular emphasis on transparency and
the use of reliable sources, including documentary evidence, independent verifiable
sources and the opportunity to reply (see annex 1). The Panel takes great care not to
disclose identifying information, when necessary, in order to protect sources given
the extreme levels of insecurity in Haiti.
5. In October 2025, February 2026, and May 2026, the Panel conducted fact-
finding missions in Haiti (Port-au-Prince and Cap-Haitien), in Santo Domingo, and
in Miami, United States of America. The Panel is grateful for the support it received
from the respective national authorities, as well as from the United Nations Integrated
Office in Haiti (BINUH), the United Nations Support Office in Haiti, the United
Nations country team and non-governmental actors on the ground.
6. During its visits to Haiti, the Panel met with representatives of Haitian State
institutions, including the Prime Minister, the Minister of the Interior and Territorial
Communities, the Minister of Justice, the Minister of Defence and the Minister of
Economy and Finance. It also engaged with the leadership of the Haitian National
Police and the customs, financial institutions and anti-corruption units, political and
private sector actors, as well as representatives of civil society organizations,
individuals living in gang-controlled areas, and other victims of gang-related
violence, including sexual and gender-based violence. The Panel also met with the
Special Representative of the Secretary-General for Haiti and Head of BINUH, and
other representatives of BINUH, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime

C. Implementation of the sanctions regime
1. Arms embargo
8. The arms embargo continues to be violated on a regular basis. While authorities
in Haiti, the Dominican Republic and the United States of America have taken steps
to prevent trafficking and prosecute traffickers, they remain largely insufficient given
the scale of the challenge. Precise estimates of flows remain difficult to establish, but
several indicators point to sustained violations. Gangs have maintained their capacity
to fight and replenish their stocks of arms and ammunition (see sect. II.C). Haitian
authorities intercepted several illicit shipments at ports of entry during the reporting
period, having seized none in the period covered in the previous final report
(S/2025/597, para. 17). United States authorities intercepted firearms and ammunition
destined for Haiti and are investigating and prosecuting a range of trafficking
networks.
9. Haitian authorities have cooperated with the Panel and shared information on
cases of transnational trafficking. Regarding sources and transit countries, at the time
of drafting, the United States was yet to respond to the Panel’s tracing requests but
provided information about ongoing judicial cases. The Dominican Republic
continued to offer positive support to the Panel’s investigations. Jamaica responded
to the Panel’s request for information and The Bahamas requested an extension. The
Turks and Caicos Islands, through the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern
Ireland, had not responded at the time of drafting.
2. Asset freeze
10. The Panel notes that very little progress (since that documented in S/2025/597,
paras. 13–16) has been made regarding the freezing of assets for individuals under
United Nations sanctions. The Panel is concerned about the absence of a national
coordinated process to identify and freeze assets of individuals sanctioned by the
United Nations, which erodes the impact of the sanctions regime in Haiti (see
annex 3).
11. The Panel sent written communication to the Bank of the Republic of Haiti and
some commercial banks for an update and was informed that only accounts belonging
to Kempes Sanon (HTi.009) and to Prophane Victor (HTi.006) (see also S/2025/356,
para. 9), both with very meagre amounts, had been frozen (see annex 3). The limited
amount of funds identified and frozen reflects the delayed action of financial
institutions following designations, resulting in sanctioned individuals transferring
their assets or concealing them under the names of third parties.
12. Haitian commercial banks also stated that they had frozen the bank accounts of
several gang leaders following the imposition of either United Nations sanctions or
unilateral sanctions on gang leaders and Viv Ansanm (HTe.002). Banks added that
they had also blacklisted anyone they identified as being associated with or supporting
gang operations.

3. Travel ban
14. The Panel did not identify nor did receive any information on attempts to violate
the travel ban during the reporting period. However, the Panel is concerned by
persistent fraud in the issuance of Haitian passports in the country.2 According to
Haitian officials, passports are issued in excessive numbers, often with falsified fiscal
stamps, thereby enabling the embezzlement of fees and bribery from applicants
seeking genuine documents for illicit purposes. Some passports have been issued to
foreign nationals and/or under fake names. These shortcomings increase the risk of
sanctioned individuals obtaining multiple passports and travelling under false
identities to circumvent the travel ban.
D. Evolution of the political and security context
1. Political transition
15. The current mandate of the Panel began during a politically tense transition
period marked by significant friction between members of the Transitional
Presidential Council and the Prime Minister, Alix Didier Fils-Aimé, in the run-up to
7 February 2026, which marked the end of the mandate of the Transitional
Presidential Council (S/2026/241, sect. I.C). Ultimately, full executive power was
transferred to the Council of Ministers led by the Prime Minister, with strong support
from international partners.
16. On 23 February 2026, a National Pact for Stability and the Organization of
Elections was adopted and signed by a range of political, civil society and economic
actors, with the aim of creating the security and governance conditions necessary for
elections. Subsequently, a reshuffled governmental Cabinet was installed in March.
However, five months after its adoption, the implementation of the Pact – particularly
regarding the provisions on the restoration of security – remains very slow, further
weakening domestic confidence in the transition.
17. Some progress has been recorded in electoral preparations, including the
registration of political parties, the adoption of an electoral decree (see para. 18) and
an agreement over the electoral budget. Although the authorities announced an
updated electoral calendar with the first round scheduled for December, political
observers who met with the Panel noted that elections were unlikely to be held in
2026.

2. Security situation
20. After years of instability and given the absence of effective governing
structures, gangs have continued to fill the governance void in parts of the West,
Artibonite and Centre Departments, while trying to extend their influence in other
departments. The intensification of security operations by the Haitian authorities has
resulted in the recovery of some areas from gang control in the capital. However,
gains remain fragile and national roads connecting the capital to the rest of the country
remain under gang control (see sect. II.A.1). The situation in the lower Artibonite also
worsened considerably, as increasingly powerful gangs carried out attacks of
exceptional brutality (see sect. II.A.4).
21. Beyond the impact of security operations, which have resulted in significant
civilian casualties, the population has also suffered severe consequences of gang
attacks and infighting, particularly in the capital and in the Artibonite Department.
After holding together for more than two years, there were violent fractures in the Viv
Ansanm gang alliance during the reporting period (see sect. II.A.2).
22. The scale of security challenges, combined with the limited capacity of national
institutions, has resulted in a fragmented security response with a proliferation of
armed actors. On the one hand, the Government’s security approach is relying heavily
on international actors, including a private security contractor and the Gang
Suppression Force. On the other hand, the population is increasingly turning to self-
defence groups, Haitian private security companies and the Protected Areas Security
Brigade (S/2024/704, annex 26) to fill the security vacuum. For some of these actors,
the unstable security situation has become a business opportunity.
23. While some of these security actors operate within a recognized legal
framework, others function outside any formal oversight mechanism, with the result
that the absence of accountability contributes to impunity and fails to prevent further
human rights violations (see sect. II.B).
(a) National security and defence forces
24. The absence of a national security strategy hampers a comprehensive approach
to fighting gangs, the prioritization of objectives and the management of resources.
25. Although security operations appear more coordinated and bolstered by
increased logistical capacities (S/2026/241, para. 17), the Haitian National Police
continues to face the same challenges previously reported by the Panel: an insufficient
number of personnel, limited equipment, human rights abuses and corruption issues
(see annex 5).
26. The reporting period was marked by a shift in the approach to the national armed
forces. The Government committed to rebuilding the Haitian Armed Forces,
following years of limited institutional support since their remobilization in 2017.
Concrete steps being taken include recruitment, procurement of equipment, training
programmes and the project to develop a new military doctrine.4 Despite slow
progress, the Haitian Armed Forces have become more involved in security
operations in support of the Haitian National Police.

(b) International private contractors
27. The Government has recently extended by a year the contract of the international
private military company in charge of supporting security operations through
helicopter support, drone strikes and ground engagement (S/2025/597, para. 28).5 The
task force, of which the contractors are part, has expanded its area of operations to
the lower Artibonite since April 2026.
28. The reporting period also saw the privatization of security support extending
beyond the fight against gangs to border control. The Government signed a 10-year
contract with an international company for border security reinforcement and customs
revenue optimization.6 The company is remunerated through a percentage of Haitian
customs revenue, which is the country’s second largest source of revenue. The deal
has drawn criticism from many Haitian stakeholders, raising concerns about national
sovereignty and the substantial revenues the company stands to collect during the
contract. Notably, this company and the one mentioned in the paragraph above share
the same leadership.
(c) Transition from Multinational Security Support Mission to Gang
Suppression Force
29. During the reporting period, the Multinational Security Support Mission
transitioned into the Gang Suppression Force and in April 2026, the Kenyan personnel
of the Multinational Security Support Mission (and subsequently, the Gang
Suppression Force) departed. While the participation of the Multinational Security
Support Mission in Haitian National Police operations and its presence in the lower
Artibonite had some deterrent effects on gang activity,7 the general security situation
continued to deteriorate, notwithstanding their presence in the country.
30. The new troops of the Gang Suppression Force began their gradual deployment
to Haiti in April 2026, conducting their first operational activities in Port-au-Prince
in June 2026. The Gang Suppression Force made its first arrests in mid-June and
transferred individuals to the Haitian National Police, which created challenges for
the police and the judicial system with respect to absorbing the likely high number of
upcoming cases. The Gang Suppression Force seized several firearms and
ammunition in July (see para. 117).
(d) Haitian private security companies
31. With more than 100 registered private security companies and many more
operating without a licence, the sector remains poorly regulated.8 Its governance is
further complicated by the involvement of former and current senior government
officials and officers of the Haitian National Police. The sector represents a major
source of employment for the country. Many companies procure arms and
ammunition illicitly, including of calibres in excess of domestic limits (see
S/2023/674, paras. 110–113, and S/2024/704, para. 41).

A. Gang dynamics
1. Gang dynamics amid intensified security operations
34. Since late 2025, security efforts by Haitian authorities have contributed to
reducing gang control in areas of the West Department (see annex 5). Estimations
suggest a decline in gangs’ territorial control to about 70% of the Port-au-Prince
metropolitan area, down from 85% (see S/2026/241) (see annex 6).9
35. Security gains remain fragile. The inability to contain gang leaders is widely
seen as a major setback for security operations. This can be partly explained by the
gangs’ integration into criminal networks that span government and economic sectors,
thereby constraining police operations and investigations. Despite the gangs’ apparent
autonomy, ties persist between gangs and State and private actors – particularly ahead
of elections, when territorial control and perceptions of stability are critical.
36. Political drivers have triggered violence in the commune of Kenscoff and the
Artibonite Department. They have also shaped gangs’ expansion efforts into southern
Grande-Anse Department, where Guy Philippe – a former senior police officer who
has pursued political ambitions and backed Viv Ansanm (S/2024/704, paras. 31, 52
and annex 6) – met repeatedly with Jean Misnord (a.k.a “Fado”), the third-in-
command of the Simon Pelé gang (see annex 7).10
37. Certain businessmen, as well as serving Haitian police officers, are exploiting
the unstable security situation, including road blockades (see annex 8), to generate
illicit revenues. Collusion between gangs (e.g., Tokyo, Simon Pelé and Ti Bwa) and
Haitian police officers – who supply weapons and enable gang movements – weakens
security operations, contributing to gang leaders evading capture.11 Gangs remain
structured and resilient, demonstrating adaptability in the face of increasing security

Department, the 400 Mawozo and Canaan gangs continue to hold sway over strategic
towns (S/2025/597, sect. II.A.3) and a former senator has used armed groups to

gangs – some linked with Viv Ansanm – and certain agents of the Protected Areas

proliferated, often supporting the police and the Protected Areas Security Brigade.

prevention within an institutional framework (see annex 11). However, many
members are involved in criminal activities, remain illegally armed and funded and
risk perpetuating cycles of violence by contributing to the next generation of gangs.
2. Fractures within Viv Ansanm alliance
41. Signs of Viv Ansanm fracturing appeared during clashes between gangs in the
Cul-de-Sac plain area (see annex 12), and in the Cité Soleil commune of the West
Department, during which 613 people were killed (including 214 individuals not
associated with gangs) and 375 others were injured (including 139 individuals not
associated with gangs), between 6 March and 31 July 2026.16
42. Clashes in the Cul-de-Sac Plain were motivated by competition over territory
and revenue control. Despite some shifts, gangs were divided into two camps. Gangs

64. Between October 2025 and April 2026, 3,165 people were killed and 1,551

(a) Evolution of calibres and types of firearms
94. Since its establishment in October 2022, the Panel has noted a clear shift in the

Annex 1: Methodology and Opportunity to Reply
The Panel adopted a decision rule of consensus, meaning that all members of the Panel were required to agree on the text,
findings and recommendations of the report. However, if any member of the Panel had a difference of opinion or reservation,
the report could still be adopted by a majority vote of three out of four members.
The Panel reviewed social media and other open sources, but no information was used as evidence unless it could be
corroborated by multiple independent sources. This was done to ensure that the information was reliable and met the highest
achievable standard of proof.
The Panel provides individuals and entities recommended for sanctioning the opportunity to reply. This gives individuals
or entities the opportunity to present an alternative narrative and to provide concrete and specific evidence in their support.
The Panel's methodology for the opportunity to reply is as follows:
1. Providing an individual or entity with an opportunity to reply should be the norm.
2. An individual or entity may not be given the opportunity to reply if the Panel concludes, based on their own assessment,
that there is a risk that by doing so it would:
a) Result in the individual or entity destroying evidence, tampering with a witness or moving assets if they receive
advance notice of a possible recommendation for designation;
b) Restrict the Panel's further access to places or vital sources;
c) Endanger Panel sources or Panel members;
d) Jeopardise humanitarian access for humanitarian actors in the field including the United Nations or any other
local or international organizations; or
e) For any reason that is clearly justified by the circumstances.
After an individual or entity is designated by the Security Council or the Sanctions Committee, it is possible to request a
de-listing process through the mechanism established by the Sanctions Committee.150

Annex 7 – Gang presence in southern Haiti amid security operations
Southwestern outskirts of the capital as a growing hub for gangs
Drone strikes and police operations in areas stretching from downtown Port-au-Prince - including Marché Salomon and the
Bicentenaire - up to Rue Saint-Honoré have driven gangs to the southwestern outskirts of Port-au-Prince.
While the police dismantled many checkpoints in downtown, gangs set up other illegal tolls in the streets of Boulosse
neighborhood (Port-au-Prince commune), in the southern zone of the capital. At least five checkpoints are held by gang
leaders including Izo, and Emmanuel Salomon, a.k.a “Manno” (5 Segond), and Bougoy, Kilik and Ti Lapli (Grand
Ravine)164. Christ-Roi Chery (a.k.a “Chrisla”), head of Ti Bwa has also established at least three checkpoints along the
alternative route connecting Carrefour to Gressier communes, in southwestern edge of the capital. Since movement along
the RN2 - linking Port-au-Prince to the South - has been severely disrupted by insecurity, residents are subject to a 3–6-
hour mountainous journey, in which motorcycle riders pay $250 HTG at each toll belonging to Chrisla165. Of note, several
economic actors benefit from the blocking on the RN2 (see Annex 8).
On another note, Carrefour commune continues to be a haven for Viv Ansanm and acolytes such as Dimitri Herard (HTi.008),
where they move freely within the community. Despite the absence of the police, schools, banks, and markets remain
operational. Behind the “apparent normalcy” in the commune lies a parallel system of gang governance which replaced
public authority (see Annex 5 of S/2025/597). For instance, Chrisla and other gang leaders rely on the commercial bank
agencies in Carrefour for transactions linked to kidnappings, extortion, and investment activities (see section II.E.A.1.a)166.
When entering banks, gang members sometimes leave their firearms at the security entrance and proceed inside to access
services167. On 11 May 2026, Chrisla renewed his calls on the government to appoint a close associate of his for a senior
public position in the commune, while threatening to paralyze municipal institutions if his demand was not met168. Chrisla’s
demand reflects further efforts to entrench influence within formal governance structures and secure political leverage.
Attempts of gang expansion to southern departments
Attacks from 13 April 2026 in the South-East department, in the localities of Kajak (3rd section Macary) and Seguin (1st
section Fond Jean Noël), in Marigot commune, illustrate the expansion of gang violence beyond traditional gang
strongholds. This also marks one of the first major security crises in a region previously considered relatively safe. Viv
Ansanm gangs carried out coordinated assaults, killing at least five residents and eight members of a self-defense group,
burning the Seguin police substation, destroying two police vehicles and seizing police motorcycles and uniforms169 .
Reinforced by gang members coming from Kenscoff, Viv Ansanm renewed clashes on 20 April 2026, after security
operations on 16 April 2026. Violence triggered the displacement of approximately 5,656 individuals170.
Attacks over Seguin marked a significant development. The area sits in a strategic high-altitude area, overlooking key
corridors linking the coastal city of Jacmel (with cultural and economic significance), the mountainous and agricultural
areas of La Vallée and Kenscoff, and Port-au-Prince. In addition, control over Seguin enables and facilitates drug and arms
trafficking activities, through the corridor of Carrefour-Kenscoff-Seguin-Marigot and use of the port of Marigot and the
border cross-point of Anse-à-Pitres171. It is not surprising, therefore, that links between Chrisla (who controls Carrefour
commune), and Izo (involved in drug trafficking) have grown closer. The Panel previously visited Jacmel, as well as the
Dominican town of Pedernales located directly opposite Anse-à-Pitres in Haiti and witnessed the high vulnerability of the
region to drug trafficking (see section 2.D of S/2025/597).
With the takeover of southern regions, Viv Ansanm would effectively complete a de facto encirclement of the capital, given

Annex 8 - Insecurity as a driver of economic interests: the RN2 blockade
For over two years, movement along the National Road #2 (RN2), linking the Port-au-Prince metropolitan area and the
southern region - including the departments of Nippes, South, South-East and Grand’Anse - has been completely cut off by
walls erected along the RN2 and by gang violence.
In November 2023, the Mariani district of the Carrefour commune, lying on the main corridor connecting Port-au-Prince to
the southern region of Haiti, fell under the control of the Grand Ravine “Team Asansè” (also known as Team Ascenseur),
led by Beliose Louis Jeune (a.k.a “Bout Ba”). This has hampered the movement of people, goods and services along the
RN2 (see section II.A.2 and annex 8 of S/2024/253).
In May 2024, the Grand Ravine gang extended into the neighbouring Gressier, located to the south of Mariani, which has
been under the control of the 103 Zombie group of the Grand Ravine gang, led by Charlemagne Dorin (a.k.a “Ti Bebe
Bougòy”) (see S/2024/704).
The reopening of the RN2 has been one of the main concerns of the new municipal authorities of the Gressier commune.
Efforts by the municipality and the local population resulted in the destruction of the walls impeding movement, on 19 May
2026. On 29 May 2026, the police station in Gressier reopened after being closed for two years, although it has relocated
to another area176. However, as of the time of drafting, no police operation has been effectively carried out to enable traffic
to resume along the RN2. In fact, the disruption on the RN2 has been tied to major interests, benefiting for instance, business
operators handling private flights and barges and controlling ports177. Insecurity creates economic incentives that benefit
many.
Air and sea routes have been used to bypass gang-controlled areas and roadblocks thereby benefitting air and maritime
operators. By sea, this route connects the National Port Authority (APN) terminal in Port-au-Prince to coastal access points
such as Petit-Goâve, Grand-Goâve, and Léogâne, located in the southern edge of the capital. Private barge owners charge
between $ 2,500 - $5,000 USD per container, depending on the value of the cargo being transported for one-way trip
between the APN port, in Port-au-Prince, and southwestern communes of Petit-Goâve and Miragoâne178. At the beginning
of 2025, someone paid about $ 300,000 HTG (approximately $ 2,300 USD) to travel one-way from APN to the southern
region179. A number of police officers ensure the security of the about 8 barges plying this route and reportedly receive
substantial financial compensation from barge owners. Private actors also reportedly pay for police security around the
APN zone180.
During the Panel visit to Jacmel, South-East department, in June 2025, civil society representatives informed the Panel that
operators of air taxi services used to charge $1,000 USD per flight connecting Jacmel to Port-au-Prince in 2023, which
could carry up to three passengers only. If there was one passenger, they paid the price of three, and if there were multiple
passengers, the cost was shared among them.
Sources disclosed to the Panel how they received pressure from business figures to slow down their efforts for the
unblocking of RN2, as well as for the security of the APN port area181.
The Panel will continue its investigations into the involvement of private actors in the instability linked to gang control of
roads and port areas.

Annex 9 – Key challenges hindering effective security operations
1. Collusion between gangs and corrupt police officers
Police investigations launched in February 2026 revealed links between at least six police officers and the Tokyo gang’s
leader, Ralph Woodmyr Louissant (a.k.a “Chalè”), including a police officer called Charlemagne Volmar (a.k.a “Titit” or
“Steeve”).
The criminal network was involved in various criminal acts, notably kidnapping, murder, attempted murder, armed robbery,
and criminal conspiracy. Chalè’s gang was for instance responsible for the kidnapping of several individuals. On 11 March
2025, the gang kidnapped two individuals in Delmas 31 (released after paying ransoms of $ 400,000 HTG and 465,000
HTG each (approximately $3,100 USD to $3,600 USD), and one in Cité Militaire, released after paying $ 2,000,000 HTG
(approximately $15,000 USD). Other kidnappings were reported on 4 April 2025, along the airport road, and on 7 July
2025, where two individuals were kidnapped in Delmas 33; where victims were also released after paying ransoms. The
gang also robbed the victims’ vehicles182.
Charlemagne Volmar was in charge of picking up Chalès’ girlfriend, Wideline Louis (a.k.a “Wide”) at the airport after her
return from the Dominican Republic. Based on a confidential report, Chalè would always call Charlemagne after his gang
members had carried out a kidnapping to inform him of any potential loss of life. Charlemagne’s role also consisted of
identifying HNP checkpoints, thereby enabling gang members to carry out kidnappings without being intercepted by the
police. Charlemagne Volmar also undertook other duties, such as verifying the financial status of certain individuals prior
to their abduction. He was also responsible for transporting large sums of money intended for the purchase of ammunition
on behalf of the gang. He also identified another police officer known as Poupy, who sold an AR-15 rifle to Chalè183.
Another police officer, Friendy Jean-Baptiste, a.k.a "Ti Solèy," also member of the Tokyo gang, was responsible for
supplying the gang with firearms and ammunition. His function as police officer and ties to all Viv Ansanm members enabled
him to become a key supplier of firearms and ammunition. He also informed Chalè about all police operations targeting the
city center, particularly the Tokyo neighborhood and the Saint-Martin Street184.
In another case, on 13 January 2026, the police arrested Jean Misnord (a.k.a “Fado”), the third chief of Simon Pelé gang,
led by Stevenson Albert (a.k.a “Djouma”). Fado disclosed that two police officers that escaped prison with Djouma are part
of the gang, that includes over 180 individuals armed with fusils185.
In October 2023, a police officer named Cleevens Degazon (a.k.a “My Man”) was arrested for his association with the Ti
Bwa gang, led by Christ-Roi Chery ( a.k.a “Chrisla”). Despite being dismissed from the police in August 2023, My Man -
who was also associated with the 5 Segond and Grand Ravine gangs - was colluding with Ryvelor Domond, an active officer
of the judicial police. The latter was in frequent communication with Chrisla and had contacts with influential Ti Bwa
members, including other police officers by the names of Sony Caidor (a.k.a “Kzo”), Shadbir Rooster Joseph (a.k.a “Peter”
or “POG”), Gaspard Henold, and Joseph Sonson186.
2. Gangs’ resilient structures and adaptive responses to ongoing security pressure

Annex 10 - Gang reorganisation in unstable post-operational settings: the case of Bel-Air
Intensified security operations notably undertaken in early 2026, initially displaced gangs from their areas (see paragraph
17 of S/2026/241). However, unstable post-operational scenario led to a security vacuum that was progressively filled by a
diffuse, mobile and opportunistic presence of gangs, renewing instability193. The current situation in Bel-Air, following
security efforts in early 2026, provides a good example.
Bel-Air is a strategic area for gangs. It also offers a fallback zone and a strategic corridor, facilitating regrouping and
logistical support for criminal activities, notably kidnapping. Key actors in Bel-Air highlighted gang dynamics such as
territorial consolidation and the re-emergence of violent hotspots across key sectors, after security operations. After the
MSS base relocated out of Bel-Air, no security mechanism prevented gangs of returning to the area. Police patrols were
mainly concentrated in securing Port-au-Prince downtown area and state infrastructure rather than focusing on Bel Air
neighbourdhoods194.
Despite Les Argentins gang being nearly dismantled by Viv Ansanm (see Annex 3 of S/2026/241), its leader - Kempes Sanon
(a.k.a “Kempes”) - continues to operate with the Grand Ravine gang, led by Renel Destina (a.k.a “Ti Lapli” - Hti.003).
Kempes exercises criminal control over the Magloire Ambroise corridor, a key axis linking downtown to central and
southern neighborhoods, and shaping access to Bel-Air via Champ de Mars, in the city center; thus, particularly useful for
kidnapping activities.
As the result of security operations against 5 Segond stronghold, most of its members had to leave Bel-Air to fight. The
Gran Ravine gang has then exercised strongest hold in several areas of Bel-Air. Additionally, Jamesley, the gang leader of
Baz Demeplè maintains a presence in the area, despite renewed operations against his hideout in late May 2026195.
Additionally, residents have been rendered vulnerable to gang recruitment due to the fragmentation of their urban
settlements with lootings and destruction of buildings, which resulted into massive displacement of residents and the
breakdown of services. Likewise, residents are reluctant to return to their neighborhoods of origin. Gaps in community
violence reduction programmes - an issue that broadly affects the country – contributes to continued relapse of violence in
environments controlled by gangs.

Annex 12 – Viv Ansanm inter-fighting in Croix-des-Bouquets and Cité Soleil communes
Between 6 March and 3 June 2026, the conflict between two contending blocs of Viv Ansanm gangs caused heightened
tensions in several neighbourhoods of the communes of Croix-des-Bouquets and Cité Soleil of the West department. During
clashes, residents were caught in the crossfire, hit by bullets inside their homes, or targeted over suspected ties to gangs or
to the police. Gang members deliberately burned homes and other buildings, as well as vehicles. They also used sexual
violence - especially against women and girls - as punishment for living in areas controlled by rival groups. Public transport,
local businesses, and industrial activities came to a standstill196.Several factors triggered the clashes. Following early 2026
operations in lower Delmas (see Annex 2 of S/2026/241), the Tokyo gang aimed at relocating to Village de la Renaissance
(Cité Soleil commune) and re-establishing its operational capacity by setting up a checkpoint along a local road to generate
criminal income. Further, on 6 March 2026, after Chen Mechan members killed two Canaan members over Tokyo’s
relocation, gangs such as 400 Mawozo and Tokyo supported the Canaan gang to confront Chen Mechan, Pierre VI and Terre
Noire gangs197. From 6 to 11 March 2026, at least 74 people were killed, comprising 20 individuals with no reported links
to gangs. A further 76 people were injured, including 40 nonassociated with gangs198.
Chen Mechan then shifted alliance and joined the gangs of 400 Mawozo and Canaan to take control over the territories of
Pierre VI and Terre Noire. Another key driver of the clashes was the setup by the Pierre VI gang of an illegal tollbooth along
the Duvivier Road, to generate revenue from the extortion of drivers, which displeased Jeff, the leader of Canaan gang199.
During the first wave of gang violence, from 6 to 18 April, at least 111 women and girls were victims of rape and gang rape.
From 19 April to 9 May 2026, renewed gang clashes were driven by the Tokyo gang’s efforts to retain its presence in Village
de la Renaissance. Sexual violence was also reported in this new peak of violence, with at least 46 women subjected to rape,
including gang rape. A third wave of gang violence from 10 May to 3 June 2026 resulted in at least 147 people killed,
including 33 people with no reported links to gangs. Several additional casualties were reported200 . Intense violence
impacted commercial activities and humanitarian operations.
On 21 April 2026, the Barbancourt Rum company stated that part of its sugarcane plantations was destroyed by fire.

Context
The Cul-de-Sac Plain area, in the West department, encompasses several communes, including Croix-des-Bouquets,Cité
Soleil and Tabarre. It holds critical strategic importance within the Port-au-Prince metropolitan area, serving as a key
corridor linking the capital to other parts of the country. The Plain also hosts industrial zones, and densely populated
neighborhoods and lies near the international airport.
The area witnessed violence of such intensity not seen since at least late 2023208. At that time, Viv Ansanm was not yet
formed, and gangs integrated the rival alliances, G9 and G-Pèp. In September 2023, the death of G9-leader, Tysson
(Carrefour Drouillard gang) triggered clashes in Cité Soleil commune between two G9 camps (Boston, Belekou, Terre
Noire and Pierre VI against Chen Mechan and Carrefour Drouillard, supported by the G-Pèp-Brooklyn gang) (see Annex 4
of S/2024/253). Further, in November 2023, the death of Iskar Andrice (a.k.a “Iskar”), leader of the Belekou gang and co-
founder of G9 led to the resurgence of further territorial disputes in the area, between G9 and G-Pèp (see para. 28 and Annex
4 of S/2024/253).
While the activation of Viv Ansanm alliance in 2024 reduced inter-gang fighting over territorial expansion and shifted focus
toward attacks on state institutions and consolidation of strongholds (see section II.A of S/2024/704), sporadic clashes
continued among affiliated groups, underscoring persistent internal rivalries and competition over territory and illicit
revenue sources. For instance, clashes erupted in June 2025 between the gangs of 400 Mawozo, and Chen Mechan over a
checkpoint in Lilavois (Croix-des-Bouquets). Residents were killed in Santo 25 neighborhood, including some who were
inside homes that were set on fire by 400 Mawozo gang members209.
After a relative decrease in violence in Croix-des-Bouquets reportedly due to the relocation of many 400 Mawozo members
to the Centre and Artibonite departments 210, the beginning of March 2026 marked an escalation of violence in the area.
Following the destabilization of the Tokyo gang (led by Ralph Woodmyr Louissant, a.k.a “Chalè”) – which lost territory
after intensified police operations in early 2026 (see section II.A and Annex 2 of S/2026/241) - the gangs of Canaan and
400 Mawozo initially supported the establishment of the Tokyo gang in the area of Village de la Renaissance, in the Croix-
des-Bouquets commune. While Chen Mechan was initially aligned with Pierre VI and Terre Noire to oppose this move, the
gangs of Canaan and 400 Mawozo ultimately persuaded Chen Mechan to extend its control over areas held by Pierre VI
and Terre Noire gangs211.
Residents and local stakeholders deplored the absence of police in the area, despite the proximity of the conflicts to police
headquarters212. On 29 April 2026, a group of private companies whose facilities are located near the site of the attacks,
denounced critical conditions of the National Road #1 (RN1) and the Route 9, which hinders effective police operations.
Economic stakeholders - whose companies account for thousands of direct and indirect jobs - then urged Haitian authorities,
on 30 April 2026, to rehabilitate these strategic sections and the access to the international airport, as well as to reassert
control over the area, and protect the population. They emphasized the economic and social importance of their activities
and highlighted the risk of the area becoming a new ‘lost territory’, on the doorstep of the international airport and the HNP
headquarters213.
Several on-the-ground police operations - with the first taking place only on 28 March 2026 - and one Task Force drone
operation were launched. Further, on 5 June 2026, a truce was agreed amongst Viv Ansanm gangs including the reduction
of armed presence (removing checkpoints, watchtowers, and barricades), the specific control of zones and the end of
fighting. According to the agreement, the Fourgy area, in Cité Soleil, would remain a neutral and non-aggression zone,

Annex 13 – Sustained gang violence in Kenscoff217
More than a year and a half since the first large-scale attack against Kenscoff, on 27 January 2025 (see Annex 6 of
S/2025/597), insecurity persists in Kenscoff’s communal sections, even if some areas in the centre appear relatively calm.
Gangs continue to maintain a persistent presence in localities such as Nouvelle-Touraine, Bongars, Grand Fond, Belle
Fontaine, Belot, Furcy, Kafou Bèt, and Sourçailles. To makes matter worse, amid intensified security operations, gangs
continue to infiltrate the commune - particularly through a direct corridor from Carrefour commune to Kenscoff - to take
refuge in the most remote communal sections where access is extremely difficult and gangs have cut off roads 218.
According to sources, there has only been one major police operation in 2025 (see paragraph 47 of S/2025/597), and it
yielded no results. Since then, security interventions remain largely reactive, achieving limited tactical successes, and not
resulting in sustained territorial recovery. Gangs maintain their presence in rural and elevated out of reach areas, to move
freely and prepare attacks. While no permanent security presence remains to control secondary axes, gangs quickly use
them to reinsert themselves (see Annex 6 of S/2025/597).
Multiple calls have urged the national authorities to provide the police with assets such as helicopters, motorcycles, and
terrain-adapted vehicles, as well as sustained capabilities for territorial consolidation. Sources interviewed by the Panel call
for units capable of operating off-road, at high altitude and at night - as attacks systematically occur between midnight and
early morning - and in mountainous terrain.
The communal sections - the source of agriculture for the municipality - have been devastated by gangs. For instance,
Bongars was overrun by gangs on 21 January 2025, resulting in 279 people killed, 500 residences burnt and the displacement
of thousands of residents. As for the Nouvelle Touraine communal section, it is fully under gang control, that arrived on the
locality on 3 March 2025. The police and State are totally absent, and gangs have carried out massacres, looted everything,
destroyed livelihoods and 90% of the population has fled Nouvelle Touraine. Gangs have also controlled local markets
through an illegal arrangement whereby people have to give 50% of everything they sell to gangs. Livestock is valued at
approximately $ 4,000 HTG for goats, $ 6,000 HTG for pigs, and $ 80,000 USD for cattle, of which half must be surrendered
to gangs. In the case of vegetables, they sometimes seize the entire production219.
The Panel interviewed farmers that were driven out of their land by violence and have lost everything. Having depended on
agriculture for their survival, they now struggle in difficult conditions in other parts of the capital, as their entire way of life
was destroyed.
In early July 2026, coordinated attacks by Viv Ansanm triggered a new wave of violence in Kenscoff, from the night of 4 to
5 July at least until 8 July 2026, beginning in Téléco, Le Refuge, and Viard (Nouvelle Touraine communal section), before
spreading on 8 July to several other localities, including Robin, Mahotière, Dumisseau, Do Blanc, Bernard 1 and 2, Do Pak,
Kabamòn, and Dè Savann (Grand Fond communal section), with the escalation resulting in widespread destruction,
kidnapping and killings. According to the International Organization of Migration (IOM), approximately 5,840 people were
displaced as a result220. Sources on the ground described the attacks as one of the most violent ones in the reporting period,
leading to the death of at least 61 residents killed, although interviews with residents suggested a significantly higher death
toll. The municipal authorities have buried at least 43 bodies221.

Annex 15 - Coordinated attacks by Viv Ansanm across the lower Artibonite: over 100 residents killed229
Almost two years after the Pont-Sondé massacre (see paras. 36-37 of S/2025/356 and special report submitted in November
2024), Viv Ansanm launched a new wave of coordinated attacks in the lower Artibonite region of the Artibonite department.
Between 29 March and 3 April 2026, gangs of the Viv Ansanm alliance - Gran Grif (HTe.001), led by Luckson Elan
(HTi.007), Kokorat San Ras, led by Bendjy, and Canaan, headed by Jeff Larose - perpetrated a series of coordinated attacks
across at least 16 localities in the lower Artibonite region, notably in the communes of Petite Rivière de l’Artibonite,
Marchand Dessalines and Montrouis, resulting in at least 95 residents killed and 38 others injured230. However, the number
of people killed and injured is higher, as the gangs continued their attacks over successive days231. About 9,000 residents
were displaced between 28 March and 1 April 2026232.
The assaults occurred in the early hours of the day - between 02:00 and 08:00 - while residents were at home. Several
victims were shot dead after being forcibly taken from their beds or killed or injured while attempting to flee. Several houses
were burned233. In a single day - 29 March 2026 - at least 67 residents (38 men and 29 women) were killed, and 30 others
(17 men and 13 women) were injured during coordinated attacks carried out by the Gran Grif gang in the communes of
Petite Rivière de l’Artibonite and Marchand Dessalines234.
1. Context
The department of Artibonite - once considered Haiti’s agricultural breadbasket in Northern Haiti - has become one of the
most alarming centres of gang violence. The rise of gangs like Gran Grif and Kokorat San Ras (between 2015 and 2017)
drove increased insecurity through 2020, followed by a temporary decline in early 2021 after the death of the Gran Grif
leader, Odma Louissaint. From March 2022, the human rights situation deteriorated sharply.235 According to local
sources236, the intensification of violence was also marked by the attack by Gran Grif in January 2023 against the Liancourt
police station, in which six officers were killed (see para. 68 of S/2023/674).
The region has faced increasing violence not only from Gran Grif (HTe.001), led by Luckson Elan (HTi.007) but also from
Kokorat San Ras, led by Bendjy, as well as from gangs coming from the capital, namely Canaan, led by Jeff Larose (a.k.a
“Jeff”), and 400 Mawozo, led by Lanmo San Jou (HTi. 004), who advanced into the lower Artibonite, after seizing strategic
towns in the centre department in April 2025 (see S/2025/597).
To collectively oppose gang activities, since late 2025, around 30 self-defense groups distributed across the department
united into a coalition, called the Lower Artibonite Coalition. The latter is led by Francky Pierre, a.k.a “Ti Mépri”, and its
membership totals roughly 300 individuals. Ti Mépri is also responsible for the Coalition des révolutionnaires pour sauver
l’Artibonite (known as the Jean Denis coalition), based in the locality of Jean Denis (Petite Rivière de l’Artibonite
commune). The group - considered by many interlocutors as a gang rather than a self-defence group, since it regularly
commits human rights violations including the killing of inhabitants and burning of properties - was formed to resist the
activities of Gran Grif gang,237 that exponentially enlarged its area of control and influence since 2022 (see para. 49 of
S/2023/674).

Situation in Saint Marc commune
Saint-Marc consistently targeted by gangs due to its strategic importance
Saint-Marc is the second-largest city in the Artibonite department and a major port town with relatively developed
infrastructure. Its economy is largely driven by locally produced agricultural goods, while also attracting significant interest
from members of the diaspora and a wide range of economic stakeholders. The city holds strategic importance due to its
role as a key transport hub linking Port-au-Prince with the Artibonite and northern regions. Its position along major road
networks, combined with its port facilities and economic activity, makes it a critical centre for trade, logistics, and regional
mobility.
Saint-Marc thus became a major refuge for residents fleeing gang violence and a pressure point, while gangs have been
ultimately targeting to seize this strategic commune. Not only Gran Grif but also the gang of Canaan want to extend their
territory up to Saint Marc to increase criminal revenues. However, they have met the strong resistance of residents,
transporters, local merchants and self-defense groups, who are seeking to secure road access for their goods from the port
of Saint Marc to the other regions.
According to a local authority of Saint-Marc, the commune is surrounded by gangs which have taken over Montrouis, La
Chapelle, Marchand Dessalines, and Liancourt, as well as the first and second sections of Petite-Rivière de l’Artibonite. In
addition, Saint-Marc is becoming saturated, as it has received many people displaced from neighbouring communes256.
In the case of Montrouis, a local authority explained to the Panel that, since October 2025, gang members faced no resistance
to infiltrate the town, coming from the coast, the RN1 and the mountain areas. They operate freely, with each locality under
the control of a designated leader. In this touristic area, they move at their will, looting major hotels, vacation homes,
churches, and Vodoo temples. The population almost entirely displaced to other areas, such as Saint-Marc and Arcahaie
communes257.
Local authorities and residents fear attacks against the commune of Saint-Marc, notably following the alliance between
Gran Grif and Kokorat San Ras, which has led to the control of Marchand Dessalines. In this way, outraged by the violence,
residents and notably victims of the violence in Jean Denis locality, organized a protest, early May 2026, to call on concrete
action from the authorities to dismantle the gangs that are terrorising several areas in the region. Participants erected
barricades using burning tires and vehicles in several neighbourhoods, and on the RN1, bringing traffic to a complete
standstill. As a result, commercial and school activities were suspended.
Tensions escalated while police intervened to disperse the crowd. Two people, including a 16-year-old boy, lost their lives
due to police actions. Several others were injured, and people were affected by heavy use of tear gas258. While condemning
acts of violence by the population during protests, such as burning tires, vehicles, or property – including the office of the
Directorate General of Taxes (Direction Générale des Impôts - DGI) - the population deplored the disproportional use of
force against protestors.
Amid worsening security conditions, on 5 May 2026, local authorities in Saint-Marc imposed a 72-hour curfew across the
municipality. The measure restricts night-time movement and requires police authorization for public gatherings, with
participants identified259.

Annex 17: The use of privately made firearms by armed actors in Haiti
The Panel had previously reported the presence of PMFs manufactured from 80% receivers (see S/2024/704) in the arsenal
of gangs, as well as the transfer of firearms components in violation of the embargo. During the reporting period one PMF
was documented by the Panel in Cap-Haitien, and several PMFs, likely built in the United States, were seized as part of
shipments having violated the arms embargo. Investigations are ongoing.
PMFs are firearms produced by individuals rather than by licensed commercial manufacturers. They may be assembled
from part kits, unfinished receivers, or self-manufactured components, including those produced using 3D-printing
technology. PMFs are often fabricated and acquired by individuals seeking to circumvent firearms regulations. As they are
generally unserialised, they are particularly difficult to trace and therefore present significant challenges for law
enforcement authorities.
The rapid evolution of manufacturing technologies, particularly 3D-printing, now enables the production of increasingly
sophisticated and reliable firearms that can be difficult for an untrained observer to distinguish from commercially
manufactured weapons.
Although the scale of the phenomenon remains difficult to quantify, the proliferation of PMFs appears to be increasing in
North America and across the Caribbean region, including Haiti261.
Seizure of a FMGC-01 in Cap-Haitien
Documented by the Panel in October 2025, the firearm below was seized on 19 July 2025, in La Madeline, near Cap-Haitien
from a car transporting three individuals who claimed to be members of the Ouanaminthe Brigade de Surveillance des Aires
Protégées (BSAP) unit262. As indicated in previous reports (see S/2024/704, Annex 26), the BSAP is a rogue armed brigade
composed of autonomous units operating across the country outside of protected areas, whose members are involved in
both local security and criminal activities, including arms trafficking.
This firearm is a rare variant of an advanced 3D-printed firearm, the FGC-9 (for Fuck Gun Control 9mm). It is composed
at 90% of 3D printed parts mixed with components that can be sourced from any hardware store.263 The FGC-9 was designed
around 2020 by an individual in Europe and a detailed fabrication manual was published online264. Compared with earlier
generations of 3D-printed firearms, the design is more advanced and robust, and relatively easy to produce - it no longer
requires manufactured firearms components. This firearm could technically fire hundreds of rounds during its lifetime.

Annex 18: Dominican ammunition in use by Haitian gangs
During the reporting period, the Panel documented several types of ammunition that appear to have been diverted from
Dominican national stockpiles. Multiple security sources consulted by the Panel, both in the Dominican Republic and in
Haiti, indicated that corrupt officers within the Dominican police and armed forces were involved in the diversion of
materiel, including sales in border areas for onward transfer to Haiti.
Ammunition from the Dominican police
During its most recent inspections of seizures of ammunition in Port-au-Prince in 2026, the Panel noticed that ammunition
consistent with those that had been diverted from the Dominican police as previously reported by the Panel in 2025 (see
S/2025/597, section II.C.2.c and Annex 25), were in use by 400 Mawozo and several gangs in Bel-Air, Port-au-Prince.
In 2024, the Panel investigated the seizure of several boxes of 5.56x45mm ammunition (5000 rounds) from non-state actors
in Mirebalais, Centre department of Haiti. The Panel shared the information with the Dominican authorities, who conducted
an inventory of the Police stockpile and arrested several officers (see Annex 25 of S/2025/597). This shed light on the
diversion of 908,001 items, mostly ammunition, from the Dominican National Police stockpile, including more than
489,000 rounds of 9mm, 230,000 rounds of 5.56x45mm, and 26,000 rounds of 7.62x39mm ammunition, as well as 93,000
12-gauge shells266.
While it is difficult to accurately estimate the amount of materiel that reached Haiti, some Dominican security experts
explained to the Panel that, given the fact the market for 5.56x45mm and 7.62x39mm ammunition in the Dominican
Republic is quite limited, it is likely that most of this materiel was moved on to Haiti as these are the two primary calibers
used by gangs267.
Ammunition from the Dominican Armed Forces
The Dominican Armed Forces apply the good practice of marking their ammunition, which allows for better management
of their stockpiles, and preventing, identifying and investigating diversions.
The Panel identified several types of ammunition marked ‘ERD’ (Ejercito Republica Dominicana – Dominican Armed
Forces) during its latest inspections of seized materiel in February 2026 (see photographs below). The ammunition was
consistent with those produced by Industria Militar de Colombia (Indumil). Responding to a Panel’s official
communication, the Colombian authorities confirmed that the items had been produced by Indumil and were parts of two
contracts signed with the ERD and the Dominican Ministry of Defense respectively in 2022.
The Panel contacted the Dominican authorities, asking which units these particular lots of ammunition had been allocated
to and whether they could share any information about the results of their investigations into these diversions.
The Dominican authorities responded that the ammunition had been procured in 2022 from a producer in Colombia.
Furthermore, they told the Panel that, in December 2025, a second lieutenant from the ERD had been arrested in San Juan
de la Maguana Province, Dominican Republic, along with a Haitian national, while transporting 1516 rounds of ammunition
on a motorbike268. The prosecution case is ongoing.
The Panel is yet to receive additional information about which units used the various lot numbers or whether further
investigations are ongoing.

Annex 19: Arms and ammunition trafficking to armed actors in the Artibonite department
The surge in armed violence in the Artibonite during the reporting period (see Annex 15) was sustained by the capacity of
armed actors - both self-defence groups and gangs - to procure arms and ammunition through multiple trafficking channels.
These include the purchasing of materiel to networks procuring in the Dominican Republic as well as individuals using the
port of Saint-Marc to transfer items from the United States. For instance, in May 2026, the seizure of one firearm in second
hand good coming from the United States led to the domicile of a trafficker where additional materiel was seized (see Annex
16). Beyond international supply, armed actors benefit from diversions from HNP officers, while gangs also receive materiel
through inter-gang transfers.
Acquisition of materiel by self-defence groups
Self-defence groups in the lower Artibonite, while much less equipped than the gangs they oppose, have increasingly
managed to procure materiel. Financial contributions from the local population- sometimes collected forcibly at checkpoints
– and the diaspora are supporting them in purchasing materiel.
In December 2025 and January 2026, the Haitian Customs in Cap-Haitien intercepted several parcels from maritime
containers coming from the United States with firearms and ammunition (see Annex 16). The trafficker, Clément Thelusma
was arrested and explained that he was selling to various clients, including to the Jean Denis Coalition (see para. 61 of the
present report).
Self-defence groups also obtain ammunition from HNP officers with whom they collaborate during operations. In May
2026, a video shared with the Panel shows materiel stolen by the Gran Grif gang from members of the Jean Denis Coalition,
including a box of 5,56x45mm ammunition recently imported by the FADH, of which some had been transferred to the
HNP (see para. 113 of the present report)271.

Through theft by HNP officers
Investigations by the Panel indicate that a network of UDMO officers in the Artibonite department have been involved in

Their modus operandi included the gradual syphoning of boxes of 20 rounds of 5,56x45 mm ammunition that they would

Annex 20: Loss of materiel from the Multinational Security Support Mission (MSS)
Paragraph 14 of resolution 2700 (2024) calls for the MSS to implement weapons and ammunition management processes
and to report to the Panel any diversion of weapons and ammunition, including through theft and loss.
During their deployment in Haiti, two Kenyan officers lost their lives and one went missing in clashes and ambushes by
gangs, while others were forced to abandon vehicles, potentially with materiel inside. The Panel requested detailed
information on all the materiel lost, including types, calibers, manufacturers and serial/lot numbers for firearms/ammunition.
The Panel also asked to know the names of the gang(s) which likely took custody of the materiel.
In addition, the Panel documented several rounds of ammunition seized from gangs which seem consistent with those used
by the Kenyan Police while deployed in Haiti. The Panel requested the Kenyan authorities to confirm whether the
ammunition below was indeed used by Kenyan MSS personnel and how the rounds escaped their custody.

Annex 21: The diverse ways characterizing kidnappings
- Targetting affluent areas, including in the ‘green zones’: Gang influence is being felt in areas including those considered
to be ‘safer,’ or initially considered as ‘green zones’, such as Pétion-Ville, in their effort to kidnap ‘high-value’ targets for
maximizing revenue (ransom). Gangs infiltrate those areas by deploying ‘spies’ to identify potential targets based on their
social status (wealth) or those with family ties abroad.277
- Involvement of some HNP officers: Some HNP officers also undertake kidnappings in a pattern similar to gangs that
impersonate them. Security agents are therefore faced with the challenge of differentiating between gang-impersonated
kidnapping and police-engineered kidnapping.278 In one case, four HNP personnel were linked to a kidnapping incident
near the Hotel Montana area in April 2026. This led to some of their HNP colleagues nick-named “amis des quatre - friends
of the four” to mobilize protesters on 27 April 2026, in Delmas, Turgeau, Canapé-Vert and Pétion-Ville in West department
to agitate until they were released.279
- HNP Tip-offs: The inter-dependence between gangs, government staff and some HNP officers is not new. This relationship
is visible in situations where gangs are tipped off by some HNP insiders about impending HNP/security operations
(S/2026/241, para. 19; and Annex 12 of the present report). Sources described to the Panel how some HNP officers look
aside during patrols to allow gangs escape with victims. They also alert gangs about specific locations of potential victims
and their movement patterns.280
