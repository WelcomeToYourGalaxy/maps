Summary
The present report contains the findings of the Working Group of Experts on People
of African Descent following its visit to Suriname from 24 March to 2 April 2025. The report
provides an overview of the current legal, institutional and policy framework and of measures
taken to prevent racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance faced by
people of African descent in Suriname, underscoring positive developments as well as
implementation gaps. The Working Group describes the situation, highlights good practices
and the main challenges, and makes recommendations.

I. Introduction
1. At the invitation of the Government and pursuant to Human Rights Council
resolutions 9/14 and 54/26, the Working Group of Experts on People of African Descent
carried out a country visit to Suriname from 24 March to 2 April 2025. The delegation
included Barbara Reynolds (the Chair of the mission), and Miriam Ekiudoko and Isabelle
Mamadou (experts).
2. During the visit, the Working Group assessed the human rights situation of people of
African descent in Suriname, and gathered information on racism, racial discrimination,
xenophobia and related intolerance. The Working Group examined laws, policies and
strategic plans designed to prevent racial discrimination and safeguard the rights of victims
of racism, as well as the State’s responses to systemic, multiple and intersecting forms of
discrimination.
3. The delegation visited Paramaribo (district and capital), Totness (Coronie district),
Moengo and Albina (Marowijne district), Brownsweg (Brokopondo district) and Onverwacht
(Para district). It met with the Minister of Justice and the Police, the Permanent Secretary of
the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and senior officials from the Ministries of Justice and the
Police, Public Health, Education, Science and Culture, Social Affairs and Housing, Labour,
Regional Development, and Natural Resources. Within the Ministry of Justice and the Police,
it met with representatives of the Human Rights Bureau and the Bureau for Women and
Children. The delegation also met with representatives of the General Bureau of Statistics,
the Human Rights Commission of the National Assembly, the Land Rights Bureau in the
Office of the President of the Republic, and local authorities. Furthermore, the delegation
visited the Central Penitentiary Institution in Santo Boma, the Opa Doeli youth transit
detention centre in Paramaribo, a community centre in the Latour neighbourhood of
Paramaribo and a school in Peto Ondo village in Moengo.
4. The Working Group thanks the Government of Suriname for accepting the request to
visit, and for its fruitful cooperation and efforts to facilitate meetings with different
ministerial departments, national institutions and local authorities. The Working Group
thanks all the officials at the national and local levels who engaged in dialogue, shared
presentations and documents and replied to its questions.
5. The Working Group also thanks the Indigenous Kaliña people at Pierre Kondre village
for warmly receiving the delegation and providing testimony of mutual solidarity, both
historical and present, and members of the nearby Maroon communities, who shared their
struggles for legal recognition of their collective rights as well as their difficulties in
accessing social services fully.
6. In each city and district visited, the Working Group met with people of African
descent, victims of racial discrimination and their families, human rights defenders, lawyers,
academics, Maroon communities and their leaders, and women’s groups, including from
rural and remote areas, and with civil society organizations led by people of African descent,
including Maroons and Creoles in the interior and in urban areas respectively. The Working
Group extends its gratitude to the resident coordinator and the United Nations country team
for their support, and to the non-governmental organization Na Afrikan Kulturu fu Sranan
(Our African Culture of Suriname) for its assistance in organizing and coordinating meetings
with civil society.
7. The Working Group notes that Suriname prides itself on being ethnically, culturally
and religiously diverse and on the peaceful co-existence and respect among the different
communities living on its territory, as symbolized by the nationally celebrated concept of the
Flower Garden.

II. Background
A. The presence of people of African descent in Suriname and the
institutions of slavery, colonialism and racism
8. The multi-ethnic, multicultural and pluralistic society of Suriname is deeply rooted in
the inter-imperial entanglements in the country between European imperial powers. In 1667,
through the Treaty of Breda, Suriname was ceded to the Dutch Republic by the British in
exchange for New Amsterdam (modern-day New York City).1
9. The origins of the African presence in Suriname can be traced back to the
mid-seventeenth century expansion of the English sugar frontier. Following the success of
sugar cultivation in Barbados, planters sought new frontiers for capital and labour. Between
1650 and 1675, Suriname became a focal point for this diaspora. This expansion redirected
Barbadian agricultural expertise and enslaved labour to the South American mainland.2
10. Enslaved Africans were first brought to Suriname in 16513 by British planters under
the patronage of Francis Willoughby,4 at which time the first permanent settlement of
Europeans was established.5
11. The transition to Dutch control in 1667 did not alleviate the conditions of the enslaved;
rather, it intensified the plantation system. The plantation economy that emerged was
predicated on the brutal exploitation of enslaved Africans, who were imported in massive
numbers to clear forests and cultivate sugar. By the eighteenth century, Suriname had become
one of the most profitable sugar colonies in the world, yet also the most lethal. The
demographic imbalance, with enslaved Africans vastly outnumbering Europeans, created a
volatile environment that facilitated the most significant phenomenon in Surinamese history:
the Maroon resistance.6
12. On 1 July 1863, slavery was formally abolished in Suriname but ended in practice in
1873 for those who were already enslaved, after a mandatory 10-year transition period.
13. The General Bureau of Statistics of Suriname reports that Maroons (21.7%) and
Creoles (15.7%) combined account for roughly 37.4% of the country’s population.7
B. The Maroons and the formation of tribal communities
14. Maroons (or Businengre) are the descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped from
plantations into the dense rainforest of the interior. Over centuries, these escapees formed
distinct tribal societies, successfully defending their autonomy through guerrilla warfare
against colonial forces. By the mid-eighteenth century, the Dutch colonial government was
forced to sign peace treaties with the major Maroon tribes, including the N’djuka (in 1760),

15. Today, there are six primary Maroon tribes: the Saramaka, the N’djuka, the Matawai,

“tribal peoples” because they share similar social, cultural and economic conditions with
Indigenous Peoples, having established a deep, ancestral connection to their lands.9
C. The Creoles
16. Distinct from the Maroons are the “Creoles”, a term used in Suriname to describe the
urban and coastal Afro-Surinamese population that remained within the colonial social
structure and largely integrated into the urban and coastal society after emancipation in 1863.
While Maroons maintained a tribal lifestyle, Creoles became central to the political and
administrative development of the Surinamese State. Nonetheless, economic difficulties for
Creoles are often rooted in systemic barriers to credit, housing and employment, alongside a
historical loss of land titles in the Frimangron (“Free Man’s Land”) neighbourhood of
Paramaribo during the post-colonial transition.
D. Uprooted communities: the Afobaka Dam and the impact of extractive
industries
17. Displacement of Afro-Surinamese communities has been a recurring theme in the
pursuit of national development. The most egregious historical example is the construction
in the 1960s of the Afobaka Dam, resulting in the creation of the Brokopondo Reservoir. This
hydroelectric project, intended to power aluminium-smelting operations, flooded
approximately 1,500 square kilometres of Saramaka ancestral territory. More than
6,000 Saramaka were forcibly displaced into “transmigration villages”, without receiving
adequate compensation or legal title to their new lands.10 The ecological and social impact
was devastating; the reservoir submerged dozens of villages, sacred sites and traditional
hunting grounds. Expert witness accounts described the project as having been conducted
without any meaningful environmental or social impact assessment, leading to long-term
mercury contamination and loss of food sovereignty.11 Although the Afobaka Dam project
originated in the 1960s, its reported social, environmental and land-related consequences
constitute an inherited legacy that continues to affect displaced communities and to influence
contemporary perceptions of mistrust of State-led economic projects.12
E. Land and resource rights, and environmental issues
18. The Working Group acknowledges the stated intention of Suriname to resolve land
rights issues. It is, however, highly concerned that despite binding judgments of the
Inter-American Court of Human Rights, the State has failed to adopt appropriate measures to
find a definitive solution to the problem of land rights for Surinamese of African descent.
19. Among critical issues are the absence of legislation recognizing collective rights of
Surinamese of African descent over lands and resources; land dispossession, which
particularly affects people of African descent; land tenure, land ownership and use;

20. Article 41 of the Constitution stipulates that “natural riches and resources are property

In practice, this has been understood by the State as meaning that all land and natural
resources without proven private title belong to the State and must be used to promote
economic development.13 Information gathered indicates that the State has used this “domain
principle” to justify granting concessions to extractive industries on lands traditionally
occupied by Afrodescendent tribal communities, without their consent. This has resulted in
disputes with people of African descent, and contradicts international and regional human
rights standards.14
21. The intersection of land rights, environmental degradation and economic conflict in
Suriname has distinct impacts on Maroons and Creoles because they maintain a spiritual and
cultural connection to the rainforests. The State continues to grant mining and logging
concessions on the Maroon traditional territories without formal delimitation or
demarcation.15
22. Gold mining, both small-scale and industrial, often occurs on Maroon lands. These
activities lead to deforestation, river siltation, and displacement of traditional livelihoods,
allegedly often with the tacit approval of the State rooted in the “domain principle”.16
Consequently, the State’s reliance on gold mining and timber exports creates a structural
incentive to deny communal land rights, which leads to ongoing environmental degradation
and mercury poisoning in Maroon territories, constituting a failure to guarantee the right to a
clean and healthy environment.
23. The Working Group commends the accession by Suriname to the Minamata
Convention on Mercury, in 2018, and the subsequent development of the national action plan
on artisanal and small-scale gold mining, in conformity with article 7 of the above-mentioned
Convention. However, it is concerned that the most pressing environmental crisis in
Suriname is the prevalence of artisanal and small-scale gold mining. Artisanal and
small-scale gold mining utilizes mercury to amalgamate gold, leading to the release of
approximately 10,000kg of mercury into the Surinamese environment annually. This
mercury enters the aquatic food chain as methylmercury, bioaccumulating in predatory fish,
which are a primary protein source for Maroon communities.

Aloeboetoe et al. v. Suriname (1993)
25. The first significant case involving Suriname at the Inter-American Court of Human
Rights was Aloeboetoe et al. v. Suriname. In December 1987, during the Surinamese War of
the Interior, members of the National Army detained and murdered seven Saramaka Maroons
near the village of Atjoni.17 The State accepted responsibility for the killings. The judgment
of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights on reparations in this case required the State
to provide compensation based on Saramaka kinship structures and customary law, which
was considered as a precedent for the recognition of “tribal identity” in international law.18
The Moiwana Massacre, of 1986, and Moiwana Community v. Suriname (2005)
26. On 29 November 1986, the Suriname National Army attacked the N’djuka Maroon
village of Moiwana, massacring approximately 40 residents – mostly women, children and
elderly people – burning the village to the ground.19 Survivors fled into the forest and
eventually to French Guiana, becoming refugees. For nearly two decades, the State failed to
conduct a criminal investigation or to allow the survivors to return to their lands.
27. In the case of Moiwana Community v. Suriname, the Inter-American Court of Human
Rights ruled that the State had violated the survivors’ rights to judicial protection and freedom
of movement. The Court recognized the “all-encompassing relationship” between the
N’djuka Maroon people and their traditional lands, noting that the inability to perform burial
rituals for their murdered kin on their ancestral soil caused profound spiritual and
psychological suffering.20 The Court ordered the State to investigate the massacre, to provide
reparations and to legally title the collective land ownership.
Saramaka People v. Suriname (2007)
28. The ruling in Saramaka People v. Suriname holds substantive influence for the rights
of people of African descent. The Saramaka challenged the State’s granting of logging and
mining concessions on their territory without consultation or compensation. The Court
considered that “the Saramaka people make up a tribal community whose social, cultural and
economic characteristics are different from other sections of the national community,
particularly because of their special relationship with their ancestral territories, and because
they regulate themselves, at least partially, by their own norms, customs and/or traditions”,21
and recognized that they had distinct rights to the natural resources found within their
traditional territories, which could be restricted only under specific circumstances, and that
such restriction applied also in cases involving the granting of mining and logging
concessions.

Saramaka people, in conformity with their customs and traditions, regarding any
development, investment, exploration or extraction plan within Saramaka territory;
(b) The State must ensure that the community receives a reasonable benefit from

G. Judicial non-compliance and denial of collective legal personality
30. Despite these judgments from the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, Suriname
has exhibited a persistent pattern of judicial non-compliance. At the time of the Working
Group’s visit, the State had yet to enact a comprehensive legislative framework that
recognizes the collective legal personality of Maroon and Indigenous communities. The
implementation of these judgments is still debated in Suriname. The State indicates obstacles
in operationalizing them while acknowledging that the process to give effect to the judgments
is still “under study”, with consideration of different implementation options and in
consultation with the populations concerned. The Working Group is of the view that without
this recognition, tribal communities cannot hold legal title to land as a group, making them
vulnerable to “land-grabbing” by private interests and the State.
III. Legal, policy and institutional framework and steps taken for
the protection of the human rights of people of African
descent
A. Legal framework
31. Suriname is a Party to most of the core international human rights treaties, including
the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination.
However, the State is not a Party to the International Convention on the Protection of the
Rights of All Migrant Workers and Members of Their Families, the International Convention
for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, the Optional Protocol to the
Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or
Punishment and the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and
Political Rights aiming at the abolition of the death penalty. Nor has it accepted the individual
complaints procedures under the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms
of Racial Discrimination, the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Economic,
Social and Cultural Rights, the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the
Child on a communication procedure or the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the
Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women. The Working Group regrets that
Suriname has not ratified the Inter-American Convention against Racism, Racial
Discrimination and Related Forms of Intolerance and the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples
Convention, 1989 (No. 169). The Working Group invites Suriname to accept the human
rights treaties mentioned above to which it is not yet a Party.
32. Article 8 of the country’s Constitution establishes the principle of equality and
non-discrimination, stating that “no one shall be discriminated against on the grounds of
birth, sex, race, language, religion, origin, education, physical or mental condition, or any
other status”.23 Articles 8 to 39 (chaps. 5 and 6) of the Constitution guarantee civil, political,
economic, social and cultural rights and freedoms. Noteworthily, article 28 stipulates equal
rights for all employees irrespective of age, sex, race, nationality, religion or political
opinions. This constitutional anti-discrimination framework is complemented by laws that
have been enacted to give effect to the promotion of the principle of non-discrimination and
equality for citizens and foreign nationals, such as articles 126 sub (a), 175, 175 sub (a), 176,
176 sub (b), 176 sub (c) and 500 sub (a) of the Penal Code. Notably, the definition of
discrimination in article 126 sub (a) of the Penal Code applies to everyone, taking the
principle of equality into consideration, while its article 175 sub (a) prohibits organizations
from promoting and inciting racial discrimination and criminalizes incitement to hate,
discrimination or violence. Article 500 sub (a) of the Penal Code criminalizes occupational

B. Institutional and policy measures
34. The Working Group welcomes the establishment by the National Assembly (the
unicameral parliament) of Suriname of a Human Rights Commission, which consists of seven
parliamentarians from the whole spectrum of political representation. The Commission is
tasked with studying bills and reporting its findings to the National Assembly. Its main
functions consist of mainstreaming human rights into the work of the parliament, and scrutiny
of all bills and of the executive’s policies to ensure their compliance with the country’s
human rights obligations. It can initiate and carry out inquiries, ascertain and review the
executive’s reasons for not acceding to or ratifying existing international human rights
treaties and scrutinize the executive’s response to judgments of national, regional and
international courts with implications for human rights. The Commission has the power to
propose amendments to legislation, to hold hearings and consultations, to conduct visits
within the country, 24 to receive complaints emanating from groups and to issue
recommendations to the Plenary of the National Assembly and to the Government. The
Commission had reportedly heard and consulted with the Maroon populations/people of
African descent with regard to land rights issues. The Working Group appreciates this avenue
as an additional forum available to people of African descent to raise their concerns.
35. The Working Group notes that, following several recommendations from human
rights bodies, Suriname is currently working to establish a national human rights institution,
with a draft law at its final stage, which had been submitted to the Ministry of Justice and the
Police in January 2025 for further transmission to the National Assembly. The Working
Groups believes that establishing an independent national human rights institution with a
mandate on racial discrimination issues will provide additional remedies to people of African
descent where their rights are violated.
36. The Working Group notes that Suriname established a National Committee for the
Commemoration of the Abolition of Slavery, on 19 March 2022, with the aim of organizing
national commemoration activities from June 2022 to August 2023, including the preparation
of a study on the transatlantic slave trade, trade and slavery, and the contemporary legacies,
and their impact on Suriname. It also notes that the Committee developed a 12-point national
reparation programme as a framework for such activities. The Working Group sought
information regarding the current status and activities of the Committee. However, it remains
unclear to the Working Group whether the Committee is currently operational and, if so, what
impact its work has had.

IV. Manifestations of racial discrimination
A. Entrenched impact of the legacies of enslavement and colonialism
38. People of African descent endured centuries of slavery followed by a period of
colonialism. One hundred and sixty-three years after the abolition of slavery and 51 years
after the country’s independence, Surinamese of African descent, the largest racial group in
the country, are still subjected to the legacy of enslavement and colonialism, and suffer subtle
and passive forms of discrimination, marginalization and exclusion, which manifest
themselves in many ways.
39. Some testimonies explained that this unfavourable situation of people of African
descent is partly due to negative perceptions from others, expressed through formulas such
as “these people do not invest in themselves, they do not save, they do not work hard, they
lack self-esteem and self-confidence”. Other Surinamese of African descent admitted that
they were “living in mental slavery”, thus conveying that the legacy of enslavement was still
vivid among many of them. These statements do not indict Surinamese of African descent
only, but all Surinamese. Most interlocutors across racial groups acknowledged that
Surinamese of African descent were not enjoying their human rights on an equal footing with
other racial groups. This leads the Working Group to conclude that one of the most egregious
harms to these communities, as Maroons, Creoles, plantation groups and other tribal
communities, has been the negative impact on their identity formation, especially of children
and adolescents. The vibrancy, courage and tenacity of their forebears of just a century ago
has been suppressed, and the need to survive has reportedly led to a certain passivity and
internalization of slurs and stereotypes.
40. Another pervasive form of discrimination manifests in the Government’s unequal
distribution of support. Communities in districts visited in the interior reported receiving
significantly less support than other districts inhabited by other ethnic and/or racial groups.
Accordingly, the very limited State assistance and investment suggest that the disadvantaged
situation of people of African descent is not merely a consequence of living in remote areas
but originates also from the adoption and existence of laws, public policies and practices that
have a discriminatory impact on them. The long-standing default by the Government on
giving effect to the judgments of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which are
favourable to the rights of Surinamese of African descent in Suriname, has fostered and
conveyed a sentiment among Surinamese of African descent that their rights are not treated
as being equal to those of the rest of the population.
41. In pursuance of its desire to preserve unity, harmony and concordance among the
diverse racial groups living in its territory, Suriname has an opportunity to make a decisive
break from the legacies of enslavement and the past. These pervasive legacies inhibit people
of African descent, especially Maroon communities, from enjoying their rights fully on an
equal footing with others, leading to structural racial discrimination and underlying forms of
racism. Suriname has an occasion to examine the current situation of this population in a
frank, transparent and inclusive manner to build a society free from any kind of
discrimination and exclusion.
B. Lack of recognition of the contributions of people of African descent to
Surinamese society
42. While acknowledging the information provided by the State, according to testimonies
and experiences shared, people of African descent suffer from a lack of recognition at
multiple levels. This encompasses the very limited presence of positive narratives on their

43. Structural racial discrimination has led many people of African descent to feel and
express that they do not fully belong to Surinamese society. Although different groups of
people of African descent collectively represent the majority of the population according to
the 2012 census, they continue to be left behind according to most socioeconomic indicators.
Their voices are reportedly marginalized compared to the rest of the population. “Our voices
do not count”, stated a woman during a meeting with Afrodescendent communities. This
concern of being ignored by authorities was repeatedly brought to the attention of the
Working Group during visits in Maroon-majority districts, coupled with complaints that
despite the deterioration of their living conditions in several areas, most of their grievances
have been overlooked and have remained unaddressed.
44. The impact of the prevailing civil, political, economic and social conditions facing
Surinamese of African descent poses a threat to their viability as a group. In the first instance
is the explicit failure to recognize Afro-Surinamese as a unified demographic – which appears
as a direct legacy of the divide-and-conquer strategies used throughout centuries of slavery,
colonization and segregation. Secondly, in Suriname, the colour of one’s skin continues to
cause division, perpetuated by the preference for some racial groups over others. This
division continues to fuel the racial slurs that many have indicated they have experienced,
particularly at school. Afro-Surinamese youth and young professionals shared their
experiences of having their ambitions to further their education or to enter certain professions
questioned, and their way of life disdained. Thirdly, the Working Group heard testimonies
from some people of African descent that misperceptions and stereotyping of Surinamese of
African descent reverberates, to some extent, in the education and economic sectors of
Suriname, which contributes to feeding injustices.
C. Unequal development across regions
45. While noting information from State representatives about initiatives taken to promote
development in districts of the interior – inhabited mostly by Maroon communities – and
about challenges in this regard, including lack of funding, the Working Group observed
noticeable disparities in development between districts of the interior and the capital, as well
as between certain districts. These disparities, in regard to which the State acknowledged a
need for improvement, are characterized by unstable electricity, poor road conditions and
inadequate or underdeveloped health, education and water infrastructure. The expression
“going to town” repeatedly used by some communities and local authorities symbolizes the
challenges that they face and the inevitability, in some situations, of travelling to Paramaribo,
the capital, to access appropriate services.
46. To foster development in the hinterland and across districts, the State has adopted a
decentralization policy, which is aimed at granting them greater autonomy. However, this
policy has not proved fully functional, partly due to a lack of funding for implementation. In
Moengo, the Working Group was informed that despite the development or design of
district-level strategies and plans, lack of funding prevented their realization, prompting an
interlocutor to describe this decentralization process as entirely “passive”.
47. This “passive decentralization process” and the reportedly limited State investment is
also noticeable in the capital’s peri-urban areas, particularly where large numbers of people
of African descent live. Across all of these areas, the Working Group observed substandard
physical infrastructure – specifically with regard to roads, waste management and electricity
– along with a lack of adequate education and health facilities and services, and of properly
trained professionals. The impacts of inadequate and insufficient employment opportunities
and severe difficulties in securing decent housing are reportedly particularly evident in these
peri-urban areas and settlements, as are the overcrowding, poor sanitation and environmental
degradation, coupled with the inability of occupants or their landlords to maintain the
properties.

D. Civil and political rights
49. The concerns outlined below are a summary of testimonies and reports received
during the visit.
Lack of comprehensive disaggregated data
50. The Working Group welcomes, as a good practice, the collection of data
disaggregated by race, ethnicity and national origin on the prison population at Santa Boma
prison. Nevertheless, the Working Group expresses regret that it did not receive from State
representatives any other reliable, comprehensive data disaggregated by race, colour,
ethnicity, national origin, age, sex and other status and socioeconomic indicators in all areas
of life regarding people of African descent and other groups in Suriname, while noting the
State’s position on the collection of data on race and ethnicity in several areas, such as the
public service. It regrets that, even where disaggregated data are collected and maintained by
public authorities, access to such data may be constrained by the requirement to submit a
special request. The Working Group considers that, subject to applicable safeguards for
privacy and the protection of personal data, aggregated and disaggregated official statistics
should be proactively published, regularly updated and made readily accessible in the public
domain. The Working Group recognizes that the production of comprehensive disaggregated
statistics requires a coordinated national statistical system. While the General Bureau of
Statistics has a central role in producing independent official statistics, relevant
administrative data are maintained by ministries and institutions responsible for areas
including health, education, law enforcement and justice. Collecting data would nevertheless
enable Suriname to design targeted measures – including special measures – and other
policies aimed at promoting and facilitating the advancement of people of African descent,
fighting against the discrimination they endure and monitoring the effectiveness of these
measures. It would have allowed the Working Group to properly assess the extent to which
people of African descent confront specific challenges compared to others.
Stereotypes, negative perceptions, and degrading expressions and hate speech,
including through media, especially social media
51. Discussions and testimonies shared with the Working Group reveal that stereotypes
regarding people of African descent remain prevalent in Surinamese society, often
manifested through derogatory expressions and disdainful attitudes. This encompasses
treating and/or stereotyping people of African descent as being lazy, lacking in
self-confidence, prone to criminal behaviour and unworthy of high-ranking positions. A
conversation at a community centre illustrated this issue. An adolescent girl said: “Yes, we
have self-esteem, but other people bring us down. Other people think that people of African
descent are still slaves and must do what others ask them to do.” A young volunteer at the
centre agreed, noting that “people think that people of African descent are not educated and
have bad luck”. Another testimony concluded, similarly, that “when a crime is committed,
people automatically assume it was committed by a person of African descent”.
52. While the Constitution of Suriname prohibits discrimination based on race, ethnicity
and religion, the State lacks specific and comprehensive legislation that criminalizes racist
hate speech. Negative and degrading expressions are reportedly widespread on social media,
primarily directed at men of African descent. Despite the existence of a special unit to
monitor it, online racist hate speech has spread out increasingly, with no other solution to
counter it effectively. Reporting by victims of cases of racist hate speech remains insufficient.

Deprivation of liberty, administration of justice, and law enforcement
54. The Working Group heard concerns regarding discrimination within the
administration of the criminal justice system, including juvenile justice and law enforcement.
This includes a disproportionate representation of Afro-Surinamese males in the overall
prison and detention population in Suriname, including youths, as exemplified by the Opa
Doeli youth transit detention centre in Paramaribo. Men and boys of Afro-Surinamese
descent receive disproportionately more severe penalties than others.
55. In Santa Boma prison, one of the country’s main prisons and populated mostly with
people of African descent, the Working Group found conditions comparable to detention
centres that it had visited in other countries. Nevertheless, three major concerns emerged and
deserve consideration. The first concerns the lack of nutritional value and the monotony of
the meals. The second regards the lack of structured opportunities for the education and skills
training of inmates to ensure their successful reintegration into society. The third issue
concerns healthcare in the facility, specifically with regard to mental health, the overuse of
paracetamol for any ailment, the administration of medication by non-medical personnel and
lack of access to specialized preventive healthcare.
56. At Santa Boma prison, authorities have reported the presence of foreign
Afro-descendant detainees, primarily from Brazil, Colombia, Guyana, Haiti and Venezuela
(Bolivarian Republic of). However, the State does not ensure some legal safeguards, in
particular interpretation and translation services for those who do not speak the official
language, to receive clear information about their rights and detention conditions.
Sometimes, other detainees assist these foreigners in understanding their rights and
obligations, as well as with legal and administrative procedures.
57. The Working Group emphasizes that these failures, besides potentially constituting
discrimination, are in contradiction of international standards protecting the rights of
detainees irrespective of their migration status, and place foreign detainees of African descent
in a position of vulnerability to discrimination and abuse. The lack of accessibility of
information in languages they understand has, in several cases, resulted in prolonged
detention of foreign detainees of African descent without them understanding the reasons for
their detention or the processes affecting them, thereby worsening their legal insecurity. The
authorities informed the Working Group that every detainee had the right to a State-appointed
legal adviser and legal assistance. The Working Group was not, however, able to assess the
accessibility of legal advisers and legal assistance and thus the effective enjoyment of this
right by foreign detainees, particularly those requiring interpretation or translation.
58. The reported disproportionate underrepresentation of Surinamese of African descent
within the police and the judicial apparatus, including in the offices of the courts, is a concern
to the Working Group. It is unclear about the deeper reasons for such underrepresentation –
whether it is due to a lack of qualified candidates, a lack of awareness-raising initiatives
directed at Afrodescendent communities about recruitments, or discriminatory practices.
Concerns were brought to the Working Group’s attention about police misconduct during
interactions with Afro-Surinamese people, as well as about reprisals faced by
Afro-Surinamese advocates and activists in recent years. Such encounters may result in
mistrust on the part of these communities of the police and judicial authorities. The State
must investigate these reported encounters, including with regard to potential violations of
the right to freedom of expression. Ensuring adequate representation of people of African

Political and public participation, representation, and governance
59. Despite people of African descent in Suriname collectively constituting the largest
demographic in Suriname, the absence of disaggregated data has prevented the Working
Group from verifying the proportion of their representation in political and public affairs,
including in the parliament, elective bodies at all levels, and high-level administrative and
executive positions, as well as the inclusion of women of African descent in these positions.
Nevertheless, information gathered suggests that people of African descent remain
underrepresented in public and political affairs in Suriname due to structural discrimination,
notwithstanding the State’s promotion of the “One Nation” policy.
60. Despite the information provided on efforts undertaken by the State, the limited
participation of people of African descent, particularly those living in the interior, is evident
as regards consultation about laws and projects that may affect them. Testimonies corroborate
the lack of adequate and meaningful consultation of people of African descent in the
elaboration of legislation, especially concerning land rights, land leases, and mining
concessions granted on their ancestral lands. The principle of free, prior and informed consent
has not always been adequately and meaningfully implemented. This has affected various
communities in the hinterland, as exemplified in Brokopondo district. Although the
Government provided information about engaging with communities on land rights
consultations, several residents in Brokopondo and further testimonies denied that they had
been consulted about the development of mining activities within their territories and
reported that the principle of free, prior and informed consent was largely disregarded. The
State has not always ensured that in all instances, the positions, perspectives and proposals
of Maroon communities are adequately reflected in the drafting of laws and policies related
to their collective territorial rights. Their continued exclusion from the decision-making
processes regarding their rights and well-being remains a pressing concern.
61. Also, the Working Group is of the view that the absence of participation of people of
African descent in decisions affecting them creates tensions in the country, violates their right
to participate in public affairs and fosters mistrust toward the Government. It emphasizes that
consultations must be meaningful; therefore, Suriname should take all appropriate measures
to ascertain the legitimacy of community representatives and consult with them in all
situations where people of African descent may be impacted.
62. The Working Group learned that protests regarding land rights and the proposed bill
on collective rights had led to severe tensions between the State and Afrodescendent
communities, creating major obstacles to dialogue and collaboration. Police forces had
allegedly been deployed to disperse demonstrators, with protesters facing terror, reprisals and
incarceration. Additionally, while some spaces for dialogue with the State exist, in practice
the Government reportedly limits recognition to large non-governmental organizations and
government-affiliated foundations as legitimate interlocutors. Meanwhile, organizations
addressing land rights issues and collective legal recognition of Afrodescendent communities
are marginalized, often ignored by the Government and politically stigmatized.
Representation in media
63. The underrepresentation and negative portrayal of people of African descent in media
are of concern to the Working Group. Shared testimonies as well as information before the
Working Group state that media ownership is concentrated within groups other than people
of African descent, which significantly influences the narratives and perspectives that
dominate public broadcasting. Several media programmes are produced in the languages of
these groups, which limits accessibility by Afrodescendent communities and impedes the
representation of their voices and concerns in media. The language barrier, coupled with very
limited diversity among media professionals, perpetuates negative stereotypes about
Afrodescendants, and risks perpetuating their marginalization in public discourse.

Freedom of religion
64. The Working Group welcomes the establishment of the Interreligious Council of
Suriname, in 2000, which promotes interreligious harmony among the various religious
groups in Suriname. It notes that since 2006, Suriname has formally incorporated
African-based religions into the broader national religious framework, aiming to preserve
and honour the country’s rich cultural and religious diversity. However, some African-based
religious traditions and expressions, especially Winti and Rastafari, reportedly remain
marginalized and significantly stigmatized. Examples include the stigmatization of some
students wearing their hair in locks – a core tenet of the Rastafari religion – being denied
access to Christian schools. The Ministry of Education has yet to remedy the situation, despite
complaints raised by several organizations. The Working Group regrets that while the
Council includes representatives from Christian, Hindu, Muslim and Jewish groups,
representatives from African-based religions have not yet been admitted.
E. Structural racial discrimination and economic, social and cultural
rights
Health
65. The Working Group found that while certain hinterland districts primarily populated
by people of African descent possessed outpatient clinics, they nevertheless lacked hospitals
and pharmacies of good standing and a sufficient number of physicians, specialists and
ambulance services. Therefore, people of African descent are compelled to travel long
distances to the capital or better-equipped districts to receive adequate care, in particular for
more complex health issues, especially chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes
but also certain childbearing complications. This situation prompted a resident of Totness to
request that a hospital be built in the local area to avoid having to go “to town” (to the capital)
to access medical care. While individuals with a low income possess State-issued health cards
allowing for free medical care, this system only covers certain treatments. In the absence of
data available to the Working Group, testimonies suggest that the prevalence of chronic
diseases is significant. The Working Group also learned that the number of patients with
HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections was increasing, with many persons of African
descent remaining unaware of their medical condition.
66. In another vein, many people of African descent in the districts visited suffer from
mental health issues, yet adequate care is either unavailable or unaffordable. In particular,
those dealing with post-traumatic slave syndrome and chronic racial stress require
immediate, appropriate attention and targeted intervention.
Education
67. In districts visited, the Working Group learned that access to secondary schools, high
schools and vocational education remains challenging for many children of African descent.
Sometimes, they have no choice but to pursue their education outside their home districts,
including in the capital. The districts of Brokopondo, Coronie, Marowijne, and particularly
the towns and villages of Para, Totness and Moengo, are emblematic of this situation. The
shortage of specialized educators, a scarcity of qualified teachers in certain fields that
facilitate admission to higher education or university and poor educational tools for many
children of African descent hinder access to education. Information received highlights poor
educational conditions, such as substandard school buildings and facilities and inadequate
sanitation, and a very limited number of playgrounds and sport facilities for children in
several schools.
68. In Albina, testimonies indicate that approximately 50% of children continue their
education in neighbouring French Guiana due to its proximity and better quality of schooling.
This “educational migration” is driven by the above-mentioned factors. Additionally, some
children of African descent who moved to pursue schooling in the capital had to repeat lower
grades due to prior insufficient preparation.

69. The fact that Dutch is the sole language of instruction poses a significant barrier for

Group expresses concern regarding the unavailability of curricula in the Sranan Tongo
language. Furthermore, the lack of curricula on Afrodescendent people’s history and
contributions to the nation’s development risks perpetuating stereotypes and derogatory
treatment or expressions against them. The Working Group was informed that the fact that
the history of slavery had been addressed in schoolbooks mainly from the economic angle
and from a Eurocentric perspective had overshadowed the harms of the transatlantic slave
trade, the sufferings of the enslaved and the legacies of this past. In addition, the Working
Group learned that some foundations and associations were “handling” the history of people
of African descent in schools. The Working Group is of the view that the teaching of the
history of people of African descent is primarily a duty of the State and should be done
through school curricula.
Housing
70. Surinamese of African descent frequently encounter significant obstacles in obtaining
adequate and affordable housing. Many are compelled to live in cramped, crowded spaces,
such as two-bedroomed homes that accommodate more than eight persons. Homes, and
sometimes even offices, lack adequate running water, sanitation and waste removal and stable
electricity. For example, the Working Group was informed that in Brokopondo district,
running water had not been supplied to homes and offices for more than two months.
Although State-led social housing programmes exist, they remain largely theoretical, as the
eligibility criteria often exclude individuals in dire financial situations, thereby restricting
access to them for low-income Afro-Surinamese. In some instances, housing programmes
have been completely discontinued, such as in Brownsweg, and many residents have been
left with no options for accessing more affordable and adequate housing. Although State
interlocutors reported the possibility of applying for a housing subsidy, testimonies collected
in the interior indicated that some obstacles prevented low-income Afro-Surinamese from
fulfilling the criteria.
Employment and economic empowerment, and lack of economic opportunities
71. The limited economic opportunities for Surinamese of African descent can be traced
back to constraints imposed on them following the abolition of slavery persisting through
colonialism and post-independence.
72. Despite measures taken by the State, including services available for jobseekers as
well as the existence of the Equal Treatment in Employment Act (which is not well known
or publicized), which allows for complaints in cases of discrimination in employment, the
reduced employment opportunities and limited economic empowerment for people of
African descent needs immediate attention. Regrettably, the lack of data makes it impossible
to measure its magnitude. Nevertheless, according to testimonies, Black entrepreneurs are
largely confined to small-scale enterprises, due to various obstacles in securing business
loans – coupled with a prolonged application process that they consider to be singularly
humiliating and exhausting. In certain geographical areas, securing a credit or insurance is
reportedly virtually impossible, depressing local investment. The “civil war”, which took
place several years ago, has been pointed to as one of the causes of the underinvestment, as
it left a stigma that has had negative effects regarding potential investment in the areas
concerned in the interior, either by the State or the private sector.
73. The Working Group learned, across the districts it visited, for example in Totness
(Corinie District), that people of African descent faced harsher difficulties in starting a
business, and about the limited work opportunities, due to the lack of funding and necessary
equipment, including for the development of agriculture and employment opportunities. In
that context, many women of African descent in the interior expressed a strong desire to start
or develop small businesses as income-generating activities but were constrained by
difficulties in securing funding and government support.
74. Industries and projects (e.g. gas and oil) developed in the territories where people of
African descent mostly live in the hinterland do not benefit them as regards employment
opportunities as they are mainly unskilled. Even when they graduate, job opportunities for

Cultural rights: promotion and preservation of cultures and traditions
75. Under international human rights standards, Suriname has the obligation to promote
and protect the cultural rights of all groups living in its territory. However, Maroon
communities reported challenges in preserving their cultural heritage and traditions.
Restricted resources for cultural education, the limited number of venues for gatherings or
ceremonies and the impact of modernization threaten their traditional practices. The Working
Group learned from some communities in the interior that their cultural projects are rarely
approved and not funded, and that others “steal” them. Without adequate support, oral
histories, music, crafts and dances risk dilution and loss over time.
76. Communities of African descent frequently face obstacles in accessing sports and
recreational facilities. Inadequate infrastructure – such as sports fields, gyms and training
centres – restricts physical fitness and diminishes youth participation in organized athletics.
Environmental pollution, climate change and impacts on health and livelihoods
77. Afro-Surinamese communities living in the interior face several environmental
challenges that affect their well-being. One significant issue is the degradation of natural
resources due to illegal gold-mining activities and deforestation. These activities not only
harm the environment but also disrupt local ecosystems and livelihoods. Multinational
companies with concessions for gold-mining utilize hazardous and chemicals, such as
cyanide and mercury, in extraction processes, causing severe damage both to the environment
and to human health. These issues are often compounded by climate change, which has
triggered more frequent and severe flooding, particularly near waterways, disrupting and
affecting local housing and agriculture. Poor waste management and limited access to clean
drinking water further exacerbate health risks in these communities, as illustrated in the Koffi
Kamp village. Addressing these issues requires sustainable State policies alongside
community-driven solutions to protect both the environment and the people who depend on
it.
F. Intersecting forms of discrimination
Protection of women and girls, children, young people and elderly people of African
descent
78. Women of African descent in Suriname meet various and profound gender-related
challenges that hinder their social and economic advancement as well as their well-being.
They continue to suffer from the historical interconnectedness of racism and sexism, which
manifests itself through systemic barriers across education, employment and healthcare due
to discrimination and limited access to opportunities. Despite their abilities, their professional
growth remains restricted by biases that undervalue their contributions to society. Social and
economic inequalities exacerbate the struggles of women of African descent in securing
housing, healthcare and financial independence. Many lack access to vital entrepreneurial
support, which makes it harder to break free from cycles of poverty in which women of
African descent are disproportionately represented. Gender-based violence against women
of African descent in and outside their communities is another critical concern of the Working
Group. It is often characterized by insufficient legal protections and inadequate and
ineffective support systems, thus potentially jeopardizing their safety and rights.
79. Several challenges negatively affect children of African descent (boys and girls) in
Suriname, disrupting their education opportunities, health and overall well-being. Early
school dropout rates are a pressing concern, driven by several factors, including financial
difficulties, unstable home environments and teenage pregnancies. Specially, the Working
Group is very concerned about the early pregnancy rates of girls of African descent, at ages

80. According to data,25 realization of sexual and reproductive health rights for

for the physical and emotional health of affected children. Such exploitation of girls of
African descent is reportedly high, especially in Maroon communities. The contributing
factors include poverty and the failure of the child protection system to adequately identify,
protect and support orphaned and undocumented children and girls. Despite measures taken
by the State, such as awareness-raising in schools, the setting up of a hotline for complaints

efforts to combat this problem must include a holistic approach in order to understand why
Afro-Surinamese children appear to be at a heightened risk of being subjected to sexual
exploitation. Targeted interventions, including stricter enforcement of laws against the
perpetrators and reinforced support systems, are necessary to prevent further exploitation and
to support the recovery, rehabilitation and social reintegration of victims. In this respect, the
Bureau of the Rights of the Child requires reinforcement in staffing and resources in order to
tackle these challenges effectively. Strengthening this institution would enable better
advocacy, monitoring and implementation of policies that protect children’s rights.
82. The Working Group is concerned that elderly Afro-Surinamese individuals
experience recurring difficulties in accessing adequate healthcare and struggle to afford the
cost of medications and medical treatments. This is compounded by social isolation, which
is reported as a growing significant issue, because traditional family support systems have
been eroded due to urbanization, forced displacement and migration. The vulnerability of
elderly Afro-Surinamese individuals is further exacerbated by financial insecurity due to
inadequate pensions or depleted savings.
LGBTQI persons
83. Experiences compiled by the Working Group indicate that Afro-Surinamese LGBTQI
persons navigate considerable daily impediments of their rights, including discrimination,
social stigma, and restricted access to supportive resources both within their communities
and in the broader national context. While there has been progress in awareness and in
advocacy for equality, homophobia and transphobia nevertheless persist in Surinamese
society, driven by cultural and religious biases. These barriers may result in LGBTQI persons
being excluded from educational and employment opportunities and may also engender
mental health issues due to isolation and a lack of acceptance.
V. Conclusions and recommendations
84. The presence and history of people of African descent in Suriname is a testament
to the spirit of resilience and resistance against the institutions of slavery, colonialism,
racism and State neglect.
85. People of African descent in Suriname, particularly the Maroon tribal
communities, remain in a precarious legal position.
86. While the Maroon tribes have successfully preserved their culture and autonomy
for centuries, their future is threatened by a legal system that fails to recognize their

87. The following recommendations are intended to assist the State in its efforts to
combat racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance. In light of
the above, the Working Group recommends that the State:
(a) Promulgate the Second International Decade for People of African
Descent (2025-2034), and fully utilize this vehicle to galvanize positive measures to
secure a viable future for Surinamese of African descent;
(b) Publish the results of the 2024 census, translate them into the main
languages spoken by communities in the country, and organize awareness-raising
campaigns through community leaders and district authorities to disseminate them
widely to people of African descent living in remote districts. Continue to collect
disaggregated data, on the basis of the principle of self-identification, across civil,
political, economic, social and cultural rights; proactively publish and regularly update
such data in accessible formats, subject to appropriate privacy and data-protection
safeguards; and use them to design, implement and assess targeted policies to promote
the inclusion of people of African descent. The Working Group encourages Surinamese
of African descent to be counted as one demographic group;
(c) Adopt a comprehensive law against racial discrimination containing a
definition thereof that is in accordance with article 1 of the International Convention
on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, expressly prohibit racial
profiling and racist hate speech in legislation, and define crimes and incidents,
incitement of racial hatred, police violence, and systemic and structural racism –
additionally, this law should guarantee a more effective and equal access to justice and
other remedies for people of African descent; and conduct training for law enforcement
officials, especially the police, and prosecutors, on racial discrimination issues and for
them to detect, register and address cases of racial profiling;
(d) Accelerate the establishment of an independent national human rights
institution in compliance with the Paris Principles, with competence to address issues
of racial discrimination, including receiving and considering complaints filed by
individuals and groups, and provide it with adequate resources to deliver its mandate
fully; and consider establishing a standing national reparations commission in line with
the work of the Caribbean Community;
(e) Enhance the capacity and opportunity for Afro-Surinamese civil society
organizations to participate in the delivery of justice and development to Surinamese of
African descent;
(f) Expedite the adoption of the Bill on the Collective Rights of Indigenous
and Tribal Peoples, with adequate, meaningful and informed consultation with
Surinamese of African descent;
(g) Accelerate the full implementation of the judgments of the
Inter-American Court of Human Rights on land rights and natural resources, in
consultation with the communities concerned, to ensure justice and accountability for
Maroon tribal communities in Suriname;
(h) Return ancestral lands to people of African descent and/or provide just,
fair and adequate compensation and agreed alternatives where this is not possible;
(i) Take the steps necessary to provide financial, educational, healthcare and
other basic infrastructure in the hinterland, and ensure that development projects are
conducted in all districts in the hinterland, with particular attention to the cities of
Moengo, Totness and Albina, to Brokopondo district, and to urban and peri-urban
areas with large concentrations of Afro-Surinamese;

(l) Ensure that mining companies fulfil their obligation to prevent and
mitigate the negative impacts of their activities on neighbouring Afrodescendent
communities, and ensure that revenues from the exploitation of natural resources in
these areas meaningfully benefit these areas with regard to the provision of adequate
infrastructure, facilities and job opportunities;
(m) Guarantee the implementation of a free, prior and informed consent

(n) Improve the conditions in places of deprivation of liberty, in particular at
Santa Boma prison which is populated mostly by people of African descent, in line with
international standards, and ensure the provision of food and water in sufficient
quantities and of sufficient quality, and adequate sanitation for all persons deprived of
their liberty, as well as education and training for inmates; and firmly address racial
discrimination against people of African descent in the criminal justice system;
(o) Hold public authorities accountable for their efforts and commitment in
eradicating anti-Black racism; and firmly combat stereotypes, negative perceptions and
degrading expressions against people of African descent through education
programmes, awareness-raising campaigns, and the prohibition of racial
discrimination and the promotion of tolerance, and ensure that those perpetrating
crimes such as incitement to hatred are prosecuted and punished, if convicted;
(p) Promptly address the healthcare facility issues faced by individuals of
African descent in the interior, ensuring that the enjoyment of the right to health is
accessible, adequate and affordable;
(q) Incorporate the history of people of African descent and their societal
contributions into all educational curricula, including the history of colonialism,
slavery, and the transatlantic trade in enslaved Africans, and provide training for
teachers accordingly in this regard;
(r) Ensure the provision of primary education in Sranan Tongo, the most
widely spoken language in Suriname, and implement Dutch and tribal language classes
in regions where needed;
(s) Ensure that children of African descent have access to quality secondary
and high school education in schools that are physically accessible and that have
good-quality education, and teachers in sufficient numbers to allow children to acquire

them equal opportunities and to cater for children with special needs;
(t) Implement a comprehensive and inclusive housing strategy to address the
housing crisis affecting people of African descent in Suriname. This includes the
expansion of affordable housing programmes, community-based development,
infrastructure improvements, land allocation and urban planning, and the
strengthening of legal frameworks;
(u) Fully implement the national action plans on artisanal and small-scale
gold mining and on climate change adaptation;

(v) Adopt and implement a comprehensive equity-focused strategy aimed at
addressing disparities among all genders, age groups and children of African descent –
this involves adopting a comprehensive approach across all sectors and implementing
enhanced and effective legal safeguards against discrimination. The State should take
steps to support poverty alleviation for Afrodescendent women and foster more
opportunities, including income-generating activities, and support Afrodescendent
women engaged in farming. Engagement with community leaders and organizations is
essential for developing culturally relevant solutions that empower Afrodescendent
communities, especially women, to advance socially;
(w) Take steps to protect religious freedom for African-based religions and
include their representatives in the Interreligious Council of Suriname;
(x) Take steps to ensure that people of African descent are fairly represented
in the media and that programmes are designed to promote their culture, and increase
the number of media professionals of African descent in the media;
(y) Enhance legal protections and promote inclusive environments to advance
the well-being of Afro-Surinamese LGBTQI persons, and adopt and implement
targeted policies and community initiatives to safeguard the well-being of elderly
