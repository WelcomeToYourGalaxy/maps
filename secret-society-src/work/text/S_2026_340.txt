Summary
The history of South Sudan is marked by a succession of ceasefires and peace
agreements. Another is now at risk of collapse.
First Vice-President Riek Machar, the leader of the main opposition group, the
Sudan People’s Liberation Movement-Army in Opposition (SPLM/A-IO), remains in
detention while on trial for a range of serious charges, including treason. He is joined
in detention by several opposition figures, as well as by a growing number of former
officials from the party of the President of South Sudan, Salva Kiir Mayardit, which
has suffered its own divisions.
Officials in Juba, however, insist that the Revitalized Agreement on the
Resolution of the Conflict in the Republic of South Sudan was never between
individuals. It therefore remains intact, they argue, as a faction of the SPLM/A-IO
has remained in Juba after a number of the party’s more prominent figures fled the
capital and pronounced the collapse of the Agreement and the Revitalized Transitional
Government of National Unity. This remaining Juba-faction has allowed the President
to claim a veneer of support for his efforts to amend the peace agreement and allow
elections to take place in December 2026, without the prior completion of a permanent
constitution or national census.
These developments have flowed from a profound loss of confidence in the
peace agreement and its mechanisms after years of halting and incomplete
implementation.
Government officials feel opposition groups have seized upon the exacting
language of the agreement to impede any meaningful progress towards the elections
that would bring an end to the power-sharing provisions from which they benefit.
Opposition leaders, in turn, believe President Kiir and the Sudan People’s
Liberation Movement (SPLM) were never truly committed to power-sharing, nor to
the unification of the country’s security forces, while key regional guarantors have
failed in their promise of neutrality. They cannot countenance, they argue, elections
under conditions that are stacked against both them and their natural constituents.
In this context, elements of the country’s leadership have argued that the current
political processes in South Sudan will not allow the country to move forward without
a military intervention that more decisively alters the balance of power in their favour.
“Sometimes you have to make war to make peace,” a security forces officer told the
Panel.
Armed confrontations between the signatories to the peace agreement, as well
as their affiliated militias, have continued in almost all parts of the country where
opposition forces retain a meaningful presence, with the focus of the most intense
hostilities shifting to northern parts of Jonglei State in late 2025 and early 2026. Both
South Sudan People’s Defence Forces (SSPDF) and Sudan People’s Liberation Army
in Opposition (SPLA-IO) forces have initiated attacks in these areas.
Despite widespread disaffection with the peace process, however, leaders in
South Sudan have found few enthusiasts for war among those expected to fight it. The
failure to pay and feed soldiers over many years has eroded morale and left SSPDF
reliant on loosely integrated or affiliated ethnic militia forces. Long-standing tribal

grievances and fears have, therefore, increasingly been exploited by military leaders
leading, inevitably, to a wave of atrocities against civilians, including in Jonglei State.
The SSPDF military campaigns also continue to be aided by an armed
deployment of Uganda Peoples’ Defence Forces in South Sudan, as well as by
helicopters and surveillance aircraft that have facilitated extensive bombardments of
opposition-held areas. Subsequent ground offensives have made substantial use of
armoured personnel carriers previously procured in violation of the arms embargo
first implemented under resolution 2428 (2018). The Panel also believes the SSPDF
procured additional small arms in early 2025 in support of its ongoing military
operations.
The humanitarian impact of renewed fighting has been catastrophic. Recent
fighting has displaced more than 320,000 individuals since January 2025, further
compounding the humanitarian impact of the ongoing conflict in the Sudan and
flooding. Widespread armed confrontations have also seriously impeded
humanitarian access, particularly in Jonglei State and the greater Equatoria region.
Several humanitarian operators have been forced to suspend their operations
following armed attacks and bombardments on both their staff and facilities in these
areas. Several humanitarian staff also told the Panel that they believed humanitarian
access and resources were being instrumentalized by all parties, but by the South
Sudan People’s Defence Forces in particular, as a means of war.
The humanitarian challenges have been further exacerbated by the economic
crisis in South Sudan. Soon after successful efforts to restore oil exports from Upper
Nile State, fighting around Heglig, in the Sudan, significantly reduced oil exports
from Unity State for several months. The Panel has also documented the continued
diversion of oil revenues, including to opaque private accounts and to large
non-competitive contracts awarded to companies tied to political and security elites.
Increased pressure on oil revenues has fuelled predation on non-oil revenues,
including the humanitarian sector, which has faced escalating financial and
bureaucratic impediments to their work, as well as waves of looting.
As many South Sudanese have lost faith in their own domestic political
mechanisms, many have called for greater support and intervention from regional
institutions and the broader international community. Both the African Union and the
Intergovernmental Authority on Development have taken an active mediation role,
calling for an immediate cessation of hostilities, the release of the First Vice-President
Riek Machar and meaningful investment in key electoral institutions. Regional bodies
and States have also taken important steps towards consolidating existing mediation
initiatives, which have grown increasingly diffuse as opposition groups have
fragmented.
“You can’t solve the problems in Jonglei without first solving the problem in
Juba,” a long-time observer of the peace process told the Panel. Sustained regional
and international support is now needed to prevent a further addition to the history of
failed peace agreements in South Sudan.

I. Background
A. Mandate and travel
1. By its resolution 2206 (2015), the Security Council imposed a sanctions regime
targeting individuals and entities contributing to the conflict in South Sudan and
established a sanctions committee (the Security Council Committee established
pursuant to resolution 2206 (2015) concerning South Sudan). The Committee
designated six individuals for targeted sanctions on 1 July 2015. With the adoption of
its resolution 2428 (2018), the Council imposed an arms embargo on the territory of
South Sudan and added two individuals to the list of designated individuals. On
30 May 2025, with the adoption of resolution 2781 (2025), the Council renewed the
sanctions regime until 31 May 2026.
2. By its resolution 2781 (2025), the Security Council also extended the mandate
of the Panel of Experts on South Sudan until 1 July 2026 so that it might provide
information and analysis in support of the work of the Committee, including as
relevant to the potential designation of individuals and entities that might be engaging
in the activities described in paragraph 7 of resolution 2206 (2015) and in paragraphs 15
and 16 of resolution 2521 (2020), as renewed by resolution 2781 (2025).
3. On 1 July 2025, the Secretary-General, in consultation with the Committee,
appointed four members of the Panel (S/2025/452). The appointment of a
humanitarian affairs expert remains pending.
4. During the mandate period, the Panel travelled to South Sudan on two occasions,
visiting Juba, in Central Equatoria State; Bentiu, Leer and Adok, all in Unity State;
and Pariang in the Ruweng Administrative Area. The Panel also travelled twice to
Kenya and Uganda.
B. Cooperation with international organizations and
other stakeholders
5. While the Panel operates independently of United Nations agencies and
institutions, it wishes to express its gratitude to the United Nations Mission in South
Sudan (UNMISS) and other United Nations staff, including in New York.
6. The Panel is grateful for the cooperation of the Government of South Sudan
during the mandate period, including for the opportunity to meet with a range of
government officials, including representatives of the Ministry for Foreign Affairs
and International Cooperation; State Governors and ⁠Chief Administrators; county
commissioners representing both the Sudan People’s Liberation Movement (SPLM)
and the Sudan People’s Liberation Movement in Opposition (SPLM-IO);
representatives of the South Sudan People’s Defence Forces (SSPDF), ⁠military
intelligence, the National Security Service (NSS), and the National Police Service;
Members of the Revitalized Transitional National Legislative Assembly representing
all parties; and the National Elections Commission. The Panel also met with senior
leaders within both factions of the Sudan People’s Liberation Movement/Army in
Opposition (SPLM/A-IO); representatives of the Ceasefire and Transitional Security
Arrangements Monitoring and Verification Mechanism; representatives of the
Reconstituted Joint Monitoring and Evaluation Commission; representatives of
several parties to the Tumaini Initiative; and representatives of humanitarian
organizations; as well as representatives of South Sudan youth groups, women’s
groups, religious groups and other civil society organizations. The Panel also met
with a number of diplomatic representations in Juba, as well as with representatives
from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Kenya, in Nairobi.

C. Methodology
9. The present report was prepared in March 2026, on the basis of the Panel’s own
independent research and investigations. The Panel conducted numerous interviews
to gather a body of credible information, obtained from a wide range of sources. The
Panel has made a conscious effort to ensure that its reporting is informed by the full
range of perspectives on the peace process in South Sudan. The Panel also drew upon
its earlier work, including previous reports to the Security Council and the
Committee, both public and confidential.
10. The Panel followed the standards recommended by the Informal Working Group
of the Security Council on General Issues of Sanctions in its report of December 2006
(S/2006/997). The Panel has corroborated the information contained in the present
report using multiple independent sources to meet the appropriate evidentiary
standards.
11. The Panel conducted its research with the greatest transparency possible, while
giving priority to confidentiality where necessary. A source, document or location is
described as confidential when its disclosure could compromise the safety of the
source.
II. Erosion of the peace process
12. The halting and incomplete implementation of the peace agreement over more
than seven years has ultimately done little to rebuild trust between communities
whose humanitarian hardships have only worsened, nor to develop security and
governance institutions that are perceived as genuinely shared and neutral.
13. “South Sudan is ruled by fear,” a religious leader told the Panel.2 “What will be
my future if my opponent takes power?” As a result, he stressed, it is easy for leaders
to persuade their communities that the struggle for power, both locally and nationally,
is “a matter of life and death.” While local political and security dynamics remain
crucial, therefore, individuals from across the political spectrum repeatedly stressed
that the erosion of the peace process is ultimately a result of the decisions and failures
of their country’s most senior leadership in Juba.
14. These fears have been evident throughout the mandate period. The fragile
power-sharing provisions of the peace agreement are collapsing, and its principal
signatories have been drawn back into direct military confrontations in several parts
of the country. Furthermore, as opposition groups have fragmented, fears and
divisions have also emerged within the party of President Kiir, SPLM, further
complicating efforts to revive a constructive political dialogue.

A. Political divisions
15. First Vice-President Riek Machar, the leader of the main opposition group,
SPLM/A-IO, has been on trial in Juba since 23 September 2025, following his
detention in March 2025. Along with seven other defendants, he faces a range of
serious criminal charges, including murder, conspiracy, terrorism and treason.3
16. As noted in the Panel’s interim report (S/2025/768), the First Vice-President’s
detention and trial have split the main opposition party. Nathaniel Oyet, who served
as deputy to Mr. Machar prior to his arrest, has fled to Nairobi, and declared that the
2018 peace agreement and accompanying Revitalized Transitional Government of
National Unity had collapsed.4 This faction retains the strongest ties to armed
opposition forces in South Sudan, though these also retain a degree of independence
within a complex and much-eroded chain of command.
17. In Juba, meanwhile, President Kiir and SPLM have sought to reframe the peace
agreement not as a deal struck with Mr. Machar, nor with any other individual, but as
an agreement between two political movements.
18. Soon after Mr. Machar’s arrest, a second group of less prominent opposition
officials appointed the Minister of Peacebuilding, Stephen Par Kuol, as the Interim
Chairperson of SPLM/A-IO and announced that they would remain in Juba and
continue to engage with the peace process.5 This faction, however, appears to retain
little legitimacy and influence with opposition communities and forces outside of Juba.6
19. In recent months, however, President Kiir and SPLM have continued their
efforts to marginalize Mr. Oyet’s faction of SPLM/A-IO by removing his supporters
and perceived sympathisers from all levels of government, replacing them either with
representatives of the rival faction led by Mr. Par Kuol, or with members of SPLM.
20. On 7 January 2026, for example, President Kiir issued a Presidential Decree7
dismissing three SPLM-IO-nominated Ministers. These were all subsequently
replaced by representatives of the SPLM-IO faction led by Stephen Par Kuol. On the
same day, a separate decree replaced 11 SPLM-IO members of Parliament with new
members nominated by Mr. Par Kuol’s faction.
21. On 19 January 2026, another decree dismissed Angelina Teny as Minister of
Interior. Angelina Teny, who is the wife of Fist Vice-President Riek Machar, was the
last remaining Minister appointed by the SPLM-IO faction led by Mr. Machar. She
was replaced as Minister of Interior by Aleu Ayieny Aleu, who represents the party of
President Kiir, SPLM, in contravention of the power-sharing provisions of the peace
agreement. 8 Similar dismissals were also made within State-level political
institutions; the opposition faction led by Mr. Oyet now consider themselves largely
unrepresented in the country’s political institutions.9

22. In parallel to the marginalization of opposition groups, however, the party of

26. Bol Mel’s rapid accumulation of positions of power had led many to consider
him a possible successor to President Kiir.15 He was, however, also considered a
divisive figure who had benefited, disproportionately, from large road construction
contracts awarded to companies with which he is affiliated.16
27. Bol Mel was also increasingly viewed, in South Sudan, as a political rival to
President Kiir’s daughter, Adut Salva Kiir.17 Earlier, in August 2025, President Kiir
appointed his daughter as the Presidential Envoy for Special Programmes, a position
that was previously held by Benjamin Bol Mel.

B. Contentious elections
28. Despite political upheaval and escalating insecurity, officials, including
President Kiir, have repeatedly emphasized the Government’s commitment to holding
elections in December 2026.18 Elections would formally end the transitional period
established by the 2018 peace agreement, which had been extended by two years on
two separate occasions. In a recent survey of 4,582 individuals in the 10 states of South
Sudan, 63 per cent of respondents wanted elections to be held in December 2026.19
29. During a meeting of signatories to the peace agreement held in Juba in
December 2025, however, participants agreed that “the continued adherence to all
outstanding prerequisites” outlined in the peace agreement would present
“insurmountable obstacles” to holding elections in December 2026.20 Participants
agreed, therefore, to amend the peace agreement in order to allow elections to be held
without the prior completion of a new permanent constitution or a new national
census.21
30. Only three of the five parties to the peace agreement formally endorsed these
amendments, with the leadership of the South Sudan Opposition Alliance and SPLM-
Former Detainees both absent.22 Moreover, Johnson Mayiik Akol, who signed on
behalf of SPLM/A-IO, was described to the Panel as “a ghost element” by several
members of the opposition group.23
31. No representatives of the faction of SPLM-IO led by Nathaniel Oyet
participated in the process, and Mr. Oyet later described the amendments as “null and
void” and as a violation of the peace agreement.24 He also stressed that elections
risked becoming deeply divisive in the current political and security environment, and
that it meant that the permanent constitution of South Sudan would be drafted by the
victors of a contentious election rather than under the power-sharing provisions of the
peace agreement.25
32. Opposition groups that are not signatories to the peace agreement also stated
that “this decision confirms that the [peace agreement] has been systematically
dismantled and stripped of its substance. With this act, the agreement has, in effect,
been finally abrogated.”26
33. Interviews with SPLM officials indicate that the decision to press ahead with
elections is, in part, the result of deep frustration with opposition leaders.27 Several
officials felt opposition groups had instrumentalised the exacting demands of the
peace agreement to stall any meaningful progress towards elections and so preserve

C. Armed hostilities between parties to the peace agreement
37. As the peace process in Juba has faltered, armed confrontations between the
principal signatories to the peace agreement and their affiliated militia groups, have
continued in most parts of the country where opposition forces maintain a presence.
In February 2026, the Ceasefire and Transitional Security Arrangements Monitoring
and Verification Mechanism tasked with monitoring the ceasefire and peace

V. Impediments to humanitarian operations
100. The conflict in the Sudan has also placed additional pressure on the
humanitarian situation in South Sudan. While population flows have slowed, South
Sudan continues, commendably, to welcome several hundred returnees and refugees
daily. Since April 2023, more than 1.3 million people have been documented as
arriving in South Sudan, with the true number likely higher.135 While South Sudanese
returnees account for the majority of the total, Sudanese refugees now make up almost
a third of all registered arrivals.
101. The humanitarian challenges in South Sudan are, however, the consequence of
multiple compounding factors. These include other external factors, such as persistent
climate-induced flooding,136 but remain driven primarily by domestic factors, and
most notably by renewed fighting and insecurity.
A. Food insecurity
102. The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification is a standardized system for
classifying hunger. It has five phases. At phase 3, families typically eat just one meal
a day and show signs of malnourishment or are forestalling this only through short-
term coping mechanisms that will harm their longer-term food security, such as
slaughtering livestock, selling productive assets, or borrowing money.

D. Illegal logging
144. Illegal logging and charcoal production remain widespread in the forests of
South Sudan, particularly in Western, Central and Eastern Equatoria States. These
provide income streams for armed groups, as well as soldiers and local officials who
go largely without pay.
145. According to individuals familiar with the industry, local chiefs and
communities control access to forests in exchange for payment. Local security forces
provide protection, tax key transport routes, and are in some cases also involved in
the transport and sale of charcoal. In opposition-controlled areas, including around
Panyume, SPLA-IO forces play a similar role.207
146. Despite a series of national and local bans, the Panel has reviewed a significant
number of documents that indicate an active and ongoing cross-border trade in
charcoal and logs.208 Despite a strong charcoal market in South Sudan, many traders
reportedly favour selling their charcoal in Uganda, where they are paid in dollars or
Ugandan shillings, and they can then purchase commodities in Uganda for export
back to South Sudan.209

local officials authorize the passage of Ugandan trucks, each carrying 300 sacks of

E. Trade in looted copper
148. Since the onset of the conflict in the Sudan, a significant trade in looted copper
has been carried out through South Sudan. Panel interviews indicate that copper
looted in Khartoum and other Sudanese cities is sold in Abyei, as well as in other
trading posts near the border with South Sudan. From here it is transported through
Wau and Juba, typically to the port of Mombasa in Kenya.211 Shipping documents
reviewed by the Panel indicate that the copper is then shipped on to buyers in India.
149. In June 2025, for example, the National Security Service issued a letter to an
Indian company, granting four trucks, each carrying 50 tons of copper, passage
through South Sudan to Kampala.212 The letter copies the SSPDF Chief of Defence
Forces and requests that all security organs facilitate the safety of the vehicles.
150. The Panel has reviewed several other documents that show trucks loaded with
100 to 600 kgs of “scrap copper” transiting through numerous checkpoints in South
Sudan, often against payment of around SSP 80,000 to SSP 100,000 to security forces
and State authorities, before being given clearance to enter either Uganda or Kenya.213
In May 2025, for example, several trucks, each loaded with 40 tons of scrap copper,
were given clearance by the National Security Service to transit from Juba to
Mombasa, in Kenya.214
151. In some cases, however, payments or permissions appear to have been
inadequate to secure safe passage. In April 2025, for example, authorities in Juba
seized a truck which they claimed contained copper illegally looted in the Sudan, as
well as from the oil industry in South Sudan.215 In January 2026, several National
Security Service officers were ordered to appear before a Court in Juba, charged with
confiscating and selling a large amount of copper seized from nine South Sudanese
traders. In January 2025, authorities in Kenya seized a truck loaded with scrap copper
travelling from Juba to Kenya, claiming the shipment was smuggled and illegal.216

B. Bilateral relations
157. Regional mediation efforts are also affected by the bilateral relations of South
Sudan with regional Member States.
158. As noted above, Uganda remains a critical regional security partner to President
Kiir, though opposition leaders stressed to the Panel that UPDF deployment in South
Sudan has eroded their confidence in Uganda as a neutral guarantor of the peace
agreement. Uganda also continues to host a large number of South Sudanese refugees,
including as a consequence of increased instability in the greater Equatoria region,
while also remaining a critical trading partner to South Sudan. Recent diplomatic
engagements have also focused on improving connectivity and border efficiency,
including in the context of a regional road network that will link the Central African
Republic, South Sudan and Uganda.227

unresolved border disputes. Lethal clashes between SSPDF and UPDF were recorded
on 28 July 2025 in the West Nile/Kajo-Keji area, as well as on 30 July 2025.228

Chief of Defence Forces, Muhoozi Kainerugaba, in Juba.229

VIII. Conclusions
164. In a recent large survey conducted in South Sudan, less than half the respondents
agreed that their country is currently at peace.237 The international community’s
concern for the rapidly deteriorating situation in South Sudan should, however, not
be contingent on the terms we use to describe it. Whether or not South Sudan is said
to have returned to war is of little consequence to those already suffering from the
collapse of the political and security structures that sought to end the cycles of
violence that have plagued the country since before its independence. The people of
South Sudan are in urgent need of regional and international support to prevent further
violence, restore unhindered humanitarian access and ensure the country’s leaders
recommit to peace.
IX. Recommendations
165. The Panel of Experts recommends that the Security Council Committee
established pursuant to resolution 2206 (2015) concerning South Sudan:
(a) Consider sending a delegation of its members to South Sudan in 2026 to
assess the current political and security situation and operating environment for
humanitarian organizations and agencies in the country;
(b) Issue a press release stating that the deployment of armed forces, including
armed private contractors, armed aircraft and armed vehicles, without an exemption
further to the provisions of resolution 2428 (2018), as renewed by resolution 2781
(2025), is a violation of the terms of the arms embargo first implemented by resolution
2428 (2018);

entities currently subject to targeted sanctions as listed in resolution 2206 (2015) and
consider additional listings, particularly in instances where there is substantial
credible evidence linking individuals to attacks on humanitarian operators further to
paragraph 7(c) of resolution 2206 (2015), or encouraging attacks on civilians by the
use of ethnic or tribal hate speech;

all investigations announced in 2025 and 2026 into the looting of humanitarian

Annex 31: Allocation of oil produced in South Sudan
All of South Sudan’s oil is produced by three joint-venture oil production companies. These are:
The Dar Petroleum Operating Company (DPOC), which produces Dar blend crude oil in Upper Nile State:
• China National Petroleum Corporation (CNPC): 41%
• Petronas: 40%
• Nile Petroleum Corporation (Nilepet): 8%
• SINOPEC: 6%
• TRI-OCEAN: 5%
The Greater Pioneer Operating Company (GPOC), which produces Nile blend crude oil in Unity State and the
Ruweng Administrative Area:
• CNPC: 40%
• Petronas: 30%
• ONGC Videsh: 25%
• Nilepet: 5%
The Sudd Petroleum Operating Company (SPOC), which produces Nile blend crude oil in Unity State and the
Ruweng Administrative Area:
• Petronas: 67.8%
• ONGC Videsh: 24.2%
• Nilepet: 8%
In August 2024, Petronas announced its departure from South Sudan’s oil industry. Its shareholding in the three
joint venture production companies is subject to an ongoing dispute. See the Panel’s report S/2025/442.
Each of these companies recoup both their costs and profits through an allocation of the oil that is produced. The
remaining oil is allocated to the Government of South Sudan. These allocations are based on Exploration and
Production Sharing Agreements and depend on the fields in which the oil is produced and current production
levels.

Annex 33: Process for the advance sale of oil by the Government of South Sudan
The Government of South Sudan primarily sells its share of the oil produced in South Sudan (see annex 31) to
international commodity traders. The government typically sells its cargos (600,000 barrels +/- 5%) three to nine
months before they are scheduled to be delivered, against an advance payment from such a trader. This system of
short-term advance payments can be distinguished from the longer-term loans the government has also obtained
from commodity traders, banks, and oil production companies. Such longer-term loans can have a horizon of several
years and are often not secured against the delivery of any specific or dated future cargos of oil.
Under the current system of shorter-term advance payments, commodity traders are typically contacted by a
representative of the government of South Sudan and offered a specific cargo of crude oil that is due be delivered
within three to nine months. The trader is offered to secure the allocation of the cargo, in advance, in exchange for
an advance payment to the government. As noted in the following annex, during 2025, these advance payments
were typically in the range of $20 to $30 million, but vary with the prevailing oil price.
In exchange for the advance payment, the trader will negotiate a discount on the value of the cargo of crude oil that
is eventually delivered. This discount is expressed in terms of a discount on the price of each barrel of oil that is
delivered. This price is, in turn, determined by an auction that is held closer to the time of delivery.
In exchange for making payment in advance, however, the buyer will typically require a larger discount than if
payment was made closer to the time of delivery, as advance payment carries additional costs and risks for the
trader. First, the trader either has to borrow the sum that is paid in advance, in which case there will be financing
costs; or the trader will have to forgo other possible returns on the funds. Second, making a payment several months
in advance also increases the risk that pipeline disruption, conflict, legal disputes, or other developments will
prevent or delay the delivery of the promised cargo. Traders either have to insure these risks, at a cost, or require
payment for bearing them.
The discount negotiated in exchange for advance payments is, therefore, typically substantially higher than the
discount that would be negotiated if the cargo was sold closer to the time of delivery. As such, the advance payment
mechanism is one that reduces the amount of revenue the Government of South Sudan generates from the sale of
its oil.
An advance payment agreement might therefore, as an example, specify that the trader will provide an advance
payment of $25,000,000 for a cargo (600,000 barrels +/- 5%) of Nile blend crude oil scheduled for delivery in
September of the current year, with a discount of $7 per barrel, applied against the winning bid when the cargo is
auctioned.
Closer to the time of the scheduled delivery of the cargo, all registered parties will be invited to bid on the cargo.
Their bids are also expressed as a discount against an agreed oil price benchmark, such as Dated Brent Crude, on
the day of delivery. The best bid may, for example, be expressed as Dated Brent Crude on the day of delivery minus
a discount of $2 per barrel.
Even when not sold in advance, both South Sudan’s Dar and Nile blends of crude oil have, historically, traded at a
small discount against the most commonly used international oil price benchmarks. This is ultimately determined
by market demand for crude oil with varying physical characteristics. In South Sudan’s case, the agreed discount
also covers additional costs imposed on the buyer by the Government of South Sudan. The e-Crude Accreditation

Permit, for example, charges buyers a fee equivalent to 0.3 percent of the value of the cargo, and is payable to
Crawford Capital Ltd., a company with ties to senior political elites.238
In a case where the cargo has already been sold in advance to another buyer, however, the auction is used solely to
set the price. The trader offering the best price is not awarded the cargo, as the cargo has already been awarded,
several months prior, to the buyer that was willing to offer payment in advance. Instead, as in the case of the
example above, this winning bid will be used to set a discount of $2 per barrel, to which the additional negotiated
discount of $7 per barrel will be added, for a total discount of $9 per barrel. The final value of the cargo will then
be expressed as, for example:
(620,000 barrels delivered) x (Dated Brent Crude oil benchmark price - $9) = total value of cargo
If this amount exceeds the value of the advance payment, the trader will pay the difference. Or, if the value of the
advance payment exceeds the value of the cargo, the trader is owed money by the Government of South Sudan.
