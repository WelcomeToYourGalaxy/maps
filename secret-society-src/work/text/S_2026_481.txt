Summary
The reporting period was marked by efforts undertaken by the Government of
the Central African Republic to implement the N’Djamena agreements of 19 April
2025 with Unité pour la paix en Centrafrique (UPC) and Retour, réclamation et
rehabilitation (3R), as well as to broaden the peace process to include the Mouvement
patriotique pour la Centrafrique (MPC), notably through the 19 November 2025
agreement, signed under Chadian auspices. These agreements effectively provided for
the groups’ return to the 2019 Political Agreement for Peace and Reconciliation in the
Central African Republic and the Luanda process, and contributed to a temporary
reduction in the activities of certain armed groups, as well as to the holding of
elections in December 2025.
Implementation of the agreements has formally commenced. Progress was most
visible in the disarmament and demobilization of UPC and 3R elements, with
approximately 1,300 total combatants, including 31 women, disarmed and
demobilized as at the time of writing. The Panel reviewed a total of 1,025 firearms
collected through the disarmament, demobilization and reintegration process, the
majority of which were functional. Nevertheless, key issues remain to be addressed,
particularly with regard to integration and reintegration modalities, including the
number of ex-combatants to be incorporated into the defence and security forces,
ranks to be granted, funding for socioeconomic reintegration and the timing of the
formal dissolution of the armed groups. Elements and networks affiliated with 3R,
UPC and MPC remained active to varying degrees.
The south-east remained a key armed-conflict hotspot. Azande Ani Kpi Gbe
(AAKG) was the most visible non-State armed actor in open clashes in Haut-Mbomou
Prefecture. The security situation deteriorated significantly around the December
2025 electoral period, with attacks on convoys, campaign teams, officials, positions
of the Armed Forces of the Central African Republic (FACA), electoral materials and
civilian infrastructure. The Panel found that the crisis in the south-east remains
complex, characterized by the presence of AAKG, partially integrated or disarmed
actors, local self-defence dynamics, cross-border linkages and a heavily militarized
State response.
The humanitarian and human rights situation in the south-east remained deeply
concerning. The Panel documented attacks on civilians, abductions, hostage-taking,
destruction of property, restrictions on humanitarian access and significant
displacement. Muslim and Fulani communities remained particularly exposed to
violent attacks and movement restrictions, while Azande civilians were caught up in
the crossfire. The Panel also received information that weapons continued to reach
the south-east from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and South Sudan, and from
within the Central African Republic.
In the north-east, the Panel found continued convergence among local armed
groups activity, transnational supply networks and spillover from the conflict in the
Sudan. Vakaga and Haute-Kotto Prefectures remained key areas of concern. Elements
linked to the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), including networks associated with
“General” Djazouli, remained active, while the Front populaire pour la renaissance
de la Centrafrique (FPRC), the Parti pour le rassemblement de la nation centrafricaine
(PRNC) and remnants of the Lord’s Resistance Army (LRA), along with Sudanese
armed elements, local self-defence groups and elements referred to as “Wagner
Noirs”, contributed to a complex and volatile security environment.

The north-east remained a key node for arms circulation. The Panel reviewed
evidence of armed elements linked to RSF operating in the tri-border area. The Panel

The Government of the Central African Republic pursued multiple regional and
international cooperation frameworks during the reporting period. Chad played a key
role as both a facilitator of armed group agreements and a security partner through

I. Background
A. Introduction
1. The present report, provided to the Security Council in accordance with
paragraph 1 of its resolution 2789 (2025), covers the period from the submission of
the Panel’s progress update in August 2025 until 15 April 2026.
2. On 29 July 2025, the Security Council adopted resolution 2789 (2025), by which
it extended the measures as set out or extended in paragraphs 2 and 4 of resolution
2745 (2024), including an arms embargo on armed and associated groups in the
Central African Republic, travel ban and asset freeze. By resolution 2789 (2025), the
Council also extended, until 31 August 2026, the mandate of the Panel of Experts as
set forth in paragraph 6 of resolution 2745 (2024) and reaffirmed paragraphs 1, 3, 5
and 7 through 12 of that resolution. The Panel was tasked with discharging its
mandate in accordance with paragraph 1 of resolution 2789 (2025).
3. Following the adoption by the Security Council of resolution 2789 (2025) in
July 2025, the Secretary-General appointed, on 25 February 2026, three individuals
with expertise in armed groups, arms and humanitarian affairs to serve on the Panel.
Two experts commenced their functions on 2 March 2026, while the third expert
began work on 6 April 2026. The Panel does not yet have a finance and natural
resources expert, but expects one to be appointed shortly, while the regional expert
position was recently abolished.
4. In its resolution 2789 (2025), the Security Council requested the Panel of
experts to provide to the Council, after discussion with the Committee, a midterm and
a final report no later than 31 January and 15 June 2026, respectively. Given the
limited time frame, the Panel pursued several lines of investigation and included in
the present report only those findings that could be assessed and verified, in
accordance with established standards of work, within the time available.
B. Field visits, and cooperation with other stakeholders
and institutions
5. Between March and April 2026, the Panel conducted one field visit to the
Central African Republic, during which it visited Bangui, and Zemio and Obo in
Haut-Mbomou Prefecture (south-east of the Central African Republic), as well as
Birao in Vakaga Prefecture (north-east of the Central African Republic). The Panel
had envisaged conducting a visit to Chad in April 2026; however, the visit could not
take place during the reporting period. The Panel remains hopeful that such a visit
may be undertaken in the near future.
6. The Panel had exchanges with other United Nations panels and groups of
experts established by the Security Council, in particular the Group of Experts on the
Democratic Republic of Congo, as well as the panels of experts on the Sudan and
South Sudan.
7. The Panel of Experts pursuant to resolution 2745 (2024) expresses its gratitude
to the Government of the Central African Republic and the United Nations
Multidimensional Integrated Stabilization Mission in the Central African Republic
(MINUSCA) for their continued support and cooperation.

C. Methodology
8. In conducting its investigations, the Panel complied with the best practices and
methods recommended by the Informal Working Group of the Security Council on
General Issues of Sanctions (see S/2006/997). The Panel maintained the highest
achievable standard of proof.
9. The Panel relied on corroborated evidence and adhered to its standards in
respect of the opportunity to reply. Further details on methodology can be found in
annex 1. The Panel has maintained transparency, objectivity, impartiality and
independence in its investigations.
II. Implementation of the peace agreement between the
Government of the Central African Republic and Unité pour
la paix en Centrafrique; Retour, réclamation et rehabilitation;
and Mouvement patriotique pour la Centrafrique
A. Overall assessment of the implementation of the 2025
peace agreements
The 2025 peace agreements and the 2019 Political Agreement for Peace and
Reconciliation in the Central African Republic
10. On 19 April 2025, following months of talks, the Government of the Central
African Republic and leaders of two prominent armed groups belonging to the
Coalition des patriotes pour le changement (CPC),1 namely Ali Darassa (CFi.015) of
Unité pour la paix en Centrafrique (UPC) and Sembé Bobbo of Retour, réclamation
et rehabilitation (3R), signed peace agreements under the auspices of the Government
of Chad in N’Djamena (see annex 2).
11. On 10 July 2025, a symbolic ceremony was held in Bangui, attended by national
authorities and representatives of armed groups, including Mr. Darassa and
Mr. Bobbo, marking the effective beginning of the disarmament, demobilization and
reintegration process for UPC and 3R elements.2
12. On 19 November 2025, the Government of the Central African Republic also
signed a peace agreement with the leader of the Mouvement patriotique pour la
Centrafrique (MPC), Mahamat Al-Khatim, in N’Djamena, where he had been in exile
since 2021. The Panel is still seeking to obtain a copy of the document (see annex 3).
13. The 19 April and 19 November 2025 peace agreements effectively meant the
return of UPC, 3R and MPC to the 2019 Political Agreement for Peace and
Reconciliation in the Central African Republic, following the groups’ exit in 2021
with the formation of CPC and their joining of the rebel coalition.
14. The N’Djamena agreements of 19 April 2025 are framed as instruments for the re-
entry of armed groups into the peace process rather than constituting a stand-alone peace
framework.3 These agreements expressly provide that armed groups will “reintegrate
the [Political Agreement for Peace and Reconciliation] and the Luanda process” and
reproduce several core principles of the Political Agreement, including the cessation

15. Moreover, the N’Djamena agreements are narrower and more transactional.
They provide for the participation of 3R and UPC in senior Government positions
within national institutions and the Administration, as well as appointments within
the military chain of command and the defence and security forces, based on
competence criteria and consultation with the leadership of the respective armed
groups. This approach goes beyond the general inclusion language of the Political
Agreement for Peace and Reconciliation and creates potential ambiguity around the
nature, scope and number of positions to be granted.
16. The agreements envisage the cantonment and registration of armed group
elements, disarmament following registration, the training of eligible combatants
following disarmament, integration into the Armed Forces of the Central African
Republic (FACA) and other defence and security forces at the end of training, and the
demobilization of ineligible combatants in line with the Political Agreement for Peace
and Reconciliation. While this sequencing is more detailed than that outlined in the
Agreement, it may also create expectations of integration that exceed existing
capacity, notably in terms of resources.
17. Both agreements are broadly consistent with the Political Agreement for Peace
and Reconciliation in their stated objectives of facilitating the re-entry of armed
groups into the peace process. However, they introduce more explicit and operational
commitments on political and administrative appointments and security sector
integration, while creating a tripartite follow-up mechanism led by Chad which differs
from the Agreement monitoring framework. The primary risks are therefore not
formal contradiction but implementation ambiguity. Challenges include the
sequencing and funding of disarmament, demobilization and reintegration; the
number of combatants to be integrated into the defence and security forces and their
military rank, and the required resources; the options available to ineligible
combatants; the funding of socioeconomic reintegration; and the allocation of
political appointments.
18. For example, there appears to be a misalignment in the understanding of the
sequencing. The dissolution of armed groups is said to have already taken place,
whereas the armed groups consider that the dissolution will be effective only at the
very end of the disarmament, demobilization and reintegration process, once all
combatants have been disarmed, demobilized and reintegrated with appropriate
military ranks and all political appointments have been made.4
19. On the political front, the armed groups have had several individuals appointed
in key adviser roles in the executive and legislative branches in Bangui, and other
senior-level appointments are expected in the forthcoming government.5
20. In the Panel’s assessment, the implementation of the 2025 peace agreements
between the Government and the leaders of UPC, 3R and MPC remained uneven
across the groups, with the agreement with MPC yet to be officially launched.

Implementation of the disarmament, demobilization and reintegration
components of the 2025 peace agreements
21. The most visible area of progress has been the disarmament and demobilization
component involving 3R and UPC. According to information gathered by the Panel
during its field visit in March and April 2026, progress in the disarmament,
demobilization and reintegration process was most tangible between July and
December 2025. Interlocutors from the political and peace process architecture
referred to there having been approximately 1,300 combatants, including 31 women,
disarmed and demobilized.8 The groups cited as participating in the broader process
were predominantly UPC and 3R, along with a few MPC combatants, with additional
participation of factions linked to the Front populaire pour la renaissance de la
Centrafrique (FPRC)/Séléka rénovée pour la paix et la justice, and some anti-balaka
elements. As of mid-April 2026, progress in disarmament, demobilization and
reintegration continued to be presented as one of the main pillars of implementation of
the Political Agreement for Peace and Reconciliation and the 2025 peace agreements.
22. As a central element of the implementation of the peace agreements, the
disarmament, demobilization and reintegration process is attempting to deliver peace
dividends while also having significant implications for the national security sector.
Several interlocutors expressed concerns about a perceived lack of clarity on exactly
how ex-combatants are to be absorbed within the defence and security forces of the
Central African Republic, including the National Police and the Gendarmerie.9 One
interlocutor in particular referred to the “necessity to avoid watering the seeds of
continued insecurity and instability by now building a professional army of the
people”.10
23. Nevertheless, information gathered by the Panel also points to shortcomings in
the implementation architecture. The Panel was repeatedly informed of the limited
visibility into the reintegration phase and inadequate clarity regarding the integration
process. Interlocutors reported that the disarmament, demobilization and
reintegration process was paused on 13 December 2025 in the run-up to the electoral
period, initially for a period that was expected to last three weeks, but that activities
resumed from the beginning of March 2026 and are expected to accelerate following
the April 2026 voting round.11 Several interlocutors also highlighted technical
challenges, including insufficient resources and limited capacity, which remain key
obstacles, and that the issue of integration and reintegration requires urgent attention
to prevent a rollback of the progress to date.
24. The Panel also received repeated indications that the integration component was
marked by uncertainty over the number of ex-combatants to be absorbed, the ranks to
be granted and the institutional actors responsible for the process.12 According to
various sources, senior military officials were not aligned on the number of elements

B. Review of arms and ammunition collected through disarmament,
demobilization and reintegration following the 2025
peace agreements
32. The Panel conducted a review of arms and ammunition collected through the
formal national disarmament, demobilization, reintegration and repatriation
programme in the Central African Republic. This review draws upon a verified data
set comprising 1,025 individual weapons records collected between July 2025 and
March 2026, containing detailed information on the location of collection, weapon
type, calibre, number, provenance and functionality (see annex 4). The data set further
identifies the armed group associated with each collected item, thereby providing
insight into the relative participation of various factions in disarmament initiatives.
33. As at the date of writing, among the 1,025 firearms collected, 976 weapons
(95.2%) were assessed as functional at the time of handover, 46 weapons (4.5%) were
classified as non-functional, obsolete or requiring repair, and 3 weapons were
recorded as “undetermined” (see annex 5). This high proportion of functional
weapons aligns with standard disarmament, demobilization and reintegration
protocols whereby only functional weapons are meant to qualify for the process.

III. Situation in the south-east of the Central African Republic
A. Security and armed group dynamics
40. The south-east of the Central African Republic remained a key armed conflict
hotspot during the reporting period. Incident reporting from September 2025 to March
2026 shows a clear concentration of armed confrontations in Haut-Mbomou and
adjacent areas, with Zemio, Mboki, Bambouti, Obo, Djema and Dembia featuring
repeatedly.29 The period was characterized above all by the operational prominence
of AAKG, which became the most visible non-State armed actor in open clashes. The
material reviewed by the Panel suggests that the south-east experienced a marked
militarization during the electoral period and in its immediate aftermath.
41. Field interlocutors described AAKG as having been weakened in comparison
with its earlier configuration, while remaining active across several red zones in Haut-
Mbomou. Bambouti, Mboki, Djema, areas around Zemio and the Guénékoumba area
were all cited as continued centres of AAKG presence.30 Several interlocutors also
emphasized that Bambouti appeared to operate with a distinct dynamic of its own. At
the same time, some AAKG members were said to be interested in disarmament,
demobilization and reintegration, while others sought to remain in the bush for fear
of reprisals.31
42. The Panel heard from various sources, including some AAKG members, about
the group’s different demands, of which three were consistent requests, namely: the
departure from Haut-Mbomou of Russian instructors as a condition for AAKG

Célestin Bakayogo-Leman and Elie Gomengue (aka Elie Pasteur); and a request for a
dialogue process with the authorities.32 The Panel observed that the Government sent
a delegation to Haut-Mbomou in early April 2026 to prepare the ground for a potential
dialogue.33

the towns and surrounding areas.34 Second, the group appears able to exploit cross-
border community networks with the Democratic Republic of Congo and South
Sudan. Third, State authority remains in its infancy outside a limited number of rural
centres. Multiple interlocutors stressed that the extension of State authority and
services in Haut-Mbomou remained very limited, particularly in remote areas along

56. The humanitarian and human rights situation in the south-east remained deeply

attacks affecting civilian infrastructure and severe restrictions on humanitarian

Targeting of civilians and ethnic- or religious-based attacks
58. Field interlocutors consistently described a climate of fear among Muslim
communities in and around Zemio. They reported that Muslims not involved in
hostilities were unable to circulate safely along the axes, access their fields or obtain
schooling and healthcare, and, in some cases, they have expressed the wish to be

60. The current field material suggests that these earlier patterns of communal
targeting continued in 2025 and 2026. On 30 November 2025, AAKG elements
reportedly killed five Fulani civilians near Mboki, and contributed to heightened
intercommunal violence.53 On 16 December 2025, on the Zemio-Dembia axis near

killed, although the Panel was not able, on the basis of the material reviewed for the

61. The Panel was also informed about the challenges of official attempts to identify

members described scarification as a general cultural practice, security actors are said
to be using scarification as evidence of AAKG affiliation and consider it a part of
their initiation rituals.55 This exposes civilians and non-combatants to suspicion and
potential targeting.

C. Humanitarian situation
87. The humanitarian and human rights situation in the north-east remained
concerning throughout the reporting period. Since 2024, the Panel has documented
how the conflict in the Sudan has driven refugee flows into the Central African
Republic, strained relations between refugees and host communities in Vakaga
Prefecture and complicated food security and humanitarian delivery.96 Interlocutors
met by the Panel in April 2026 stated that the population of the Korsi neighbourhood
of Birao had grown considerably, outnumbering the local population. According to
official figures, since 2023 more than 35,000 Sudanese refugees have fled to the
Central African Republic, with approximately 22,000 residing in Korsi. In addition,
over 6,900 former Central African refugees have returned from the Sudan.97 These
trends underscore the sustained demographic and humanitarian pressure created by
the conflict in the Sudan.98
88. Information available to the Panel indicates that civilians in Vakaga and adjacent
border areas continued to face grave abuses at the hands of RSF or other Sudanese
armed elements, including killings, the burning of houses, displacement, abduction
and torture.99 Civilians continued to be directly exposed to cross-border violence, as
illustrated by the killing of farmers on the Bili-Bili–Am Dafok axis and reported
reprisals against women on 15 April 2026 (see para. 79 above).

Annex 1: Methodology
1. The Panel ensured compliance with the methodological standards recommended by the Informal Working Group of the
Security Council on General Issues of Sanctions (S/2006/997). These standards call for reliance on verified, genuine
documents and concrete evidence and on-site observations by the experts, including taking photographs, wherever possible.
When physical inspection is not possible, the Panel will seek to corroborate information using multiple, independent sources
to appropriately meet the highest achievable standard, placing a higher value on statements by principal actors and first-
hand witnesses to events.
2. The Panel uses satellite imagery of CAR procured by the United Nations from private providers to support investigations,
as well as open-source imagery, where relevant. Commercial databases recording aviation and maritime data will be
referenced as required. Public statements by officials and armed group representatives issued as press releases
(communiqués), audio clips or media appearances were verified by the Panel. Any mobile phone records, where relevant,
from service providers are also accepted as factual. While the Panel wishes to be as transparent as possible, in situations
where identifying sources would risk exposing them or others to unacceptable safety risks, the Panel has not included
identifying information in the Panel report but has placed the relevant evidence in United Nations secure archives.
3. The Panel reviewed and included reference to social media posts, where relevant to its investigations. However, evidence
and information gleaned from social media was only used in Panel report where it could be corroborated using multiple
independent or technical sources, including eyewitnesses, to meet the appropriate evidentiary standard.
4. The spelling of toponyms within the Central African Republic and broader region often depends on the ethnicity of the
source or the quality of transliteration. The Panel has adopted a consistent approach in reports. All major locations in CAR
and other regional countries are spelled or referenced as per the UN Geographical Information System (GIS) map.
5. The Panel has placed importance on the rule of consensus among the Panel members and agreed that, if differences and/or
reservations arise during the development of reports, it would only adopt the text, conclusions and recommendations by a
majority of four out of the five members including the coordinator. In the event of a recommendation for designation of an
individual or a group, such recommendation would be done based on unanimity.
6. The Panel is committed to impartiality in investigating incidents of non-compliance by any party.
7. The Panel is equally committed to the highest degree of fairness and will endeavor to provide the opportunity to reply to
Member States, entities and individuals involved in the majority of incidents that are currently in investigation. Their
response will be taken into consideration in the Panel’s findings for the final report.
