## Multi-country surveys by institution

### Finding 1: Police and elected officials tie for first worldwide
- **Figure:** "Police and elected officials" were tied highest, at 36% seen as highly corrupt. Highest group by region: Asia Pacific, police 39%; Sub-Saharan Africa, police 47%; Europe and Central Asia, elected representatives 31%; Americas, police and elected representatives 46% each; Middle East and North Africa, elected representatives, tax officials and government officials 45% each. Nearly one in four people paid a bribe for a public service in the previous 12 months.
- **Measures:** public perception, plus self-reported bribery. 162,136 adults in 119 countries, territories and regions, March 2014 to January 2017. The pages give no figures for judges, presidents' offices, local councillors, business executives or religious leaders, and none for bribery by service.
- **Source:** Global Corruption Barometer: citizens' voices from around the world, and the 2017 results analysis, Transparency International, 2017. https://www.transparency.org/en/news/global-corruption-barometer-citizens-voices-from-around-the-world and https://www.transparency.org/en/gcb/global/global-corruption-barometer-2017/results/analysis
- **Directness:** weak proxy. It is the widest ranking found, but it measures perception of corruption.

### Finding 2: Africa, where police lead by a clear margin
- **Figure:** Share saying "most" or "all" are involved in corruption: police 48%; Members of Parliament 38%; civil servants 37%; judges and magistrates 35%; tax officials 35%; Office of the Presidency 35%; local government councillors 33%. Share of service users who paid a bribe: police assistance 35%; other police encounters 33%; government identity document 25%; medical care 20%; public school services 19%.
- **Measures:** 26,777 face-to-face interviews in 18 countries, with each country weighted equally. Afrobarometer Round 8, August 2019 to March 2020. Local councillors were not asked about in Angola. Bribery figures exclude people with no contact with the service.
- **Source:** Afrobarometer Dispatch 421, Afrobarometer. The URL carries the date 24 Jan 20, which conflicts with fieldwork running to March 2020, so the publication date is unsettled. https://www.afrobarometer.org/wp-content/uploads/migrated/files/publications/Dispatches/ad421-africans_see_growing_corruption-afrobarometer_dispatch-bh-24jan20.pdf
- **Directness:** weak proxy for perception. Bribery by service is a close proxy for petty corruption, with the citizen rather than an organised party as the payer.

### Finding 3: The EU reverses the order, with police near the bottom
- **Figure:** Share saying most or all are corrupt: Members of Parliament 28%; business executives 25%; president or prime minister's office 23%; bankers 23%; national government officials 22%; local government representatives 19%; NGOs 16%; judges and magistrates 14%; police 11%. Bribery: 7% of people who used at least one of six public services paid a bribe. Personal connections were used by 33% of service users, or 28% of all people. The share saying government is run by a few private interests is given as 53% in the text, while the chart labels it 63%.
- **Measures:** 40,600 adults in 27 EU states, October to December 2020.
- **Source:** Global Corruption Barometer EU 2021, Transparency International, 2021. https://www.transparency.de/fileadmin/Redaktion/Aktuelles/2021/GCB-2021_Report.pdf
- **Directness:** weak proxy. This is the main evidence against "police first", and the only survey read that rates companies and bankers alongside officials across many countries.

### Finding 4: Pacific, Fiji reading, including the military
- **Figure:** Fiji: Members of Parliament 17%; police 17%; business executives 15%; senior government leaders 12%; civil servants and companies extracting natural resources 11%; military leaders 10%; civil society and NGO workers 10%; community leaders 9%; bankers 7%. The article defines the measure ("most or all people") only for Parliament.
- **Measures:** Global Corruption Barometer Pacific 2021. More than 6,000 adults in 10 countries and territories, February to March 2021. Only Fiji's results were read, as reported in the press.
- **Source:** "Report surveys public perception of corrupt institutions; MPs and Police top list", Fiji Times, 17 November 2021. https://www.fijitimes.com.fj/report-surveys-public-perception-of-corrupt-institutions-mps-and-police-top-list/
- **Directness:** weak proxy, for one country. It is the only survey read here that rates military leaders.

## Single-country readings

### Finding 5: Philippines, where customs stands far below police
- **Figure:** SWS net sincerity ratings in fighting corruption: Bureau of Customs -55; House of Representatives -25; Philippine National Police -16; Bureau of Internal Revenue -4; Armed Forces +4; trial courts +6; Supreme Court +42; Office of the President +54; Securities and Exchange Commission +63. The article calls Customs the most corrupt agency.
- **Measures:** executives' ratings of each agency's sincerity, not corruption itself. 966 companies in seven areas, face to face, 14 November 2014 to 12 May 2015.
- **Source:** "Customs most corrupt gov't agency—SWS", Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2015 (year inferred from the survey dates). https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/717153/customs-most-corrupt-govt-agency-sws
- **Directness:** weak proxy, for one country. It does show that a survey of firms can rank customs well above police, and it rates the regulator and president as good.

### Finding 6: Mexico, firms' bribe requests
- **Figure:** Firms with at least one bribe payment request: 15.8%. Expected to give gifts to get a construction permit: 8.2%. Expected to give gifts in meetings with tax officials: 6.9%.
- **Measures:** owners and top managers at 1,322 firms, March to October 2023. Figures for other types of official, and all world averages, were not read.
- **Source:** Enterprise Surveys, Mexico 2023 country profile, World Bank, 2023. https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/content/dam/enterprisesurveys/documents/country/Mexico-2023.pdf
- **Directness:** close proxy for company-to-official bribery in one country. The firm is the payer, but this is mostly small-scale extraction, not steering.

## Detected cases

### Finding 7: Who received foreign bribes, by type of official
- **Figure:** Employees of state-owned enterprises 27% of cases; customs officials 11%; health officials 7%; defence officials 6%; heads of state and ministers 5% of cases but 11% of total bribes. Bribes averaged 10.9% of transaction value and 34.5% of profits, equal to $13.8 million per bribe. Intermediaries were involved in 3 of 4 cases. By sector: extractive 19%, construction 15%, transportation and storage 15%, information and communication 10%. The listed recipient types add to only 56%; the rest were not given.
- **Measures:** concluded foreign bribery cases, "more than 400", from February 1999 to June 2014. Only detected cases from countries that enforce the Convention.
- **Source:** "OECD: an analysis of the crime of bribery of foreign public officials", Corruptie.org, summarising the OECD Foreign Bribery Report. Secondary source, year not read. https://www.corruptie.org/oecd-an-analysis-of-the-crime-of-bribery-of-foreign-public-officials/
- **Directness:** close proxy. It is the only source read where an outside party (a company) pays and the official is sorted by type. It covers one kind of outside party only.

### Finding 8: Corruption inside companies
- **Figure:** "Nearly half the cases in our study (48%) involved some form of corruption." Median loss for corruption: $200,000 (662 cases). By region, the highest is Southern Asia at 74% and the lowest is United States and Canada at 35%. By department: purchasing 79%; executive and upper management 65%; sales 49%; administrative support 46%; finance 45%; operations 44%; customer service 40%; accounting 36%. 54% of frauds involved more than one perpetrator. 20% of perpetrators showed an "unusually close association with vendor/customer".
- **Measures:** 1,921 occupational fraud cases investigated January 2022 to September 2023, in 138 countries and territories. Corruption here covers conflicts of interest, bribery, illegal gratuities, extortion, kickbacks and bid rigging. The report gives no separate rate for these, no figure for outsiders colluding with insiders, and no corruption split by rank. Investigated cases only.
- **Source:** Report to the Nations 2024, Association of Certified Fraud Examiners, 2024, copy hosted by Ivey Business School. https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/media/kjljj5cy/2024-report-to-the-nations.pdf
- **Directness:** close proxy for the company category. The purchasing figure fits vendors paying insiders, but that link is my inference. Multiple perpetrators may all be insiders.

### Finding 9: Italy's formal findings of mafia infiltration in local councils
- **Figure:** 169 municipal councils dissolved for mafia infiltration, 2010 to 2022. Calabria 75 (44%); Sicilia 36 (21%); Campania 30 (18%); Puglia 18 (11%); Lazio 3; Piemonte 2; Basilicata, Emilia-Romagna, Liguria, Lombardia and Valle d'Aosta 1 each. The share of all Italian municipalities was not given.
- **Measures:** dissolutions decided after a police investigation, a Prefetto assessment, a Ministry of the Interior decision and a presidential decree.
- **Source:** Lavezzi and Quatrosi, "Go Green Without the Mafia! Dissolution of Infiltrated City Councils and Environmental Policy", arXiv, 2025 (year inferred from the arXiv number). https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18410v1
- **Directness:** direct, and the only direct count read here. It covers one country, one outside party and one level of government, and counts only what the state found and chose to act on.

### Finding 10: Mexico's police vetting, with failure not tied to crime
- **Figure:** 2019 tests: 26,700 officers failed control and confidence tests, and 392 lost their jobs ("Just 1.4%"). National average fully certified: 43%, from Querétaro 96.4% to Jalisco 2.7%. 2010: about 3,200 federal officers fired, nearly a tenth of the force, for "substandard performance"; 465 charged with breaking the law; 1,020 facing discipline after failing screening, with suspected criminal links among the reasons alongside medical problems. No count of confirmed criminal ties. September 2010 headcount: 427,354 active police, with 206,298 tested.
- **Measures:** one country. Certification requires, among other things, no links to organised crime, but no source reports how many failed for that reason. The number tested in 2019 was not given, so no failure rate can be stated.
- **Sources:**
  - "Only 1.4% of cops let go after flunking evaluations", Mexico News Daily, 10 February 2020, citing SNSP via El Universal. The page title was not confirmed; the URL says "only-1-4". https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/only-1-4-of-cops-let-go-after-flunking-evaluations
  - "Mexico fires 3,200 federal police officers", Boulder Weekly, carrying the Los Angeles Times, 2010. https://boulderweekly.com/?p=3284
  - "52% of the national police force in Mexico has not been submitted to 'confidence' tests", Justice in Mexico, 29 September 2010. https://justiceinmexico.org/52-of-the-national-police-force-in-mexico-has-not-been-submitted-to-confidence-tests/
- **Directness:** weak proxy. It shows a large vetting problem but cannot be read as an infiltration rate.

### Finding 11: China's party discipline totals
- **Figure:** About 110,000 Communist Party officials faced disciplinary action in 2023, with cases up 13%. 45 senior officials investigated, a record by the newspaper's own tally. Nine senior military officials were dismissed, and Foreign Minister Qin Gang and Defence Minister Li Shangfu were removed. No split by rank or institution.
- **Measures:** enforcement counts from the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection. Whether an outside party was served is not stated.
- **Source:** "China's anti-corruption watchdog says 110,000 Communist Party officials faced disciplinary action last year", South China Morning Post, 2024. https://www.scmp.com/news/china/politics/article/3250206/chinas-anti-corruption-watchdog-says-110000-communist-party-officials-faced-disciplinary-action-last
- **Directness:** weak proxy. It may be mostly self-dealing, and purges can be political.

### Finding 12: Central banks, a handful of named cases
- **Figure:** Six officials in five countries: governors in Poland (questioned, not charged), Latvia (charged with taking bribes from a bank in exchange for help with regulatory issues) and Slovenia (criminal complaint over a bailout); deputy governors in Romania (arrested) and Bulgaria (two charged, with 16 others, over failing to supervise a collapsed bank). The article reports no convictions, and those named deny wrongdoing where the article records a response.
- **Measures:** one article, Eastern and Central Europe only, about 2019. It offers no worldwide count and no rate.
- **Source:** "Crime Probes Haunt Central Bankers From Black Sea to Baltics", Bloomberg, about 2019. https://www.bloombergquint.com/global-economics/criminal-probes-haunt-central-bankers-from-black-sea-to-baltics
- **Directness:** weak proxy. Only Latvia alleges an outside party paying for favours, and these are allegations.

### Finding 13: FIFA's executive committee, one body at very high saturation
- **Figure:** The Associated Press names 24 people who sat on FIFA's executive committee since 2010, with bans, indictments, guilty pleas or investigations. Many were accused or under investigation, not found guilty; a few were sanctioned over conduct during inquiries, not for taking money. The committee's total membership over the period was not given, so no share can be stated.
- **Measures:** one international sports body, to 3 December 2015.
- **Source:** "Tainted members of FIFA's executive committee since 2010", Sports Illustrated / AP, 2015. https://www.si.com/soccer/2015/12/03/ap-soc-fifa-tainted-executive-committee
- **Directness:** close proxy for one body. It is also a poor stand-in for international bodies generally, since it is not an intergovernmental body and much of the conduct is members selling votes or self-dealing.

## Reading

1. Across surveys, police and elected legislators come out worst most often: tied worldwide, police clearly ahead in Africa, and legislators ahead in the EU and joint first in Fiji.
2. This is not universal, since EU respondents put police last of nine at 11% and put business executives and bankers above judges, officials and local government.
3. Surveys overstate bodies people meet in daily life, because a traffic stop is visible and a captured regulator is not, so these rankings mostly track petty contact.
4. Where the payer is a company, the ranking changes: state-owned enterprise staff lead at 27% of OECD cases and customs follows at 11%, while police are not among the types read, and heads of state and ministers take 11% of bribe value from 5% of cases.
5. The only direct count read is Italy's 169 councils, which shows local government can be formally found infiltrated but says nothing about other institutions or countries.
6. Guess: counted in people compromised, security and border bodies (police especially, then customs) hold the largest share, about 35-50%, because police alone outnumber legislators by hundreds to one in the ten countries read; companies follow at about 15-30%, since ACFE's corruption share and the purchasing figure point to outside vendors, and legislators, parties and local office together at about 15-25%.
7. Guess: judges and prosecutors take about 5-10%, with ministers, heads of state, central banks and international bodies each under 5% by people, but much larger when counted by money and power steered.
8. These percentages are my judgement and not calculated from anything read, so I am sure of the broad order by people only at low to moderate confidence, and of the shares at low confidence.
9. The biggest gap is the step from corruption to infiltration: no source shows how much of this conduct served an outside party, and that share probably differs by institution, for example lower for self-dealing Chinese officials than for bribed purchasing managers (also a guess).
10. Also missing are any measures for intelligence services and prisons, all world staff counts other than parliamentarians, and rates for international bodies, whose only evidence here is one sports federation.
