## A. How common bribery is among firms

### A1. What the main bribery indicator counts
- **Finding:** "The percent of firms experiencing at least one bribe payment request across 6 public transactions dealing with utilities access, permits, licenses, and taxes." The six are electrical connection, water connection, construction permit, import licence, operating licence, tax inspection or meeting. Refusing to answer counts as "yes".
- **Measures:** Registered formal firms with 5 or more employees; data for around 154 economies, each surveyed roughly every 4 to 5 years. Page gives no world or regional figure. Updated 31 March 2023.
- **Source:** Metadata for SDG indicator 16.5.2, UN Statistics Division, 2023. https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/metadata/files/Metadata-16-05-02.pdf
- **Directness:** Weak proxy. It records officials asking firms for payment, which may be extortion, not a firm getting inside government. It leaves out contracts.

### A2. Country range of bribery incidence
- **Figure:** Highest, Syrian Arab Republic, 69.60%. Lowest, Estonia, 0.00%. No years given.
- **Measures:** Same indicator as A1, latest values by country.
- **Source:** IndexMundi, reproducing the World Bank indicator IC.FRM.BRIB.ZS. https://www.indexmundi.com/facts/indicators/IC.FRM.BRIB.ZS
- **Directness:** Weak proxy, and the values are undated.

### A3. Regional shares expected to give "gifts"
- **Figures:** South Asia: almost half of firms expected to give "gifts" to secure a government contract; 1 in 4 to get an operating licence. East Asia and Pacific: 1 in 3 to get a construction permit. Sub-Saharan Africa: 27% expected to give "gifts" to public officials "to get things done". Middle East and North Africa: over 50% call corruption a major constraint.
- **Measures:** Enterprise Survey results as described in a World Bank blog: "more than 13,000 firms in 135 countries" as summarised (this firm count looks low for 135 countries and should be checked). Survey years not stated. No world figure.
- **Source:** "How corruption affects businesses around the world in 5 charts", World Bank Blogs, 7 Dec 2015. https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/governance/how-corruption-affects-businesses-around-world-5-charts
- **Directness:** Close proxy for the contract figure, since a contract gift buys a decision. Weak proxy for licences and permits. The numbers are about expected payments, not admitted ones.

### A4. Graft experienced per request, Africa and Latin America
- **Figure:** About 1 in 10 (9.8%) across 9,519 requests for public services; Africa above 19%, Latin America below 7%. Licensing overall 11.3% pooled. Gifts averaged 2.1% of firm sales in the 2006 Enterprise Surveys data. Contract corruption is excluded from the index.
- **Measures:** 33 economies (18 African, 15 Latin American), nearly 10,000 firms.
- **Source:** González, López-Córdova and Valladares, "The Incidence of Graft on Developing-Country Firms", World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 4394, Nov 2007. https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/33d68334-9954-54e9-b40e-20434aa77141/download
- **Directness:** Weak proxy. Small payments for services, mostly not steering of decisions.

## B. Firms buying the rules (state capture)

### B1. Hellman, Jones, Kaufmann: definition and grouping
- **Finding:** State capture is "shaping the formation of the basic rules of the game (laws, rules, decrees, and regulations) through illicit and nontransparent private payments to public officials". High-capture countries, where more than a quarter of firms report a significant impact: Azerbaijan, Bulgaria, Croatia, Georgia, Kyrgyzstan, Latvia, Moldova, Romania, Russia, Slovak Republic, Ukraine. Low-capture: Albania, Armenia, Belarus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Kazakhstan, Lithuania, Poland, Slovenia, Uzbekistan. Captor firms are "a minority in all countries", with no exact share legible. So 11 of 22 countries were above the one-quarter line.
- **Measures:** 1999 BEEPS survey, June to August 1999, 22 transition economies in Eastern Europe and Central Asia. Six activities asked about, including "sale of Parliamentary votes on laws to private interests". Per-country table values were not legible in this copy.
- **Source:** Hellman and Jones paper on capture, hosted PDF (Montclair State course copy), year not confirmed on the summary. https://msuweb.montclair.edu/%7Elebelp/PSC643IntPolEcon/JonesStateCorruption.pdf
- **Directness:** Direct measure of perceived effect, one region, 1999. It counts firms hurt by the sale of laws, not firms buying them or laws sold.

### B2. Hellman and Schankerman: country extremes
- **Figure:** Over 30% of firms in Azerbaijan, Moldova, Russia and Ukraine report a significant impact from the sale of parliamentary legislation or presidential decrees; fewer than 10% in Armenia, Belarus, Hungary, Slovenia and Uzbekistan. "Only a small proportion" of affected firms report making such payments.
- **Measures:** 1999 BEEPS, over 3,000 firms in 20 countries, 125 to 150 per country with more in Russia (550) and Ukraine (250).
- **Source:** Hellman and Schankerman, "Intervention, corruption and capture", Economics of Transition 8(3), 2000. https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mnaoi/page4/POLI227/files/page1_27.pdf
- **Directness:** Direct for that region and year. The "small proportion" finding matters: a few firms can affect many.

### B3. Hellman and Kaufmann: who gains
- **Finding:** In high-capture economies, captor firms grow more than twice as fast as other firms and are more than five times as likely to report improved security of property rights; in low-capture economies they show few advantages. Foreign investors with local partners and domestic headquarters are nearly twice as likely to engage in capture as domestically owned firms. Six institutions measured: parliament, executive apparatus, criminal courts, civil courts, central bank, political parties. Values are in charts not in the text.
- **Measures:** Nearly 4,000 firms, 22 economies, late 1999.
- **Source:** Hellman and Kaufmann, "Confronting the Challenge of State Capture in Transition Economies", Finance & Development, IMF, Sept 2001. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2001/09/hellman.htm
- **Directness:** Close proxy. The six institutions map onto this project's categories, but I did not get a share for each.

### B4. Which industries (expert perception)
- **Finding:** Sectors most likely to bribe officials: public works and construction; real estate and property development; oil and gas; heavy manufacturing; mining. Least likely: information technology, fisheries, banking and finance. Most likely to engage in state capture (rules influenced by payments, legal or illegal): public works and construction; oil and gas; mining; real estate. Banking and finance ranks considerably worse on capture than on bribing officials. Country scores (10 is clean): Belgium and Canada 8.8, United States 8.1, Brazil 7.4, India 6.8, Mexico 6.6, China 6.5, Russia 5.9.
- **Measures:** 2,742 senior business executives in 26 countries rating firms from 22 leading exporters, on 19 sectors.
- **Source:** Transparency International, 2008 Bribe Payers Index press release, 9 Dec 2008. https://www.transparency.org/en/press/20081209-ti-report-emerging-economic-giants-show-high-levels-of-corporate-b
- **Directness:** Close proxy for where it concentrates, not how much. Perception only. Its capture measure includes legal payments, which this brief excludes.

### B5. Firms with politicians inside them
- **Figure:** 532 of 19,884 listed firms in 42 countries are politically connected: 2.68% of listed corporations, 7.76% of world market capitalisation. Connected means a large shareholder (10% or more of votes) or top director is a member of parliament or government, or is closely related to a top politician or party. Highest: Indonesia, 22.08% of listed firms including close relationships. Lowest, 0%: Argentina, Brazil, Czech Republic, New Zealand, Norway, Peru, Poland, South Africa, Venezuela. Connections are more common where corruption is perceived as high, significant for some measures but not all, causality not established.
- **Measures:** Listed firms only; a draft of Faccio's paper (year not confirmed on the summary).
- **Source:** Faccio, "Politically Connected Firms" (draft), University of Michigan workshop copy. https://www.bus.umich.edu/pdf/mitsui/workshopdocs/facciopaperrvs.pdf
- **Directness:** Close proxy. It is a count of ties, with no test that anything was steered. **Cuts against easy reading:** the 0% for Brazil and South Africa sits beside the two biggest documented capture cases below, so visible formal ties miss the bribery route.

## C. Money

### C1. World bribe total
- **Figure:** The IMF estimates public sector corruption drains "$1.5 trillion to $2 trillion annually" in bribes, about 2% of global GDP, extrapolated from 2005 World Bank research. Bribes only; indirect costs said to be substantially higher.
- **Measures:** An extrapolated estimate, not a count. Reported in a newspaper; the IMF paper itself ("Corruption: Costs and Mitigating Strategies") not read.
- **Source:** Daily Sabah, 13 May 2016. https://www.dailysabah.com/economy/2016/05/13/imf-global-corruption-costs-trillions-in-bribes-lost-growth
- **Directness:** Weak proxy. Does not separate companies from other payers, or big steering bribes from small service payments. Rests on one old base.

### C2. Procurement
- **Figure:** Public procurement is 12% of global GDP, about $11 trillion (2018 data). Bribes range from 8% to 25% of procured value. The most conservative estimate, 8%, gives about $880 billion. An EU study (PwC/OLAF): inefficiencies equal 18% of project budgets concerned, two-thirds attributed to corruption.
- **Measures:** A rate applied to a spending total, not measured loss. Database referred to covers contracts across 170 countries.
- **Source:** Erica Bosio, "Reducing corruption in public procurement", World Bank Blogs, 17 Aug 2021. https://blogs.worldbank.org/developmenttalk/reducing-corruption-public-procurement
- **Directness:** Close proxy, but the 8% is an assumption. It overlaps with C1, so the two should not be added.

### C3. Detected foreign bribery
- **Figure:** 427 foreign bribery cases concluded since the OECD Convention took force in 1999. Average bribe 10.9% of transaction value. A majority of bribes were paid for public procurement contracts. Intermediaries used in most, and corporate leadership involved or aware in most.
- **Measures:** Concluded enforcement cases only, across parties to the Convention. Summary of the OECD report; percentages for procurement and officials' level not on this page.
- **Source:** Basel Institute on Governance summary of "OECD Foreign Bribery Report". https://baselgovernance.org/publications/oecd-foreign-bribery-report-analysis-crime-bribery-foreign-public-officials
- **Directness:** Direct as instances, but a very small detected sample, and it reflects where enforcement is active. It excludes domestic bribery by design.

## D. The large national cases

### D1. South Africa: Gupta-era loss
- **Figure:** Zondo commission estimate that the state lost R57,344,912,379.34 during the Gupta era. Transnet and Eskom account for more than 97%. R41.2 billion in "illegal contracts" under Brian Molefe. The Gupta family allegedly received at least R15.5 billion; Zondo concluded their benefit likely far exceeds it. Capture of state-owned entities said to begin in 2011.
- **Measures:** Identified contract losses in one family network, final report, June 2022.
- **Source:** Sarah Smit, "R57 billion: How the Guptas played Monopoly with state money", Mail & Guardian, 23 June 2022. https://mg.co.za/news/south-africa/2022-06-23-r57-billion-how-the-guptas-played-monopoly-with-state-money/
- **Directness:** Direct, one country.

### D2. South Africa: what followed
- **Figure:** R57 billion was spending "tainted by State Capture" per PARI; "some estimate" the true cost at R500 billion; R10 billion recovered (SIU and Asset Forfeiture Unit, per the 2025 State of the Nation Address). "No high-profile politician or corporation has been successfully prosecuted." Capture found to have run 2009 to 2018.
- **Source:** Daily Maverick, 30 July 2025. https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2025-07-30-state-capture-failure-to-implement-zondo-commission-recommendations
- **Directness:** Direct for one country. The R500 billion is not sourced on this page.

### D3. South Africa: breadth of the bodies named
- **Finding:** Named bodies include Eskom, Transnet, SAA, Denel, SABC, Alexkor, PRASA, SARS, National Treasury, State Security Agency, SAPS, National Prosecuting Authority. Over 300 witnesses testified; eight mandate extensions; inquiry cost close to R1 billion. Bosasa, a private contractor, allegedly paid R300,000 a month to Zuma, per testimony. Part 5 published 22 June 2022.
- **Source:** "Zondo Commission", Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zondo_Commission
- **Directness:** Direct for spread across institution types, but an encyclopedia page, and the allegations are testimony. Not every body named was entered by companies; the inquiry also covers political factions.

### D4. Odebrecht
- **Figure:** About $788 million in improper payments to foreign officials in at least 12 countries (Angola, Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Guatemala, Mexico, Mozambique, Panama, Peru, Venezuela), 2001 to 2016, run through a "Division of Structure Operations". US$349 million of it in Brazil. US$3.336 billion in construction contracts obtained, US$1.9 billion in Brazil. Odebrecht agreed penalties of up to $4.5 billion but could pay only $2.6 billion.
- **Sources:** US v. Odebrecht case summary, FCPA Clearinghouse (A&O Shearman), filings of 21 Dec 2016. https://fcpa.aoshearman.com/us-v-odebrecht ; Global Anticorruption Blog, 19 Sept 2023, citing US data. https://globalanticorruptionblog.com/2023/09/19/guest-post-the-judicial-annulment-of-the-odebrecht-settlement-in-brazil-and-its-implications/
- **Directness:** Direct: one firm with a dedicated bribery division, from an admitted plea.

### D5. Lava Jato scale
- **Figure:** 278 convictions; evidence against 533 defendants; 1,450 search and seizure warrants; about US$800 million returned, per Brazil's Attorney General's Office when the task force ended (early February 2021). A second source: 429 people investigated and indicted, 159 convicted; R$6.2 billion (about US$2.5 billion) misappropriated from Petrobras; 18 companies; at least 11 countries; prosecutors cooperated with 61; kickbacks of 1% to 5% to directors; congressmen, senators, governors, federal ministers and former presidents Collor, Temer and Lula implicated. The two sets of counts disagree and are not reconciled.
- **Sources:** OCCRP, 8 Feb 2021. https://www.occrp.org/en/news/brazil-dismantles-anti-corruption-task-force-behind-lava-jato ; Wikiwand copy of Wikipedia "Operation Car Wash". https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Odebrecht_scandal
- **Directness:** Direct, one country plus spillover. The second source is an encyclopedia copy; use the OCCRP count first.

### D6. Lava Jato reversal
- **Finding:** In 2023 Justice Toffoli, alone, invalidated the Odebrecht plea deal and ruled none of its evidence lawfully usable, on grounds that prosecutors dealt with US and Swiss counterparts without formal requests. The authors say this threatens "the vast majority" of task force convictions, and is unlikely to affect the US plea. Lula's conviction was overturned on venue grounds.
- **Source:** Global Anticorruption Blog, 19 Sept 2023 (an opinion piece critical of the ruling). URL as in D4.
- **Directness:** **Cuts against** treating court counts as settled. The convictions are not a stable measure, though admissions in the US plea stand.

## Reading

Companies have strong evidence behind them as a major source of infiltration, but no study here gives a share. The best measure of buying the rules, the 1999 BEEPS, found half of 22 transition countries had over a quarter of firms affected, while only a small part of affected firms admit paying. That suggests few captor firms with wide effects, and it covers one region a quarter-century ago. Contract bribery looks like the main route: the OECD says a majority of detected foreign bribes were for procurement, almost half of South Asian firms expect to pay for a government contract, and the Odebrecht and Gupta cases both run on contracts. State-owned firms feature heavily, with Transnet and Eskom over 97% of the R57 billion and Petrobras central to Lava Jato. So entry goes mainly into state enterprises, procurement officials and ministers overseeing them, and then parties and legislators, though this is drawn from a few big cases.

Guess: by count of incidents, companies are likely the largest or second-largest outside party, ahead of organised crime in rich countries, because this route needs only a contract and a motive. Guess: by money, companies are also large, since a conservative $880 billion in procurement sits against a $1.5 to $2 trillion world bribe figure, but both are extrapolations and not all procurement bribes come from firms acting as outsiders. Confidence is moderate on direction and low on size; I would not defend a percentage from this alone.

The evidence that cuts against it: bribe requests are often officials squeezing firms, so high survey numbers overstate firms getting in. Faccio's connected firms show 0% in Brazil and South Africa, so formal ties and actual capture can part ways, and her count shows ties, not steering. Convictions can also collapse, as Lava Jato shows. Missing are world and regional survey figures, regulatory capture, and company-into-company infiltration, so the share going into companies as institutions has no evidence here at all.
